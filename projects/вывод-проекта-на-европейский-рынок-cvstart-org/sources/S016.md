# Living and working conditions: Germany

03/07/2026

## Finding a job

Finding a job 

If you want to work in Germany, useful information is available on the Federal Government’s ‘Make it in Germany’ portal for skilled workers from abroad (www.make-it-in-germany.com). The website provides:
- a ‘Quick-Check’ tool on training and employment opportunities in Germany,
- assistance on finding a job, and
- information on living and working in Germany.

Information is available not only in German, but also English, Spanish, French and 15 other languages.
Tip: 
You can contact advisers directly via www.make-it-in-germany.com. Scroll to the bottom of the website and click on the links provided for information on how to contact an adviser via email or the dedicated hotline. You will also find an ‘FAQ’ section here. The advisers will support you in finding and applying for a job and provide guidance on living conditions in Germany. You can also register for web tutorials on specific topics, such as the recognition procedure or living conditions in Germany; register at https://www.arbeitsagentur.de/vor-ort/zav/events/webtutorials
You will find Germany’s biggest jobs portal at https://www.arbeitsagentur.de/jobsuche. Information is available not only in German, but also in English.
Click on ‘Coming to Germany to work’ (https://www.arbeitsagentur.de/int/de/arbeiten-in-deutschland) for a step-by-step guide on coming to Germany, published by the Federal Employment Agency (BA) in German, English and Spanish. You will find information there about the requirements for accessing the German employment market and the relevant services offered by the BA and the International Placement Service (Zentrale Auslands- und Fachvermittlung, ZAV). You will also get access to a personal dashboard when logged in on the Federal Employment Agency portal, where you can create your employment market profile, receive messages via the mailbox service – as well as further recommendations to improve your chances on the German employment market – or request direct access to advice via the ZAV.
To register, go to the following link and click on ‘Jetzt registrieren’ (‘Register now’): https://www.arbeitsagentur.de/int/de/beratung-unterstuetzung
The EURES-Portal publishes jobs from organisations that are looking for jobseekers from the EURES area. You can also create an applicant profile in this portal.
Companies also publish vacancies in daily newspapers, in private online job sites and on their own websites.
You can publish your personal profile online on social media, in the various business networks, e.g. LinkedIn or Xing, and on job portals. In this way, interested companies will be aware of you and will be able to contact you directly.
It is also common in Germany to send speculative applications.
Private placement agencies offer another alternative to finding a job. They look for a suitable position for you. Enquire in advance about charges if you want to use the service of a private placement agency.

In Germany, a written job application normally consists of:
- a cover letter,
- a CV (with a photo),
- copies of qualifications, certificates, evidence of professional experience, and
- work samples, where applicable.
The cover letter should not run to more than one side of A4 and should briefly and concisely express the following:
- why you are looking for a job,
- what interests you about the post advertised and the company, and
- why you think you are the best person for this job.
Explain why you want to work in Germany. End your cover letter expressing the wish to be invited to a personal or a video interview – and of course with: ‘‘Mit freundlichen Grüßen’ (Yours sincerely).
The CV, in table format, should not be more than two pages long and should be written ‘in reverse chronological order’, meaning that the most recent information is listed first. Divide your CV into the following sections:
- Contact information
- Employment record
- Schooling and vocational training and, where appropriate, tertiary education
- Language skills (categorised into mother tongue, advanced working knowledge, spoken and written fluency, good command or basic knowledge)
- Other professional knowledge and experience (e.g. computer skills)
- Hobbies and non-occupational activities (e.g. volunteering)
Do not forget to sign and date your CV. We also recommend that you use the Europass format, at https://europass.europa.eu/de
Enclose with your application copies of supporting evidence for all training and continuing education, periods of practical training and previous jobs that are mentioned in your application. In some cases, for example when applying to a small business, you should have your qualifications and work references translated into German. Even if you already have professional experience, you will often have to enclose your school certificates with the application.
Despite current trends towards anonymity in selection procedures, a friendly application photograph with a neutral background remains part of a job application in Germany. It is attached either to the cover sheet of the application folder or to the top right-hand corner of the CV.
More and more employers in Germany now only accept applications made by e-mail or on their company’s own online application forms.
- When applying by email: It is advisable to compile a single PDF document containing all your application documents, such as cover letter, CV, certificates and references, and photograph, and send it as an attachment. You should ensure that the size of the file does not exceed two megabytes (2 MB).
- If an online application form is used: If necessary, upload the various application documents as separate PDF files.
You can often also request the name of the contact person by telephone. This means that your cover letter and application documents can be addressed personally.
Tip: With larger organisations, you can contact the press office or the marketing department to get interesting information about the company. You can then mention this information in the cover letter.
Good luck!

## Traineeships

Definition and eligibility 

### Definition
Traineeships provide insights into everyday working life. They serve either as preparation for choosing a career or as a means of gaining work experience. Although traineeships are not vocational training as such, they are a good way of preparing to go on a training course or to start a career. They are often undertaken before or during a vocational education course. In some training courses they are mandatory. Depending on educational background, traineeships can be an admission requirement for school or higher education. Sometimes they are part of higher education studies in the form of a practical semester. For companies, traineeships are a good way of acquiring new talent.
There are many types of traineeships:
- Accreditation traineeship: Compulsory traineeship after a theoretical and practical training course specific to a certain field in order to achieve recognition of a vocational qualification.
- Practical semester: For students as part of the examination regulations for courses of study.
- Shipping traineeship: Voluntary traineeship for school leavers to learn about technical maritime careers and the requirements for them.
- Taster work placement: A traineeship lasting a few days for school pupils to learn about working and training conditions.
- Work experience for school pupils: In most federal states, work experience is mandatory in Year 9, 10 or 11. This is usually organised via the school. Work experience lasts between two and four weeks.
- Social work experience: Pupils at many schools are required to complete social work experience in addition to their standard work experience for school pupils. Unlike work experience for school pupils, it is not about getting a first insight into working life, but about promoting social skills and recognising the value of social work.
- Course-related traineeship: Additional vocational qualification that is both practical and voluntary to increase the chances of finding a job for students from all subject areas.
- Trainee: Programme of training specific to the job and the company for new graduates starting a career. The main purpose of these traineeships is to recruit new graduates.
- Preliminary traineeship / specialised traineeship: Compulsory traineeship that must be completed before or during a training course or higher education in the relevant subject area.
### Eligibility
Nationals of the European Union (EU) and the European Economic Area (EEA) have free access to traineeships in Germany.
Since traineeships are in principle classed as employment, there are special rules for citizens of other countries. Information on these rules, as well as other information on immigration, is available on the website of the Federal Foreign Office, including in English, French, Spanish, Arabic and many other languages: https://www.auswaertiges-amt.de/de/service/fragenkatalog-node/-/606790.
As a rule, at least a basic knowledge of German is required, both for communication with colleagues and for life outside the traineeship company.Information about the national quality framework 

### Implementation
Germany has a number of national and regional regulations in place, as well as social partnership agreements, which aim to ensure the quality of traineeships outside formal educational and VET programmes and thus ensure compliance with the March 2014 Council Recommendation on a Quality Framework for Traineeships.
National regulations:
- 
Minimum Wage Act(Mindestlohngesetz, MiLoG): The Minimum Wage Act has been in force since 16 August 2014. It stipulates that traineeships that do not fall under certain exceptions must pay the statutory minimum wage.
Regional guidelines and social partnership agreements:
- Traineeship Guideline of the Federation of Municipal Employers’ Associations (Vereinigung der kommunalen Arbeitgeberverbände, VKA): This guideline, last amended on 1 January 2025, governs traineeships that are not covered by the Minimum Wage Act or collective agreements, including compulsory and voluntary traineeships. It abolishes the distinction between traineeships that fall under the Vocational Education and Training Act (Berufsbildungsgesetz, BBiG) and those that do not, and provides for an expense allowance of up to EUR 1,000 for compulsory traineeships.
- 
Guideline of the Collective Bargaining Association of German States (Tarifgemeinschaft deutscher Länder, TdL): This guideline defines various types of traineeship and stipulates that trainees do not have an obligation to perform any work. It emphasises the need for a written traineeship contract that defines the learning and training objectives, among other aspects.
In addition, trade unions such as the German Education Union (Gewerkschaft Erziehung und Wissenschaft, GEW) advocate the setting of binding quality standards for school work experience placements in order to support and promote collaboration between schools, social partners, businesses and politicians (https://www.gew.de/aktuelles/detailseite/qualitaetsstandards-fuer-schuelerpraktika-schaffen).
These measures ensure the quality of traineeships in Germany and compliance with European recommendations.

### Living and working conditions
The conditions of the traineeship depend on the contract between the two parties – the trainee and the company.
The traineeship contract sets out the following:
- the duration and pay of the traineeship,
- the content and the tasks to be learnt,
- the field of work,
- objectives.
If the traineeship is a compulsory component of vocational or university education, the trainee retains their educational status. For example, if the trainee is undertaking vocational training then they would retain the status of an apprentice, or if they are undertaking a course of higher education then they would retain the status of a student. This means that their educational status does not change to ‘trainee’ because they are completing a traineeship.
The working conditions for trainees in Germany vary depending on the type of traineeship, particularly in relation to remuneration, social security contributions and entitlement to social benefits.
Remuneration and minimum wage:
Whether a traineeship is paid and whether the statutory minimum wage applies depends on the type of traineeship:
- Compulsory traineeships: Traineeships that are prescribed by study and examination regulations are not subject to the Minimum Wage Act (Mindestlohngesetz). Remuneration for these traineeships is therefore not legally prescribed and is at the employer’s discretion.
- Voluntary traineeships: Traineeships that are not a mandatory part of a training course or course of study and last longer than three months must be paid in accordance with the Minimum Wage Act. There is no minimum wage obligation for voluntary traineeships of up to three months.
Social security:
The treatment of trainees under social security law depends on the type and duration of the traineeship and the level of remuneration:
- Compulsory traineeships during a course of study: Trainees are classed as normal students and are generally exempt from health, nursing care and unemployment insurance, irrespective of whether they receive remuneration. However, pension insurance is mandatory.
- Compulsory traineeships before or after a course of study: If trainees receive remuneration, they are subject to health, nursing care, pension and unemployment insurance. If the compulsory traineeship is unpaid, trainees are generally exempt from health and nursing care insurance; however, they do require pension and unemployment insurance.
- Voluntary traineeships: In the case of voluntary traineeships during a course of study, trainees are exempt from health, nursing care and unemployment insurance, provided that the trainee’s studies take priority and the working hours do not exceed 20 hours per week. However, pension insurance is mandatory. For voluntary traineeships completed before or after a course of study, trainees are generally required to have insurance in all branches of social security.
Social benefits:
Trainees are entitled to social benefits under certain conditions:
- Child benefit: Parents are entitled to child benefit for children up to 25 years of age provided that these children are undertaking initial vocational training or a course of study. Compulsory traineeships that are required in accordance with the study regulations are classed as training for this purpose. This applies to parents with a permanent residence in Germany as well as parents and spouses who generate an income in Germany.
- BAföG (Federal Training Assistance Act): Students can receive BAföG benefits during a compulsory traineeship, provided that the traineeship is required in accordance with the study regulations and certain conditions are met.

### Funding and support
If the traineeship is remunerated, the payment must comply with the regulations on the statutory minimum wage. The following are exempt from this:
- compulsory professional traineeships as part of the training (school, initial vocational training, higher education),
- voluntary traineeships during vocational or higher education up to a maximum of three months, and
- orientation traineeships for vocational training or for starting a course of higher education study, for up to three months.
Depending on the type of traineeship, contributions to health, nursing care, pension and unemployment insurance have to be paid.

## Apprenticeships

Definition and eligibility 

### Legal framework
Legal bases:
Content, duration, completion/graduation and the suitability of training establishments and staff are regulated by law at national level by the Vocational Education and Training Act (Berufsbildungsgesetz, BBiG) in conjunction with other discipline-specific laws (apprenticeship schemes, regulations and codes of professional conduct). Compulsory (vocational) training is regulated by educational acts at the regional level.
There are no formal entry requirements for accessing a dual apprenticeship. Dual apprenticeships are therefore, in principle, open to everyone. However, the majority of apprentices have an intermediate school-leaving qualification when they begin training, with some even having an advanced level qualification. Please always check beforehand the entry requirements for your desired training programme, e.g. by enquiring directly with the vocational (technical) school of your choice.
It is possible to complete an apprenticeship part-time. However, the training company must agree to a part-time apprenticeship.
There is currently no legal minimum age for the dual education system (combining an apprenticeship in a company and vocational education at a vocational school), but the Act on the Protection of Young Persons at Work (Jugendarbeitsschutzgesetz, JArbSchG) sets important guidelines: You are legally considered a ‘young person’ and can start a regular apprenticeship from the age of 15. However, many companies prefer applicants over the age of 16, since stricter rules apply regarding breaks and holidays for young people under the age of 16.

### Description of schemes
- 
Dual apprenticeships
The dual apprenticeship scheme generally lasts between 2 and 3.5 years. The dual scheme is so called because training takes place in two learning facilities:
- At the workplace (practical side): Apprentices are integrated into the real-life operating processes of the company from the outset and become familiar with work equipment and operational work processes on site. Apprentices work alongside training supervisors, who determine training progress and may provide support through additional teaching, as needed.
- Vocational school (theoretical side): The structure, sequence and content of these courses are aligned with the practical training. 
The vocational school component usually accounts for between 20% and 40%. There are a number of different models though, e.g. one to two days per week or whole blocks lasting several weeks.
Apprentices do not normally work at the company on the days when they attend vocational school. This time can be used to consolidate the material studied at vocational school.
The apprenticeship aims to provide the skills and qualifications needed to perform a skilled occupation in a changing world of work. Successfully completing an apprenticeship qualifies you to take up an immediate position as a skilled professional in one of the 327 regulated professions that are currently approved by the state (source: https://www.bibb.de/dienst/publikationen/de/20423).
- Apprenticeship system:
- The training company and the apprentice enter into an apprenticeship contract (similar to a contract of employment). This obliges the training supervisor to provide practical training to the apprentice at the workplace in accordance with legal and technical requirements. The apprenticeship generally ends with a final examination.
- The training company enrols the apprentice at the relevant vocational school and agrees to release the apprentice to attend classes at the vocational school. The vocational school is not always located in the same place as the training company. Apprentices are required to attend classes at the vocational school. The requirement to attend classes at the vocational school lasts for the duration of the apprenticeship.
- System of remuneration:
- The Vocational Training Act (Berufsbildungsgesetz, BBiG) (https://www.aufstiegs-bafoeg.de/bmbf/de/bildung/berufliche-bildung/rahmenbedingungen-und-gesetzliche-grundlagen/das-berufsbildungsgesetz-bbig/das-berufsbildungsgesetz-bbig_node.html) stipulates a minimum apprenticeship allowance (Mindestausbildungsvergütung, MAV) for all apprenticeship agreements entered into after 1 January 2020.
- For vocational training courses starting in 2026, the gross monthly allowance is at least EUR 724 in the first year, at least EUR 854 in the second year, at least EUR 977 in the third year and at least EUR 1 014 in the fourth year (source: https://www.bibb.de/de/199658.php). The minimum allowance is dynamic, which means that the minimum allowance for the first year of training will be adjusted annually to the average change in all apprenticeship allowances.
- Recognition of qualifications:
- The combination of theory and practice makes it possible for apprentices to complete work independently, including planning and quality control. The skills acquired also include the ability to integrate into the social work structure of a company independently. Successful completion of the apprenticeship serves as proof of this skill.
- Due to the apprentice’s integration into the day-to-day work of the company over a period of 2 to 3.5 years, the likelihood of the apprentice being taken on by the company upon completion of the apprenticeship is very good. According to the findings of the IAB Business Panel, in 2024, 79% of all apprenticeship graduates were taken on by the company where they completed their apprenticeship or by another company within the same group (source: https://doku.iab.de/kurzber/2025/kb2025-14.pdf (PDF)). This rate has therefore remained at a level of over 70% for the eighth year in a row.
- Specific data on the retention rate for 2025 is not yet available (as at 29 January 2026). However, a survey of 9 090 apprentices between September 2024 and May 2025 indicated that around 41.5% of those surveyed did not yet know whether they would be hired after completing their apprenticeship (source: https://www.dgb.de/fileadmin/download_center/Studien/ausbildungsreport2025_mid.pdf (PDF)).
- It is important to note that the retention rate varies from industry to industry and is higher in large companies than in small and medium-sized enterprises. Particularly high retention rates (almost 9 out of 10 apprentices) were recorded in 2024 in the finance and insurance sectors, in mining, energy and water supply, in transport and storage, and in public administration. Meanwhile, agriculture and forestry has the lowest retention rate at 60% (source: https://doku.iab.de/kurzber/2025/kb2025-14.pdf (PDF)).
- The dual apprenticeship scheme continues to be regarded by industry as the main source of skilled workers. The training is practice-oriented, high quality and reasonably short, taking an average of three years.
- Further training opportunities:
- After the apprenticeship (and usually a period of professional practice), a wide variety of further training and specialisation is available. Since this is often cost-intensive, it is common practice for the company to cover some of the costs. In return, the employees undertake to remain with the company for a certain period of time after completion of the apprenticeship.
- Financial assistance can be obtained under certain conditions under what is known as the Federal Advanced Education and Training Assistance Act (Aufstiegs-BAföG or Aufstiegsfortbildungsförderungsgesetz, AFBG). The AFBG funds preparations for more than 700 further training qualifications, such as master craftsmen, business administrators, technicians or economists. For more information on funding opportunities, visit https://www.aufstiegs-bafoeg.de.

- This is a key component in the continuing development and ongoing adaptation of apprenticeships to the real-life requirements of industry and people. The dual apprenticeship scheme is not rigid, but continues to evolve.

### Eligibility
Citizens of the EU, Liechtenstein, Iceland, Norway or Switzerland are able to start vocational training in Germany at any time. You do not need a visa to start a vocational training programme in Germany.
Citizens from other countries are also able to start vocational training in Germany. You will need a visa in order to do so. You can apply for the relevant visa at the German embassy in your country of residence.
A good knowledge of the German language is essential for successfully completing vocational or school-based training, particularly in order to be able to understand and apply the content of the theoretical teaching. This means that you must have reached at least B1 level under the Common European Framework of Reference for Languages (CEFR) at the beginning of your training. B2 level would be preferable, which should be obtained at the very latest by the end of your training. For nursing professions, some federal states require a B2 certificate to have been obtained by the end of the training.

### Living and working conditions
Despite the introduction of the minimum apprenticeship allowance, the allowance paid is not comparable with normal earning potential. It varies between a minimum of EUR 724 and roughly EUR 1 400 gross per month, depending on the profession, region and apprenticeship year. The allowance paid to apprentices rises each year of their apprenticeship.
From that allowance, a percentage is deducted to cover social security contributions (health insurance, nursing care insurance, pension insurance, unemployment insurance) (https://www.make-it-in-germany.com/de/jobs/sozialversicherung/deutsche#c17519).
This means that wages earned during the years of the apprenticeship are taken into account for the purpose of the statutory retirement pension.
Apprentices earning more than EUR 12 348 in 2026 (basic tax allowance) are also required to pay tax (https://www.bundesfinanzministerium.de/Content/DE/Standardartikel/Themen/Steuern/das-aendert-sich-2026.html).
The statutory minimum annual leave entitlement is laid down in the Youth Employment Protection Act (Jugendarbeitsschutzgesetz, JArbSchG) for young people (minors) and in the Federal Annual Leave Act (Bundesurlaubsgesetz, BUrlG) for adult apprentices. The following minimum annual leave entitlements apply, according to the Chamber of Industry and Commerce (IHK):
- at least 30 working days of leave if they are under 16 years of age at the beginning of the calendar year
- at least 27 working days of leave if they are under 17 years of age at the beginning of the calendar year
- at least 25 working days of leave if they are under the age of 18 at the beginning of the calendar year
- at least 24 working days of leave if they are 18 or over at the beginning of the calendar year.

### Funding and support
Many apprentices continue to live with their parents during their dual apprenticeship. If this is not possible, the allowance paid is not usually sufficient to cover the expense of living in one’s own home and everyday expenses.
Under certain conditions, vocational training assistance (Berufsausbildungsbeihilfe, BAB) may be requested. During a dual apprenticeship in a recognised regulated profession, BAB is paid as support for everyday expenses. One of the following requirements must be met in order to be eligible for vocational training assistance:
- the apprentice is unable to live with their parents as the training company is too far away, or
- the apprentice is over the age of 18 or married, or lives with a partner, or
- the apprentice has at least one child and does not live in their parents’ home.
Further information on vocational training assistance is available on the website of the Federal Employment Agency (https://www.arbeitsagentur.de/bildung/ausbildung/berufsausbildungsbeihilfe-bab) and from local employment agencies.
Many towns and cities have ‘Jugendwohnheime’ (accommodation for young people), where apprentices can find a roof over their heads and support away from home. They not only offer furnished accommodation, but often also a canteen, advice and free-time activities, and can be subsidised through vocational training assistance (Berufsausbildungsbeihilfe, BAB) (https://auswaerts-zuhause.de/).
Support during the dual apprenticeship is provided through the training assistance scheme (Assistierte Ausbildung, AsA). Under the AsA, apprentices receive the support they need upon request, either individually or in small groups. The content is tailored to meet individual needs. The following types of support are available:
- to study for exams,
- to stay on the ball in the vocational school,
- to resolve a dispute or resolve a problem in the company,
- to improve language skills,
- to find a suitable job after training, or
- to get your first job after training.
Information about this is available on the website of the Federal Employment Agency https://www.arbeitsagentur.de/bildung/ausbildung/assistierte-ausbildung-machen and from local employment agencies.
Since the entry into force of the Foreign Nationals Employment Assistance Act (Ausländerbeschäftigungsförderungsgesetz) (https://www.bmas.de/DE/Service/Gesetze-und-Gesetzesvorhaben/auslaenderbeschaeftigungsfoerderungsgesetz.html) on 1 August 2019, all foreign nationals living in Germany are also eligible to receive support through BAB and AsA during an apprenticeship.
BAföG may be requested during school-based vocational training at a vocational college under certain conditions. The abbreviation ‘BAföG’ stands for Bundesausbildungsförderungsgesetz (Federal Training Assistance Act).
Citizens of the European Union as well as migrants and refugees living in Germany may also receive BAföG as financial support during their studies or school years.
As a basic rule: Foreign nationals with a prospect of remaining in Germany who are integrated into society are eligible to receive assistance. This includes, for example, individuals with a right of permanent residence in accordance with EU law on freedom of movement, a long-term resident EU residence permit, or a settlement permit.

## Moving to another country

Movement of goods and capital 

The free movement of goods is one of the cornerstones of the European Single Market.
The removal of national barriers to the free movement of goods within the EU is one of the principles enshrined in the EU Treaties. From a traditionally protectionist starting point, the countries of the EU have continuously been lifting restrictions to form a ‘common’ or single market. This commitment to create a European trading area without frontiers has led to the creation of more wealth and new jobs, and has globally established the EU as a world trading player alongside the United States and Japan.
Despite Europe’s commitment to breaking down all internal trade barriers, not all sectors of the economy have been harmonised. The European Union decided to regulate at a European level sectors which might impose a higher risk for Europe’s citizens – such as pharmaceuticals or construction products. The majority of products (considered a ‘lower risk’) are subject to the application of the so-called principle of mutual recognition, which means that essentially every product legally manufactured or marketed in one of the Member States can be freely moved and traded within the EU internal market.
Limits to the free movement of goods
The EU Treaty gives Member States the right to set limits to the free movement of goods when there is a specific common interest such as protection of the environment, citizens’ health, or public policy, to name a few. This means for example that if the import of a product is seen by a Member State’s national authorities as a potential threat to public health, public morality or public policy, it can deny or restrict access to its market. Examples of such products are genetically modified food or certain energy drinks.
Even though there are generally no limitations for the purchase of goods in another Member State, as long as they are for personal use, there is a series of European restrictions for specific categories of products, such as alcohol and tobacco.
Free movement of capital
Another essential condition for the functioning of the internal market is the free movement of capital. It is one of the four basic freedoms guaranteed by EU legislation and represents the basis of the integration of European financial markets. Europeans can now manage and invest their money in any EU Member State.
The liberalisation of capital markets has marked a crucial point in the process of economic and monetary integration in the EU. It was the first step towards the establishment of our European Economic and Monetary Union (EMU) and the common currency, the Euro.
Advantage
The principle of the free movement of capital not only increases the efficiency of financial markets within the Union, it also brings a series of advantages to EU citizens. Individuals can carry out a broad number of financial operations within the EU without major restrictions. For instance, individuals with few restrictions can
- easily open a bank account,
- buy shares
- invest, or
- purchase real estate
in another Member State. EU Companies can invest in, own and manage other European enterprises.
Exceptions
Certain exceptions to this principle apply both within the Member States and with third countries. They are mainly related to taxation, prudential supervision, public policy considerations, money laundering and financial sanctions agreed under the EU Common Foreign and Security Policy.
The European Commission is continuing to work on the completion of the free market for financial services, by implementing new strategies for financial integration in order to make it even easier for citizens and companies to manage their money within the EU.Finding accommodation 

Finding accommodation in Germany is not always easy. On top of that, landlords want to know whether you are reliable and will be able to pay your rent. You should include the following documents in your accommodation application:
- proof of income and credit history (copies of your last three monthly payslips);
- a photocopy of your ID card;
- a certificate of no outstanding rent (Mietschuldenfreiheitsbescheinigung) from your previous landlord (or you can provide evidence of regular rent payments through your bank statements);
- a self-declaration form (Selbstauskunft) (providing details of your financial, family and personal circumstances);
- a parental guarantee, if you are still studying or in vocational training or an apprenticeship.
Do not forget to check whether the accommodation is offered unfurnished, partly furnished or fully furnished. Accommodation is often let without kitchen equipment (cooker, sink, refrigerator, kitchen cabinets, etc.).
You will find advertisements for accommodation in the property sections of local daily newspapers and on the internet on various real estate portals. In most cases, landlords also expect a one-off deposit of two to three months’ net rent in addition to the actual rent. This serves as a guarantee for any damage that may occur while you are renting the accommodation that needs to be repaired after you move out. The deposit is returned at the end of the tenancy if the landlord has no complaints.
Some landlords also accept something known as ‘rent guarantee insurance’ (Mietkautionsversicherung) instead; this replaces a normal deposit and ensures that things are not as financially demanding for tenants at the start of their tenancy. With rent guarantee insurance, tenants pay an annual premium to an insurance company, rather than a high one-off payment to the accommodation provider(s), but it offers landlords the same financial security as with a normal rent deposit.
Tips:
- When you move into your accommodation, create a handover log to document any existing defects and the general state of the accommodation, room by room, and have this document countersigned and dated by the landlord. This is the best way to protect yourself as far as possible against any complaints that the landlord may raise about defects that existed before you moved in.
- If you come to Germany alone and are prepared to live with other people, shared apartments (Wohngemeinschaften, WG) are a good alternative to a place of your own. In many university towns, there are also accommodation agencies (Mitwohnzentralen) that can provide rooms or apartments for a limited period of time on payment of a commission.

The implementation of the principle of free movement of people, is one of the cornerstones of our European construction, has meant the introduction a series of practical rules to ensure that citizens can travel freely and easily to any Member State of the European Union. Travelling across the EU with one’s car has become a lot less problematic. The European Commission has set a series of common regulations governing the mutual recognition of driving licences, the validity of car insurance, and the possibility of registering your car in a host country.
Your driving licence in the EU
The EU has introduced a harmonised licence model and further minimum requirements for obtaining a licence. This should help to keep unsafe drivers off Europe's roads - wherever they take their driving test.
Since 19 January 2013, all driving licences issued by EU countries have the same look and feel. The licences are printed on a piece of plastic that has the size and shape of a credit card.
Harmonised administrative validity periods for the driving licence document have been introduced which are between 10 and 15 years for motorcycles and passenger cars. This enables the authorities to regularly update the driving licence document with new security features that will make it harder to forge or tamper ­- so unqualified or banned drivers will find it harder to fool the authorities, in their own country or elsewhere in the EU.
The new European driving licence is also protecting vulnerable road users by introducing progressive access for motorbikes and other powered two-wheelers. The "progressive access" system means that riders will need experience with a less powerful bike before they go on to bigger machines. Mopeds will also constitute a separate category called AM.
You must apply for a licence in the country where you usually or regularly live. As a general rule, it is the country where you live for at least 185 days each calendar year because of personal or work-related ties.
If you have personal/work-related ties in 2 or more EU countries, your place of usual residence is the place where you have personal ties, as long as you go back regularly. You don't need to meet this last condition if you are living in an EU country to carry out a task for a fixed period of time.
If you move to another EU country to go to college or university, your place of usual residence doesn't change. However, you can apply for a driving licence in your host country if you can prove you have been studying there for at least 6 months.
Registering your car in the host country
If you move permanently to another EU country and take your car with you, you should register your car and pay car-related taxes in your new country.
There are no common EU rules on vehicle registration and related taxes. Some countries have tax-exemption rules for vehicle registration when moving with the car from one country to another permanently.
To benefit from a tax exemption, you must check the applicable deadlines and conditions in the country you wish to move to.
Check the exact rules and deadlines with the national authorities: https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/vehicles/registration/registration-abroad/index_en.htm 
Car Insurance
EU citizens can insure their car in any EU country, as long as the chosen insurance company is licensed by the host national authority to issue the relevant insurance policies. A company based in another Member State is entitled sell a policy for compulsory civil liability only if certain conditions are met. Insurance will be valid throughout the Union, no matter where the accident takes place.
Taxation
Value Added Tax or VAT on motor vehicles is ordinarily paid in the country where the car is purchased, although under certain conditions, VAT is paid in the country of destination.
More information on the rules which apply when a vehicle is acquired in one EU Member State and is intended to be registered in another EU Member State is available on this link https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/vehicles/registration/taxes-abroad/index_en.htm.Registration procedures and residence permits 

Nationals of the European Union (EU) Member States enjoy unlimited freedom of movement for workers and are not subject to any restrictions as regards work permits. The same applies to nationals of the European Economic Area (EEA) (including Iceland, Norway and Liechtenstein). Swiss nationals share the same status as nationals of EEA countries.
Nationals of countries outside the EU, EEA or Switzerland, referred to as third-country nationals, need a residence document (visa, residence permit, EU Blue Card, settlement permit, EU permanent residence permit) in order to enter Germany and remain there for the purposes of taking up employment.
In principle, family members from third countries require an entry visa. However, certain states benefit from advantages. Before entering Germany, please contact the German diplomatic mission or consular post in your country of origin to find out what rules apply to you. Persons who have a valid residence card from another EU Member State are excluded from that requirement. They are exempt from the visa requirement (Article 5(2) of the Free Movement Directive (Directive 2004/38/EC)).
The Skilled Immigration Act (Fachkräfteeinwanderungsgesetz 2.0) (https://www.make-it-in-germany.com/de/visum-aufenthalt/fachkraefteeinwanderungsgesetz) has been in force in Germany since 1 November 2023. This Act opens up the labour market to skilled workers from countries outside the European Union (EU). Before this, only academically trained professionals had access to the labour market in occupations where there was no shortage in Germany. In future, professionals with recognised foreign vocational training will also be able to obtain a residence permit for employment in all the professions for which they are qualified. The restriction to what are known as bottleneck professions will cease to apply.
An equivalent qualification and a firm job offer are necessary in order to grant a residence document for the purpose of taking up employment. You will be permitted to practise any qualified profession of which you are capable as a result of your vocational qualification. As before, the Federal Employment Agency will assess whether the conditions of employment are equivalent to those for comparable domestic employees. A priority check is no longer carried out for professionals.
It is also possible to obtain a visa for a limited period of time, for the purpose of obtaining full professional recognition or to look for a job or training position.
The accelerated skilled worker process helps to make the visa procedure faster and more predictable. An agreement must be in place between the company and the locally competent immigration authority. As soon as all the documents have been submitted and the immigration authority has given its approval for entry to the country, the skilled worker will be notified of their appointment at the foreign embassy within three weeks and will receive the visa within a further three weeks. The company must pay an additional processing fee of EUR 411 for this procedure.
The Service Center for Professional Recognition (Zentrale Servicestelle Berufsanerkennung, ZSBA) at the Federal Employment Agency (BA), situated at the International Placement Service (ZAV) of the Federal Employment Agency in Bonn, started work on 1 February 2020. The role of the ZSBA is to give advice to foreign nationals hoping to obtain recognition of their professional qualifications on the prospects and requirements of the recognition procedure and professional accreditation, and associated matters of residency. It supports these foreign nationals from the recognition procedure until they enter Germany, and supports applicants in compiling the required documents.
The ZSBA also gives advice on possible places of employment and facilitates contacts to apply for any necessary training opportunities.

## Working conditions

An opening paragraph briefly describing working conditions in Europe 

Quality of work and employment - a vital issue, with a strong economic and humanitarian impact
Good working conditions are important for the well-being of European workers. They
- contribute to the physical and psychological welfare of Europeans, and
- contribute to the economic performance of the EU.
From a humanitarian point of view, the quality of working environment has a strong influence on the overall work and life satisfaction of European workers.
From an economic point of view, high-quality job conditions are a driving force of economic growth and a foundation for the competitive position of the European Union. A high level of work satisfaction is an important factor for achieving high productivity of the EU economy.
It is therefore a core issue for the European Union to promote the creation and maintenance of a sustainable and pleasant working environment – one that promotes health and well-being of European employees and creates a good balance between work and non-work time.
Improving working conditions in Europe: an important objective for the European Union.
Ensuring favourable working conditions for European citizens is a priority for the EU. The European Union is therefore working together with national governments to ensure a pleasant and secure workplace environment. Support to Member States is provided through:
- the exchange of experience between different countries and common actions
- the establishment of the minimum requirements on working conditions and health and safety at work, to be applied all over the European Union
Criteria for quality of work and employment
In order to achieve sustainable working conditions, it is important to determine the main characteristics of a favourable working environment and thus the criteria for the quality of working conditions.
The European Foundation for the Improvement of Living and Working Conditions (Eurofound) in Dublin, is an EU agency that provides information, advice and expertise on, as the name implies, living and working conditions. This agency has established several criteria for job and employment quality, which include:
- health and well-being at the workplace – this is a vital criteria, since good working conditions suppose the prevention of health problems at the work place, decreasing the exposure to risk and improving work organisation
- reconciliation of working and non-working life – citizens should be given the chance to find a balance between the time spent at work and at leisure
- skills development – a quality job is one that gives possibilities for training, improvement and career opportunities
The work of Eurofound contributes to the planning and design of better living and working conditions in Europe.
Health and safety at work
The European Commission has undertaken a wide scope of activities to promote a healthy working environment in the EU Member States. Amongst others, it developed a Community Strategy for Health and Safety at Work for the period 2021-2027. This strategy was set up with the help of national authorities, social partners and NGOs. It addresses the changing needs in worker’s protection brought by the digital and green transitions, new forms of work and the COVID-19 pandemic. At the same time, the framework will continue to address traditional occupational safety and health risks, such as risks of accidents at work or exposure to hazardous chemicals.
The Community policy on health and safety at work aims at a long-lasting improvement of well-being of EU workers. It takes into account the physical, moral and social dimensions of working conditions, as well as the new challenges brought up by the enlargement of the European Union towards countries from Central and Eastern Europe. The introduction of EU standards for health and safety at the workplace, has contributed a lot to the improvement of the situation of workers in these countries.
Improving working conditions by setting minimum requirements common to all EU countries
Improving living and working conditions in the EU Member States depends largely on the establishment of common labour standards. EU labour laws and regulations have set the minimum requirements for a sustainable working environment and are now applied in all Member States. The improvement of these standards has strengthened workers’ rights and is one of the main achievements of the EU’s social policy.Recognition of diplomas and qualifications 

The importance of transparency and mutual recognition of diplomas as a crucial complement to the free movement of workers
The possibility of obtaining recognition of one’s qualifications and competences can play a vital role in the decision to take up work in another EU country. It is therefore necessary to develop a European system that will guarantee the mutual acceptance of professional competences in different Member States. Only such a system will ensure that a lack of recognition of professional qualifications will not become an obstacle to workers’ mobility within the EU.
Main principles for the recognition of professional qualifications in the EU
As a basic principle, any EU citizen should be able to freely practice their profession in any Member State. Unfortunately the practical implementation of this principle is often hindered by national requirements for access to certain professions in the host country.
For the purpose of overcoming these differences, the EU has set up a system for the recognition of professional qualifications. Within the terms of this system, a distinction is made between regulated professions (professions for which certain qualifications are legally required) and professions that are not legally regulated in the host Member State.
Steps towards a transparency of qualifications in Europe
The European Union has taken important steps towards the objective of achieving transparency of qualifications in Europe:
- An increased co-operation in vocational education and training, with the intention to combine all instruments for transparency of certificates and diplomas, in one single, user-friendly tool. This includes, for example, the European CV or Europass Trainings.
- The development of concrete actions in the field of recognition and quality in vocational education and training.
Going beyond the differences in education and training systems throughout the EU
Education and training systems in the EU Member States still show substantial differences. The last enlargements of the EU, with different educational traditions, have further increased this diversity. This calls for a need to set up common rules to guarantee recognition of competences.
In order to overcome this diversity of national qualification standards, educational methods and training structures, the European Commission has put forward a series of instruments, aimed at ensuring better transparency and recognition of qualifications both for academic and professional purposes.
- The European Qualifications Framework
The European Qualifications Framework is a key priority for the European Commission in the process of recognition of professional competences. The main objective of the framework is to create links between the different national qualification systems and guarantee a smooth transfer and recognition of diplomas.
- The National Academic Recognition Information Centres (NARICs)
A network of National Academic Recognition Information Centres was established in 1984 at the initiative of the European Commission. The NARICs provide advice on the academic recognition of periods of study abroad. Located in all EU Member States as well as in the countries of the European Economic Area, NARICs play a vital role the process of recognition of qualifications in the EU.
- The European Credit Transfer System (ECTS)
The European Credit Transfer System aims at facilitating the recognition of periods of study abroad. Introduced in 1989, it functions by describing an education programme and attaching credits to its components. It is a key complement to the highly acclaimed student mobility programme Erasmus.
- Europass
Europass is an instrument for ensuring the transparency of professional skills. It is composed of five standardised documents
- a CV (Curriculum Vitae),
- a cover letter editor,
- certificate supplements,
- diploma supplements, and
- a Europass-Mobility document.
The Europass system makes skills and qualifications clearly and easily understood in the different parts of Europe. In every country of the European Union and the European Economic Area, national Europass centres have been established as the primary contact points for people seeking for information about the Europass system.Kinds of employment 

The minimum age for regular employment in a business is 15. Although apprentices work in a business as part of their vocational training, they are not employed in the conventional sense and therefore enter into a vocational training contract with the company providing the training.
The majority of employees continue to have permanent, full-time employment contracts with weekly working times that are generally 38 to 40 hours.
40.1% of the workforce in Germany worked part-time in the third-quarter of 2025.
The increase in the part-time rate in recent years is partly due to a rise in jobs in sectors with a high proportion of part-time positions (health and social services, education and training), and a decline in manufacturing jobs, which accounts for a high proportion of full-time positions.
11.2% of people employed in Germany have a second job alongside their main job (https://iab.de/presseinfo/immer-mehr-beschaeftigte-arbeiten-in-neben-und-teilzeitjobs/).
The following forms of employment are particularly common in Germany:
- Vocational training relationship: This includes vocational training, vocational training preparation, further vocational training and retraining. The specific and central legal source is the Vocational Education and Training Act (Berufsbildungsgesetz, BBiG) (https://www.aufstiegs-bafoeg.de/bmbf/de/bildung/berufliche-bildung/rahmenbedingungen-und-gesetzliche-grundlagen/das-berufsbildungsgesetz-bbig/das-berufsbildungsgesetz-bbig_node.html).
- Mini-jobs: In the case of minor employment – also known as a ‘mini-job’ – a distinction is made between low-income and short-term employment.
- Employment is categorised as short-term employment if it is limited to no more than three months or 70 working days from the start date in the course of a calendar year. Under labour law, there is a fully-fledged employment relationship in each case. Short-term employment also includes seasonal work.
- Employment is deemed to be low-income employment if the remuneration generally does not exceed EUR 603 per month (as at 12 February 2026 – https://www.bmas.de/DE/Soziales/Sozialversicherung/Geringfuegige-Beschaeftigung/mini-jobs.html). Generally speaking, marginal workers should therefore be treated as part-time and full-time workers – but they are not subject to compulsory insurance (health insurance, long-term care insurance and unemployment insurance). They can be exempted from compulsory pension insurance.
- The number of hours a mini-jobber can work in a month depends on the hourly wage. This is equal to EUR 13.90 gross per hour, corresponding to the current minimum wage since 1 January 2026. If a company pays exactly this mandatory hourly wage, someone with a mini-job can work up to 43 hours per month without exceeding the earning limit of EUR 603 (source: https://www.dgb.de/service/ratgeber/mindestlohn/).
You can find further information at https://www.minijob-zentrale.de or https://www.dgb.de/service/ratgeber/minijobs/.
- Additional income limits as a student:
- There is an annual tax allowance for students. From January 2026, this allowance is EUR 12 348 (https://jugend.dgb.de/studium/faq-studierende/++co++d8431a3a-d57e-11ee-a82b-bbaaa81184f3).
- To avoid paying social security contributions on the income from their jobs performed alongside their studies (student jobs), or to keep such contributions to a minimum, students may work no more than 20 hours per week (during term time) (https://www.minijob-zentrale.de/DE/die-minijobs/studenten/detailseite). If they work more hours than this, they will their lose their student status with the Tax Office (Finanzamt).
- Exception: During semester breaks, at weekends and at night, students may work more than 20 hours without losing their working student status.
- If a student works full-time, their social security contributions will not increase but income tax will be deducted from their salary.
- As of 1 January 2026, students and school pupils may earn EUR 603 per month without BAföG being reduced. If they earn more than this, one of the consequences is that they will lose free family insurance cover, meaning that they have to take out health insurance themselves. To avoid these disadvantages, it is advisable to keep a close eye on your income and working hours.
- Part-time work: The agreed working time for part-time employees is shorter than the company’s normal working time. In principle, part-time employment is subject to the same provisions of labour law as full-time employment, since the only difference between the two employment relationships is the duration of the working time. In addition, the Part-time Working and Fixed-term Employment Contracts Act (Teilzeit- und Befristungsgesetz, TzBfG) contains special provisions. Further information can be found online (https://www.bmas.de/DE/Arbeit/Arbeitsrecht/Teilzeit-flexible-Arbeitszeit/teilzeit-flexible-arbeitszeit.html).
- Fixed-term employment relationship: There are two types of fixed-term contracts:
- Time-based contracts end at the contractually agreed point in time without needing to be terminated.
- Purpose-based contracts are concluded for a specific purpose (e.g. sickness cover, cover during parental leave, collaboration on a specific project). With purpose-based contracts, the contract ends after the employer notifies the employee that the purpose has been fulfilled. A notice period of two weeks must be given.
With regard to the permissibility of fixed-term employment, the special provisions of the TzBfG must be complied with.
- Teleworking (remote work): Remote workplaces are computer workstations set up by the employer in the employee’s home or place of residence. An employee can either work entirely from home or somewhere else away from the physical workplace (remotely), or they can alternate between working remotely and at the workplace. A teleworking job is established only when the company and the employee have laid down the teleworking conditions in an employment contract, a supplementary agreement, a works agreement or a collective agreement and the equipment required for the teleworking job has been provided. The most common form of teleworking is working from home (referred to in German as ‘Home Office’).
For companies with their headquarters in Germany, the German Liaison Office for Health Insurance – Abroad (Deutsche Verbindungstelle Krankenversicherung – Ausland) (http://www.dvka.de/) is the competent body for determining in which country social security contributions are due in the case of cross-border teleworking/remote working.
- Temporary employment relationship / employee assignment: The terms ‘temporary agency work’, ‘hiring-out of workers’ and ‘employee assignment’ refer to the situation in which an employer supplies an employee to a third party in return for remuneration and for a limited period of time. If such an employer assigns employees to other companies, the employer in question must generally have a licence to operate as a temping agency from the Federal Employment Agency. Temping agencies are inspected on a regular basis to ensure that they comply with legal regulations. An up-to-date list of all temping agencies can be found at https://www.spitzenverbaende.arbeitsagentur.de/. More detailed information can be found in the information sheet for temporary agency workers at https://www.arbeitsagentur.de/datei/merkblatt-leiharbeit_ba033905.pdf (PDF).

- entitlement to paid holiday,
- payment in lieu of untaken holiday,
- continued payment of wages in the event of illness,

In Germany, some jobs are subject to compulsory social security contributions and some jobs are exempt from compulsory social security contributions. Child labour is banned in Germany under the Act on the Protection of Young People at Work (Jugendarbeitsschutzgesetz, JArbSchG). Alongside the statutory minimum wage (EUR 13.90 gross per hour as of 1 January 2026), there are separate minimum wages in certain sectors. These minimum wages are agreed in collective agreements and are declared universally binding by the government (‘sectoral minimum wages’ – Branchenmindestlöhne). All companies in the respective sector must comply with the minimum wages (Collective Agreements Act [Tarifvertragsgesetz], the Posting of Workers Act [Arbeitnehmerentsendegesetz] and the Act on the Provision of Temporary Workers [Arbeitnehmerüberlassungsgesetz]).
Companies frequently pay remuneration exceeding the agreed pay scale to sought-after skilled workers. Higher wage and salary payments above the collectively agreed rate are characterised by the fact that collective wage agreements are applied in the company and therefore employees’ basic remuneration is regulated collectively. A voluntary, freely agreed supplement is paid in addition to this basic remuneration.
Exemptions from the minimum wage in Germany apply in the following cases:
- voluntary work
- people under the age of 18 without vocational training
- apprentices and people undergoing vocational training
- people performing mandatory traineeships as part of their school education or further education
- people performing voluntary traineeships of up to three months in length for orientation purposes before starting an apprenticeship or vocational training or a course of study
- people in long-term unemployment during the first six months of a job after ending unemployment
- young people taking part in introductory training for young people (Einstiegsqualifizierung), as preparation for an apprenticeship or vocational training, or taking part in other vocational preparation in accordance with the Vocational Training Act (Berufsbildungsgesetz)
From 1 January 2026, the minimum apprenticeship allowance (Mindestausbildungsvergütung, MiAV) for an apprentice starting their apprenticeship in 2026 rose to:
- EUR 724 for the first year of the apprenticeship;
- EUR 854 for the second year of the apprenticeship (an increase of 18% from the first year);
- EUR 977 for the third year of the apprenticeship (an increase of 35% from the first year); and
- EUR 1 014 for the fourth year of the apprenticeship (an increase of 40% from the first year).
Provision is made for an increase of 18% in the second year of the apprenticeship, 35% in the third and 40% in the fourth. The adjustment in subsequent years is linked to the average change in contractually agreed apprenticeship allowances (based on both collective agreements and individual contracts) and is made automatically. The minimum apprenticeship allowance is not the same for all apprentices in a year; it applies to apprentices who started their apprenticeship in this calendar year.
Training companies subject to collective wage agreements may pay apprentices the applicable collective apprenticeship allowances even if these are less than the above-mentioned rates. For companies not subject to collective wage agreements, the agreed apprenticeship allowance should not be more than 20% below the tariff rates applicable in their sector and region (https://www.bibb.de/de/199658.php).
At present, minimum standards for working conditions (particularly wages and paid leave) exist only in individual sectors as a result of statutory rules of the Federal Ministry of Labour and Social Affairs (Posting of Workers Act, 1996). These currently apply, among other sectors, to the main and ancillary construction industry, cleaners, postal workers, the care sector, security services and waste management (including street cleaning and winter road maintenance).
Salaries are normally transferred into the employee’s current account in the middle of the month or at the end of the month. Under trade regulations, the company is required to provide the employee with a statement setting out remuneration in a comprehensible text format. Wage tax and, if applicable, solidarity surcharge and church tax, and also the employee share of contributions for social security insurance (healthcare, pension, long-term care and unemployment insurance) are deducted from the agreed gross pay and transferred directly by the company to the relevant administrations.

Working time and breaks are stipulated in the Working Time Act (Arbeitszeitgesetz). This applies to workers, employees and people in initial vocational training. Senior management staff are not protected by this Act.
At present, the average set working week for full-time employment varies between 38 and 40 hours, depending on the collective wage agreement. As a rule, the working day should not exceed 8 hours and is restricted by law to a maximum of 10 hours. A break of at least 30 minutes is mandatory after 6 hours’ work; after 9 hours, the mandatory break is 45 minutes. A rest period of at least 11 hours must be observed after a full working day. Employees cannot normally be required to work on Sundays and public holidays.
For many workers, normal working hours i.e. eight working hours between 06:00 and 18:00 over five weekdays, is not the norm. This concerns emergency and rescue services, fire brigades, public safety workers, hospitals, restaurants, cultural institutions and transport. However, workers must have at least 15 work-free Sundays per year.
Many workers now have flexible working hours, such as ‘flexitime’. ‘Flexitime’ generally provides for core hours during which all employees must be present at the organisation. Employees are given the opportunity to work more or fewer hours within certain limits. As a rule, overtime hours can be accumulated to a limited extent and ‘taken off in lieu’ (in the form of hours or whole days off work) or (more rarely) paid out. Electronic time recording systems and working time accounts have been set up in businesses to record the hours of work performed by each individual. If such systems are not available, you should document your working hours yourself. The website of the Federal Ministry of Labour and Social Affairs (BMAS) has information on how to document your working time (sample sheet or app) (https://www.bmas.de/DE/Arbeit/Arbeitsrecht/Arbeitnehmerrechte/Regelungen-zur-Arbeitszeit/Fragen-und-Antworten/faq-arbeitszeiterfassung.html).
In Germany, employees can ask their employer at any time about working on a mobile basis; there is no legal regulation regarding this (yet). If the parties to the employment contract agree to the employee working on a mobile basis, this is usually based on a voluntary decision by the company.
Break regulations for specific categories of people: 
Young people must be granted breaks of an appropriate duration and these breaks must be determined in advance. Young people may not work longer than 4.5 consecutive hours without a break. The breaks must be at least:
- 30 minutes for working hours of between 4.5 and 6 hours;
- 60 minutes for working hours of over 6 hours.
The Maternity Protection Act (Mutterschutzgesetz) does not stipulate any different provisions for breaks for pregnant women and nursing mothers than those laid down in the Working Time Act (Arbeitszeitgesetz). However, there is an obligation to allow pregnant women who are constantly on their feet at work to briefly sit down and rest. Likewise, pregnant women who are always sat down at work must be allowed to briefly interrupt their work. Nursing mothers must be allowed to take time to breastfeed where necessary (at least two nursing breaks of 30 minutes or one nursing break of one hour per day for consecutive working hours of up to eight hours), separately from their breaks.
The break regulations for employees with disabilities are the same as the regulations under the Working Time Act. However, in accordance with Section 164(4) of Book IX of the German Social Code (Neuntes Buch Sozialgesetzbuch, SGB IX), the employer has an obligation to ensure that ‘workplaces, including the operating facilities, machinery and equipment, as well as the design of the work stations, working environment, work organisation and working hours, are established and maintained in a manner that is suitable for people with disabilities, giving particular consideration to hazards, ... taking into account the disability and the impact of this disability on the employed activity’. In addition to the company doctor, the treating doctor can also advise on such matters. Where necessary, the employer may under certain circumstances receive an employment subsidy from the Integration Offices (Integrationsämter) to compensate for the reduced performance on account of the disability.
Night/shift work:
The daily working hours of night workers must not exceed eight hours. Their hours may be extended to up to 10 hours only if the average daily working hours over a period of six calendar months or 24 weeks does not exceed eight hours.

The statutory minimum entitlement to leave is 24 working days per year (Federal Act on Leave, Bundesurlaubsgesetz). Special rules apply to certain groups of people, including young people under the age of 18 and disabled people. Full entitlement to leave is acquired only after the employment relationship has been in existence for six months.
Collective agreements stipulate leave of 30 working days for most employees. A person’s salary continues to be paid in full during this period. Anyone who consistently performs heavy or hazardous work will normally receive additional leave. Some collective wage agreements include arrangements for specific events in one’s private life. For instance, some organisations grant individual and additional special leave days for marriage, the death of a close relative or for moving house if you are transferred to a different location.
There is in principle a ban on working on public holidays, to which there are some exceptions. The Public Holidays Acts (Feiertagsgesetze) of the individual federal states determine the dates of public holidays in those federal states. The national public holidays are New Year’s Day (1 January), Good Friday, Easter Sunday and Easter Monday, Labour Day (1 May), Ascension Day, Whit Sunday and Whit Monday, the Day of German Unity (3 October), Christmas Day and Boxing Day (25 and 26 December).
There are clear rules governing absence on grounds of illness. In the event of illness, an employee must inform the organisation of their incapacity for work and the anticipated duration of that incapacity as soon as possible, as a rule no later than shortly before the start of the employee’s actual working time. In the case of illness lasting longer than three days, an employee must submit a certificate of incapacity for work issued by a doctor no later than the following working day. The employer can also require the certificate of incapacity for work issued by a doctor to be presented on the first day of illness. This obligation can be laid down in the employment contract, a works agreement or a collective bargaining agreement.
If an employee falls ill while on leave, the days on which the employee is unfit for work as verified by the doctor’s certificate are not classed as annual leave.
During parental leave, parents can be released from work by their employer to look after their child – the employment relationship is thus in abeyance during parental leave. However, parental leave also gives male and female employees the opportunity to work part-time so that they are able to devote themselves to their child and at the same time keep up with their job. Each parent is entitled to parental leave to care for and raise the child. Parental leave allowance is one of the most appreciated family benefits in Germany. It secures families’ economic livelihoods and helps fathers and mothers better reconcile family and work. It is intended to enable parents to take time to be with their child. Parental leave allowance is available as basic parental allowance, parental allowance plus and partnership bonus. These can be combined with each other. Parental leave allowance is also available to parents who live separately.
If an employee is providing longer-term care in their home environment for relatives suffering from illness, they may obtain partial or full leave from work for a period of up to six months. They must notify the employer no later than 10 days prior to the start of this period of care, and at the same time must indicate the intended duration and scope of the planned leave. A certificate must be provided as proof of the care dependency (min. care level 1). During the care leave, employees will not receive pay but will retain their social security coverage and have special protection against dismissal. This regulation applies only to companies with at least 15 employees.
Employees can take educational leave for the purpose of their further training. Employers must grant their employees paid leave for further training if they wish to take educational leave. Educational leave does not form part of normal leave. The federal states have their own laws on educational leave, which govern paid leave from work. Bavaria and Saxony are currently the only two federal states where there is no statutory entitlement to educational leave.
You can use educational leave for your own citizenship education, language courses (e.g. ‘German as a foreign language’) or for your further vocational training. The course content of the training or education received does not have to be related to your current employment. As for annual leave, you should agree on arrangements for educational leave with your employer at an early stage.

Permanent employment relationships end upon termination by at least one contracting party (employee or company), but no later than upon reaching statutory retirement age.
Permanent employment normally begins with a probation period of up to six months. During this trial period, a reduced period of notice of 14 days applies for both parties. Termination of an employment relationship must be made in writing. The statutory periods of notice are laid down in the Civil Code (Bürgerliches Gesetzbuch, BGB). As a rule, the longer an employee has been with a company, the longer the periods of notice that must be given by the company.

Fixed-term employment with a written employment contract automatically comes to an end when the agreed period expires. In those cases the organisation is not required to give notice of termination. When your employment relationship ends, you are entitled to a reference from your employer.
The termination of working life and entitlement to the standard old-age pension are currently being changed in stages. For those born up until 1946, the retirement age was 65. The retirement age for those born after that year is gradually being increased at the moment. As of 2019, the statutory retirement age for those born from 1964 onwards, is 67. However, early retirement is still possible, for example in the event of severe disability or contributions having been paid for many years. The longer contributions have been paid into the pension scheme, the higher the pension. Child-raising periods and the care of relatives are also taken into account as qualifying contributory periods.
In addition, a pension is payable for reduced capacity to work if you can no longer work or can only work part-time on account of an illness or disability. The amount of the pension in that regard depends on your contribution periods and the degree of incapacity.

Trade unions negotiate collective agreements with undertakings, which regulate income, working time and leave for the undertaking. In the event of industrial action, they organise a strike and pay their members strike pay. They help set up works councils and support employees in company conflicts and represent them in disputes with their company. Trade union members have free legal protection in disputes relating to labour and social law. Some trade unions also provide free leisure time and accident insurance. Membership of a trade union is voluntary and subject to contributions. For example, the German Trade Union Confederation (Deutscher Gewerkschaftsbund, DGB) is the umbrella organisation for eight individual trade unions, with a total membership of around 5.8 million.
The works council (Betriebsrat) (or, in the public sector, the staff committee (Personalrat)) is democratically elected by the employees. Its main tasks may be summarised as follows:
- it represents employees vis-à-vis the company in dealings with companies relating to personnel and social matters;
- it monitors compliance with laws, regulations, health and safety provisions, collective agreements and works agreements;
- it cooperates in workplace design, regulation of working time, personnel planning and in-service training;
- it must be given a hearing whenever an employee is dismissed, otherwise the dismissal is invalid.
The Works Constitution Act (Betriebsverfassungsgesetz) (or, in the case of establishments governed by public law, the relevant staff representation legislation (Personalvertretungsgesetz)) governs the representation of employees. For the appointment or election of a works council, the company in question must have at least five employees aged 18 or over.
Foreign employees have the same rights to vote and stand as a candidate as their German colleagues.

Normally the works council and the company seek to work together in a spirit of mutual trust. If the interests of the two partners diverge, however, disputes can arise.
If wage negotiations collapse and arbitration attempts fail, trade unions in Germany are legally permitted to strike. Before a strike is called, however, a secret ballot must be held in which at least 75% of unionised employees, or those entitled to vote and not barred from doing so, vote in favour of industrial action. Token strikes – brief work stoppages designed to reinforce demands during wage negotiations – are also permitted after an agreed cooling-off period. All employees of the company against which the trade union has called a strike are entitled to come out on strike, regardless of whether or not they are union members. Only civil servants are not allowed to strike in Germany.
No reprisals may be taken that affect the legal status of employees who take part in a lawful strike. Your employment contract will remain in force during a strike, but no remuneration is payable. Union members receive strike support from their union to compensate for this loss of earnings. During industrial action, employees may be locked out by companies, meaning temporary exclusion from employment and pay. However, lockouts are restricted and can only take place within the scope of the collective bargaining agreement, to ensure that things remain proportional. They can only be implemented following a decision by the employers’ association, and the trade unions must be notified so that they can review the legality of the measure. If no such notification is provided, the lockout is unlawful and the employees remain entitled to payment.

The term Vocational Education and Training refers to practical activities and courses related to a specific occupation or vocation, aimed at preparing participants for their future careers. Vocational training is an essential means to achieve professional recognition and improve chances to get a job. It is therefore vital that vocational training systems in Europe respond to the needs of citizens and the labour market in order to facilitate access to employment.
Vocational education and training has been an essential part of EU policy since the very establishment of the European Community. It is also a crucial element of the so-called EU Lisbon Strategy, which aims at transforming Europe into the world’s most competitive and dynamic knowledge-based society. In 2002 the European Council reaffirmed this vital role, and established yet another ambitious goal – to make European education and training renowned globally by the year 2010 – by championing a number of world-class initiatives, and in particular by strengthening cooperation in the area of vocational training.
On 24 November 2020, the Council of the European Union adopted a Recommendation on vocational education and training for sustainable competitiveness, social fairness and resilience.
The Recommendation defines key principles for ensuring that vocational education and training is agile in that it adapts swiftly to labour market needs and provides quality learning opportunities for young people and adults alike.
It places a strong focus on the increased flexibility of vocational education and training, reinforced opportunities for work-based learning, apprenticeships and improved quality assurance.
The Recommendation also replaces the EQAVET – European Quality Assurance in Vocational Education and Training – Recommendation and includes an updated EQAVET Framework with quality indicators and descriptors. It repeals the former ECVET Recommendation.
To promote these reforms, the Commission supports Centres of Vocational Excellence (CoVEs) which bring together local partners to develop ‘skills ecosystems'. Skills ecosystems will contribute to regional, economic and social development, innovation and smart specialisation strategies.

Adult Education and Lifelong Learning in Europe
Lifelong learning is a process that involves all forms of education – formal, informal and non-formal – and lasts from the pre-school period until after retirement. It is meant to enable people to develop and maintain key competencies throughout their life as well as to empower citizens to move freely between jobs, regions and countries. Lifelong learning is also a core element of the previously mentioned Lisbon Strategy, as it is crucial for self-development and the raising of competitiveness and employability. The EU has adopted several instruments for the promotion of adult education in Europe.
A European area of lifelong learning
In order to make lifelong learning a reality in Europe, the European Commission has set itself the objective of creating a European Area of Lifelong Learning. In this context, the Commission focuses on identifying the needs of both learners and the labour market in order to make education more accessible and subsequently create partnerships between public administrations, suppliers of educational services and civil society.
This EU initiative is based on the objective of providing basic skills – by strengthening counselling and information services at a European level, and by recognising all forms of learning, including formal education and informal and non-formal training.

## Living conditions

Summary of living conditions in Europe 

Quality of life – on top of the EU social policy agenda
Favourable living conditions depend on a wide range of factors, such as quality healthcare services, education and training opportunities or good transport facilities, just to name a few aspects affecting citizens’ everyday life and work. The European Union has set for itself the aim to constantly improve the quality of life in all its Member States, and to take into account the new challenges of contemporary Europe, such as socially exclude people or an aging population.
Employment in Europe
Improving employment opportunities in Europe is a key priority for the European Commission. With the prospect of tackling the problem of unemployment and increasing the mobility between jobs and regions, a wide variety of initiatives at EU level are being developed and implemented to support the European Employment strategy. These include the European Employment Services network (EURES) and the EU Skills Panorama.
Health and healthcare in the European Union
Health is a cherished value, influencing people’s daily lives and therefore an important priority for all Europeans. A healthy environment is crucial for our individual and professional development, and EU citizens are ever more demanding about health and safety at work and the provision of high quality healthcare services. They require quick and easy access to medical treatment when travelling across the European Union. EU health policies are aimed at responding to these needs.
The European Commission has developed a coordinated approach to health policy, putting into practice a series of initiatives that complement the actions of national public authorities. The Union’s common actions and objectives are included in EU health programmes and strategies.
The current EU4Health Programme (2021-2027) is the EU’s ambitious response to COVID-19. The pandemic has a major impact on patients, medical and healthcare staff, and health systems in Europe. The new EU4Health programme will go beyond crisis response to address healthcare systems’ resilience. 
EU4Health, established by Regulation (EU) 2021/522, will provide funding to eligible entities, health organisations and NGOs from EU countries, or non-EU countries associated to the programme.
With EU4Health, the EU will invest €5.3 billion in current prices in actions with an EU added value, complementing EU countries’ policies and pursuing one or several of EU4Health´s objectives:
- To improve and foster health in the Union
- disease prevention & health promotion
- international health initiatives & cooperation
- To tackle cross-border health threats
- prevention, preparedness & response to cross-border health threats
- complementing national stockpiling of essential crisis-relevant products
- establishing a reserve of medical, healthcare & support staff
- To improve medicinal products, medical devices and crisis-relevant products
- making medicinal products, medical devices and crisis-relevant products available and affordable
- To strengthen health systems, their resilience and resource efficiency
- strengthening health data, digital tools & services, digital transformation of healthcare
- improving access to healthcare
- developing and implementing EU health legislation and evidence-based decision making
- integrated work among national health systems
Education in the EU
Education in Europe has both deep roots and great diversity. Already in 1976, education ministers decided to set up an information network to better understand educational policies and systems in the then nine-nation European Community. This reflected the principle that the particular character of an educational system in any one Member State ought to be fully respected, while coordinated interaction between education, training and employment systems should be improved. Eurydice, the information network on education in Europe, was formally launched in 1980.
In 1986, attention turned from information exchanges to student exchanges with the launch of the Erasmus programme, now grown into the Erasmus+programme, often cited as one of the most successful initiatives of the EU.
Transport in the EU
Transport was one of the first common policies of the then European Community. Since 1958, when the Treaty of Rome entered into force, the EU’s transport policy has focused on removing border obstacles between Member States, thereby enabling people and goods to move quickly, efficiently and cheaply.
This principle is closely connected to the EU’s central goal of a dynamic economy and cohesive society. The transport sector generates 10% of EU wealth measured by gross domestic product (GDP), equivalent to about one trillion Euros a year. It also provides more than ten million jobs.

The creation of a single European market in air transport has meant lower fares and a wider choice of carriers and services for passengers. The EU has also created a set of rights to ensure air passengers are treated fairly.

As an air passenger, you have certain rights when it comes to information about flights and reservations, damage to baggage, delays and cancellations, denied boarding, compensation in the case of accident or difficulties with package holidays. Political system:
The Federal Republic of Germany is a federal parliamentary democracy. The political system involves a vertical separation of powers between the federal government and the federal states, and a two-chamber system at federal level, consisting of the Bundestag (lower house) and the Bundesrat (upper house). The following parties are represented in the 21st German Bundestag (legislative period 2025-2029) as of February 2026:
- CDU/CSU (Christian Democratic Union / Christian-Social Union): 208 seats
- AfD (Alternative for Germany): 151 seats
- SPD (Social Democratic Party of Germany): 120 seats
- Bündnis 90 / Die Grünen (Alliance 90 / The Greens): 85 seats
- Die Linke (The Left): 64 seats
- Independent/non-affiliated: 2 seats
The Bundestag is made up of a total of 630 elected MPs or ‘Abgeordnete’ . The Bundestag has its permanent seat in the Reichstag building in Berlin. The MPs are elected every four years in general, direct, free, equal and secret elections. The main responsibilities of the Bundestag are:
- to elect the Federal Chancellor;
- to enact laws at federal level;
- to oversee the Federal Government; and
- to pass the federal budget.
The Federal Chancellor proposes the Federal Ministers, who are then officially appointed by the Federal President.
The Bundesrat is the body representing the 16 federal states (Länder) at federal level. It is involved in passing legislation and federal administration, above all where the interests of the federal states are affected. The Bundesrat is composed of members of the governments of the federal states. The number of votes in the Bundesrat depends on the population of each individual federal states, and varies from three to six votes.
Administrative structure:
Germany is structured as a federal republic. Administrative tasks are distributed on three levels:
- the Federal Government
- the federal states
- the local authorities (towns and municipalities).
A large proportion of the administrative work is taken on by the federal states and the local authorities. They put many federal laws into practice at regional and local level. The local authorities have their own responsibilities as part of their constitutionally guaranteed self-government (e.g. local infrastructure, schools, social services), and are also entrusted with tasks on behalf of the Federal Government or the respective federal state. An overview of the competent authorities and administrative services can be found on the Service Search website provided by the Federal Government and federal states (https://servicesuche.bund.de).
Labour administration and support for skilled workers from abroad:
One of the largest authorities in Germany is the Federal Employment Agency, which provides services for the labour market. These include the following in particular:
- employment services
- career advice
- promoting and developing employment
- training and skills-related measures
- administration of unemployment insurance.
Depending on your specific needs and situation, the employment agency for your place of residence or the local job centre will be your point of contact.
International skilled workers can obtain detailed information before arrival from the International Placement Service (Zentrale Auslands- und Fachvermittlung, ZAV). The ZAV is a special division of the Federal Employment Agency. It supports employers with international recruitment and advises skilled workers on employment opportunities in Germany (https://www.zav.de). The ‘Make it in Germany’ portal (https://www.make-it-in-germany.com) is the Federal Government’s official information portal for international skilled workers. It provides information on:
- visas and residence permits;
- recognition of foreign qualifications;
- the employment market and job seeking; and
- living in Germany.
The Office for the Equal Treatment of EU Workers (https://www.eu-gleichbehandlungsstelle.de) supports EU citizens who are already living or working in Germany. Its aim is to inform EU workers about their rights under employment and social security legislation and to offer advice for specific problems. Information is available on its website in German and other EU languages. You can also use the website to find an information centre close to your home or submit an online form.
Legal system and main legal principles:
Germany applies a system of codified law. In other words, all legal rules and regulations are laid down in formal laws. The central pillar of private law in Germany is the Civil Code (Bürgerliches Gesetzbuch, BGB). It governs the following, among others:
- contract law
- property law
- family law
- inheritance law.
For matters under private law (e.g. employment contracts, leases), it is recommended, if necessary, to obtain professional legal advice (e.g. a lawyer or information centre).
The ‘Gesetze im Internet’ (‘Laws Online’) portal provides a comprehensive overview of applicable federal laws (https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de).

The median gross salary in Germany is currently EUR 53 900, while the average gross salary is a bit higher, at EUR 59 100, indicating some extremely high top-end salaries. Taking into account all employees in Germany (including part-time employees and workers in minor employment), the average gross monthly salary in 2024 was approximately EUR 3 667. The average net monthly income for all employees was EUR 2 697. The gender pay gap – the pay gap between men and women – is currently 9.7% (as at January 2026), meaning that it has decreased and is now in single digits for the first time in many years.
People with a number of years of professional experience earn roughly EUR 13 000 more than entry-level employees (EUR 46 250), while large companies with up to 5 000 employees generally pay up to EUR 14 000 more than small businesses with fewer than 50 employees (EUR 48 800). University graduates earn, on average, roughly EUR 17 000 gross more per annum than non-graduates (EUR 51 200).
There are significant differences in pay between various professions in Germany. Doctors earn a median salary of EUR 105 500, which is almost twice as much as the average German. Engineers earn above-average salaries too, with a median salary of EUR 75 000, closely followed by technical developers, who earn a median salary of EUR 72 250. IT specialists, and specialists in business administration and management also earn high salaries, with a median annual salary of EUR 66 750.
Right at the other end of the scale in 2025 were people working in food production (EUR 46 500), office management (EUR 47 250) and retail and crafts (EUR 48 750).
According to StepStone’s 2026 Salary Report, the ‘industry’ (as opposed to the ‘profession’) with the highest salaries in 2025 was banking (median salary EUR 70 250). Other industries with above-average salaries in 2025 include aerospace (EUR 68 000), insurance (EUR 66 500) and pharmaceuticals (EUR 66 250).
People working in agriculture, forestry and fishing and horticulture receive considerably lower salaries (EUR 47 250), as do those working in hotels, restaurants and catering (EUR 45 500). Salaries are also below average (at roughly EUR 49 750) in crafts and in the leisure industry, tourism industry, culture and sport.
There are various types of tax in Germany, which are levied at federal, state and municipal level. The main tax types are:
- Income tax: A progressive tax on the income of natural persons. The tax rate starts at 14% and rises to 42%, with a top tax rate of 45% for very high incomes.
- Value added tax (sales tax): An indirect tax on the consumption of goods and services. The standard rate is 19%; the reduced rate is 7% and applies to certain basic daily necessities such as food and books.
- Vehicle tax: A tax on the ownership of motor vehicles. The rate is determined by the cubic capacity, CO₂ emissions and emission class. You can use the vehicle tax calculator provided by the Federal Ministry of Finance ( BMF) to determine the exact tax payable on your vehicle.
- Local taxes: These include property tax for property owners and business tax for companies, for example, and are levied by the municipalities.
The net income depends on a variety of factors, including the gross income, tax bracket, social security contributions and individual tax allowances. As a rough guide, a single working person with a monthly gross salary of EUR 4 500 can expect to take home approximately EUR 2 700 to EUR 2 900 after the deduction of taxes and social security contributions. However, these figures can vary depending on the individual situation.
- Various deductions are made from the gross wages (as at 2 January 2026):
- Social security contributions: These comprise contributions to pension insurance (18.6% of the gross wage, halved between the employer and employee), unemployment insurance (2.6%), health insurance (generally 14.6% plus additional contribution) and nursing care insurance (3.6%; for persons without children: 4.2%).
- Income tax: As described above, dependent on the taxable income.
- Solidarity surcharge: A surcharge of 5.5% on income tax; however, this no longer applies to most taxpayers as of 2021, since an exemption threshold was introduced then, which applies to roughly 90% of taxpayers. The exemption threshold provides tax relief for people on low and middle incomes. The solidarity surcharge only applies if the actual annual wage tax exceeds the following values: EUR 20 350 for a single tax return; EUR 40 700 for a joint tax return
Tax concessions for certain categories of people:
- Married couples: Benefit from income splitting, whereby the joint income is halved and then taxed, which often results in a lower tax burden.
- Pensioners: Certain proportional tax allowances for elderly retired persons and other tax allowances can reduce the tax burden.
- Other categories: There are various tax allowances and flat rates, e.g. for single parents, disabled people or certain occupational groups.
Anyone residing in Germany or staying in Germany for more than six months in a calendar year must pay tax on their entire income from home and abroad. As an employee, tax is automatically deducted from wages. The amount of income tax is based on income level and marital status. Individual living conditions are taken into account when calculating taxable income. For example, unmarried persons or married persons with children are placed in different tax brackets.
Filing a tax return is optional in Germany. In some cases, there is an obligation to submit a tax return by 31 July of the following year, e.g. for those who earn additional income on top of their wages or receive unemployment benefit, sickness benefit or short-time allowance. In the case of several employment relationships or certain tax class combinations, the filing of a tax return is obligatory.
Those who are not obliged to submit a tax return do not have to submit one, but can do so voluntarily. Submitting a voluntary tax return is particularly worthwhile if, for example, the employee had to pay high income-related expenses, special expenses or exceptional costs, or got married during the year. In these cases, a tax refund may be possible.
In the case of a relocation within the European Economic Area, taxes that have already been paid may be refunded or offset under certain circumstances. It is advisable to seek advice on your individual situation, as the rules are complex and depend on the relevant double taxation agreements.

In Germany, there are two main scenarios when it comes to finding accommodation: renting an apartment or house and purchasing a property. There are specific rules and standard procedures to follow in both cases.
Renting a property:
Rental apartments and houses are often advertised by landlords in online property portals, in daily newspapers or through estate agents. Demand is high, especially in big cities, which can often lead to long and complicated application processes. Interested parties provide the required personal information, three up-to-date salary statements, a SCHUFA report (credit check) and sometimes further references. Landlords often give preference to applicants with a reliable income and good credit rating.
The tenancy agreement sets out the terms of the tenancy for both parties (rent, ancillary expenses, notice periods, repairs and maintenance, etc.). Most tenancy agreements in Germany are open-ended (but may be terminated prematurely by the landlord, e.g. if they wish to use the property themselves). The tenancy agreement often also lays down provisions on graduated rents (increase in rent over a certain period). In the event of doubt, either party may refer to this agreement. 
Legally, the rent deposit may be no more than three months’ net rent (Section 551 BGB). It is generally paid into a trust account, earns interest and is used to protect the landlord against any damage that the tenant(s) may cause to the rental property. Tenancies are heavily regulated by the Civil Code (Bürgerliches Gesetzbuch, BGB), which means that tenants are well protected. For example, the landlord is responsible for maintenance, while the tenant must treat the accommodation with due care. In addition to the net rent, there are running costs or ‘service charges’ (e.g. water, waste collection, heating, electricity), which are often charged monthly.
Purchasing a property:
Properties can be found via online portals, estate agents or local (online) networks. In popular areas, prices are high and options are limited. The purchase agreement is drawn up together with a notary and must be notarised (Section 311b BGB). Before the agreement is notarised, the buyer inspects the property and reviews the contract details (e.g. entry in the land register, encumbrances). The notary ensures legal certainty and enters the new owner in the land register. Payment of the land transfer tax (3.5% to 6.5% of the purchase price, depending on the federal state) is also coordinated by the notary. Once ownership has been entered into the land register, the purchase price is transferred by the buyer. Often, the buyer must provide proof of equity to secure financing from the bank. In addition to the purchase price, notary costs, land register fees and estate agent fees are payable. The estate agent fee is generally between 5.95% and 7.14% of the purchase price (including VAT), depending on federal state / region, and is usually shared between the buyer (up to 50%) and seller.
Role of estate agents and authorities:
These broker properties between the buyer/tenant and seller/landlord. Their services include creating exposés (a detailed source of information on the property), organising viewings and providing support with the contract process. When renting a property, authorities are often not directly involved in the process except for the registration of residence by the buyer/tenant. When buying a property, Land Registries (Grundbuchämter) and Tax Offices (Finanzämter) provide the necessary documents and process tax payments. Notaries play a key role in the property purchase process, as they notarise the purchase agreements and enter the new owner into the land register.
Availability and prices:
In urban centres and major cities like Munich, Frankfurt and Hamburg, both rents and property prices are high, and demand often exceeds supply. However, prices can vary significantly: in big cities, apartments can cost up to EUR 3 361 per square metre to buy (depending on the federal state) and up to EUR 23.35 per square metre to rent on average (Munich) (as at Q4 2025), while rural regions are often much cheaper.
Tips:
In addition to rental charges, you also have to pay running expenses and for your own consumption of water, electricity and heating. You should allow around 25-30% of your monthly rent for this purpose.
All larger cities have tenants’ associations. Addresses and contact persons can be found on the website of the German Tenants’ Association. It offers advice and support to help you deal with problems concerning landlords. However, you must generally be a member of your local tenants’ association for consultations.

Employees, including apprentices, are required to pay social security contributions. This means that companies and employees pay a contribution to statutory pension, unemployment, sickness and care insurance each month. The amount of contributions paid depends on your gross salary. As a rule, the company pays half of the contributions and the employee pays the other half. Salary deductions for social security contributions are subject to a specific upper limit (known as the ‘contribution assessment ceiling’ – Beitragsbemessungsgrenze. As of 2026, this is EUR 5 812.50 per month for nursing care insurance and EUR 8 450.00 per month for pension insurance and unemployment insurance.
If you intend to work in Germany, you must always take out health insurance as an employee (national health insurance) as soon as you sign a German employment contract. Statutory health insurance is particularly attractive for families, because family members can be included in a family insurance policy, subject to certain conditions, free of charge. Non-working spouses and children can also be included in the insurance. If you have statutory health insurance, all you need to do is to register the respective family member(s) with your health insurance fund. This family insurance option does not apply with private health insurance, for which family members must be insured separately under an additional policy. As a member of the national health insurance scheme (Gesetzliche Krankenversicherung, GKV), you are automatically also covered for nursing care.
Employees can take out private health insurance if, over the course of a year, their gross monthly income has exceeded the compulsory insurance limit of EUR 69 750 per year
(EUR 5 812.50 per month / assessment ceiling for 2026). The compulsory insurance limit is set annually by the legislator.
Self-employed people, freelancers and artists are generally privately insured regardless of their income level, as are tenured civil servants and other persons entitled to receive benefits (Beihilfeberechtigte) such as judges, members of a Landtag (regional assembly) and members of the Bundestag.
The nationwide contribution assessment ceiling for statutory pension and unemployment insurance exceeded EUR 100 000 per year for the first time in 2026 (EUR 101 400 per year).
For a temporary stay in another Member State, EU citizens and citizens of the European Economic Area (EEA) merely require a European Health Insurance Card (EHIC) in order to receive medical treatment if they fall ill.
You can find addresses and telephone numbers for doctor’s offices and dental practices on the internet. Health insurance companies also offer help in finding practices, or the Association of Statutory Health Insurance Physicians (Kassenärztliche Vereinigung) through their ‘Terminservice’ (‘appointment service’) (tel. 116117) or at https://www.116117-termine.de).
You should first make an appointment by telephone or online before visiting a doctor. For acute illnesses or accidents, you will usually be given an appointment immediately or on the same day. Otherwise, you will have to wait for several days or even weeks, particularly for specialists. Very few practices are open on Saturdays, and only medical assistance on call and public A&E departments can be accessed on Sundays.
If, after being examined, you have received a prescription for the prescribed medication from the doctor, pharmacies usually charge an additional fee of between EUR 5 and 10 per item. In the case of minor complaints, you will receive non-prescription medicines at a local pharmacy or online. You can get free medical advice (no diagnosis) in all pharmacies, even without visiting a doctor.
If medical practices are closed, a doctor on call will assist you. You can contact medical assistance on call outside surgery hours (Monday to Friday), at night, at weekends and on public holidays by calling 116117. You also have the option of going to the accident and emergency department of a public hospital. A few pharmacies are also open at weekends and on public holidays (emergency service). Further information can be found online.
If you require emergency medical services, dial 112 (nationwide).

The area of preschool childcare and education is diverse and includes day nurseries (Kindertageseinrichtungen, KiTa) for children aged 1 to 3 (baby nurseries, childminders, mixed-age kindergartens or parenting initiatives) and for children aged 3 to 6 (predominantly kindergartens, but also childminders and preschool classes). Childcare costs vary depending on the local authority. Local authorities assume the bulk of the costs, regardless of whether childcare is provided by a municipal or private institution or a childminder. Parents pay a contribution calculated on the basis of their family income. Private kindergartens are often more expensive than municipal facilities. With effect from 1 August 2019, under the Good Daycare Facilities Act (Gute-KiTa-Gesetz), all parents who receive child, housing or basic social benefits (Arbeitslosengeld II) for beneficiaries who are fit for work in accordance with Book II of the German Social Code (Zweites Buch Sozialgesetzbuch, SGB II) are exempt from KiTa fees.
Compulsory school begins with primary school (Grundschule) (Years 1 to 4) when a child reaches the age of 6. In some federal states (currently Berlin and Brandenburg), there is also a six-year primary school, or an orientation phase not attached to any school type (schulartunabhängige Orientierungsstufe) in Years 5 and 6 (Mecklenburg-West Pomerania); both count towards the junior secondary level. Attendance at state schools is free of charge. Parents have to pay only for schoolbooks, additional teaching materials, and class excursions and trips. The number of general private schools in Germany has gone up by 7% in the past 10 years, as has the number of children and young people attending such schools; in 2024/2025, this figure was 12% for Germany as a whole (as of January 2026, Destatis).
Parents are given a carefully considered recommendation for their child’s learning pathway with the half-year report for the final year of primary school. The aim of this recommendation is to help parents choose the right type of school for their child. However, the weighting attached to the primary school recommendation varies from one federal state to another. While in most federal states, this recommendation merely serves as guidance for parents when choosing a school for their child, it is partially binding in Brandenburg, Thuringia and Bavaria (https://www.bildungsserver.de/schule/uebergang-zur-weiterfuehrenden-schule-12373-de.html). The secondary school decides whether the child is accepted based on its capacity for new students. To receive a recommendation for a grammar school (Gymnasium), a child must generally be able to demonstrate average grades of 2.0 to 2.5 (depending on the federal state) in both maths and German.
There is a choice between:
- secondary modern school (Hauptschule) (to Year 9 or 10),
- intermediate school (Realschule) (intermediate school-leaving certificate at the end of Year 10), or
- grammar school (Gymnasium) (ending with the (Fach-)Abitur, the advanced certificate of education), which – depending on the Land – goes up to Year 12 or 13.
The Abitur allows immediate access to college or university education. 
A Realschule leaving certificate combined with a successfully completed apprenticeship and relevant professional experience also allows access to a university of applied sciences (Fachhochschule). 
A comprehensive school (Gesamtschule) is a special type of school that offers several types of schooling (Hauptschule and Realschule leaving certificates and Abitur) under one roof from Year 5. However, Gesamtschulen do not exist in every federal state.
Vocational dual training can start after finishing school (secondary model school, intermediate school and Abitur). It takes place both as vocational education in vocational schools and in companies. Training lasts between 2 and 3.5 years. Young people can choose from among over 330 recognised regulated professions. The term ‘recognised regulated profession’ (anerkannter Ausbildungsberuf) is defined by the Federal Education Act (Bundesbildungsgesetz) and forms the legal basis for the content of in-house vocational training.
In addition to dual apprenticeships, there is the option of full-time school-based training at vocational schools. Training qualifications suitable for a professional occupation can be obtained in two to three years. These include healthcare occupations governed by federal law, such as the nursing professions, and training in the fields of speech therapy, occupational therapy and physiotherapy.
Germany currently has over 400 publicly funded or State-recognised higher education establishments. You can find an overview of the German higher education landscape and the availability of courses at www.hochschulkompass.de.

Germany offers a balanced mix of active recreational activities and relaxed social gatherings. It is Germany’s excellent quality of life and focus on work-life balance in particular that make the country an attractive place to live and work.
Being social is important to Germans. In summer in particular, people like to get together outdoors, for example in beer gardens or at a private barbecue in someone’s garden. A barbecue offers a good opportunity to spend time with neighbours or friends.
Germany is home to an impressive variety of natural landscapes. Hiking is one of the most popular activities in Germany as it is easily accessible and cheap. Cycling is another popular activity, both as a mode of transport and as a sporting activity. Roughly half of all households in Germany have their own garden or an allotment (a rented plot of land), where they often grow flowers or vegetables. This activity combines relaxation with a sense of sustainability.
Sports clubs are an important part of German culture. Many people are a member of a club, be it for football, handball, tennis or gymnastics, or for lesser known sports like archery. These clubs provide an opportunity to meet new, like-minded people.
For Germans, going to the gym and running in the park are almost as much a part of everyday life as the daily commute to work. 11.71 million people in Germany are active members of sports clubs, amounting to 13.8% of the entire population.
Germans like to spend a lot of time in the cosy comfort of their home (‘in heimischer Gemütlichkeit’), especially in the colder winter months. Cooking and baking, often with family or friends, are popular activities, as our social games, which have a long tradition in Germany and are one of the country’s most popular pastimes. Games evenings with friends or family are therefore common.
There are lots of public parties and festivals in spring, summer and autumn, but Germans also like to organise their own celebrations and events. Typical social activities include Easter fires (Osterfeuer), making decorations during Advent, and enjoying a mug of mulled wine in the winter.
Many people are very technically adept and spend their evenings at home watching a series or film via streaming services or gaming.

Whether it is the birth of a child, marriage, divorce or the death of a relative – if you are faced with a new situation in your life or have to complete an administrative procedure, the citizens’ bureau (Bürgerdienst), citizens’ service (Bürgerservice) or citizens’ office (Bürgeramt) of your city or municipality is at your disposal. You can gain an overview of all the services on offer and access to particular procedures and forms on the internet portals of the city or municipality in which you live.
Parents have one week after the birth of a child to register the newborn in the district of the birth. However, there are often arrangements between clinics and the registry office through which the formalities are completed with ease. Otherwise, the midwife or doctor issues the notification of birth to be submitted to the registry office.
In Germany, civil law and state-recognised marriages are performed at a registry office. A church wedding can take place only between two people who have already been married in accordance with civil law, i.e. soon after a registry office wedding. Since 2017, same-sex couples have also been able to enter into marriage in Germany.
The death of a person must be confirmed in writing by a doctor (confirmation of death). Where the cause of death cannot be identified, in particular where outside intervention, outside cause, or failure to provide help is suspected, the police must be informed. The death certificate is issued by the registry office of the place where the death occurred. The medical certificate of cause of death (Todesbescheinigung), the passport and, if applicable, the birth certificate or family record book, are required for the death certificate to be issued.

Germany has a regional road network of over 230 000 km. Around 13 200 km of this are motorways. Germany comes 10th in the world in terms of countries with the largest road networks, with around 830 000 kilometres (as of December 2025). On Germany’s motorways, a kilometre-based user charge is levied only on heavy goods vehicles.
With its key location in central Europe, Germany is a hub for international air traffic. There are flight connections from Germany to all regions of the world. There are also frequent flights connecting the major cities in Germany.
The railways are the most environmentally friendly form of motorised transport, way ahead of cars and planes. An initial overview of the long-distance rail network of Deutsche Bahn and the regions’ local networks can be found in German and eight other languages on the Deutsche Bahn website at www.bahn.de.
Cheap rail fares (‘Super Sparpreis’ – supersaver fare) for journeys all over Germany are available from EUR 30, or even less in some cases. However, such cheap fares are normally subject to time restrictions – for specific dates or if booked in advance.
The standard price for a one-way train ticket from Hamburg to Munich is currently between EUR 80 and EUR 140, depending on the day. A plane ticket for the same journey costs between EUR 100 and EUR 250, depending on the airline.
The Deutschlandticket was launched on 1 May 2023. It is environmentally friendly, digital, valid nationwide and cancellable monthly. It allows the use of local public transport throughout Germany (buses, underground, S-Bahn trains, trams and RE/RB trains, but not IC or ICE trains) at a flat-rate price for an unlimited number of journeys at any time of day, regardless of federal state borders or tariff zones. The ticket is currently available for EUR 63 per month on a subscription basis.
Travellers can also travel with more than 300 long-distance bus lines on over 4 000 different routes across Germany. A ticket from Hamburg to Munich costs between around EUR 35 and EUR 50.
Car sharing is also possible in cities in Germany. The costs of local transport depend on the region.

## Employment and support for persons with disabilities

Whether you are a person with a disability considering employment or an employer seeking to support inclusive hiring, this guide provides clear, accessible advice and direct links to further resources. It is designed to help you understand the rights of persons with disabilities, available support and practical steps for successful employment and workplace inclusion in this country.

Definition and recognition 

Definition:
In Germany, a person is considered to have a disability if they have a physical, mental, intellectual, or sensory impairment that, together with barriers in society, is likely to prevent equal participation for more than six months. This definition is based on the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (UN CRPD) and is set out in Section 2(1) of Book IX of the German Social Code (SGB IX).
There are various groups: people with (impending) disabilities in general, rehabilitation patients, and people with severe disabilities. All these groups have different eligibility requirements for state benefits.
Recognition:
A person is recognised as ‘severely disabled’ if they have a degree of disability (Grad der Behinderung, GdB) of at least 50 and are legally resident, ordinarily resident, or employed in Germany. Recognition as ‘severely disabled’ is important for accessing specific rights and support, such as protection against dismissal and tax benefits.
Procedure

- The degree of disability (GdB) is determined by the local welfare office (Versorgungsamt) (https://www.bih.de/integrationsaemter/kontakt/) in your federal state.
- To apply, submit medical documentation to your local welfare office (https://www.bih.de/integrationsaemter/kontakt/). The welfare office will assess your case and issue a decision.
- If your GdB is 50 or higher, you will receive a severely disabled person’s ID card (Schwerbehindertenausweis) upon request (https://www.schwerbehindertenausweis.de/behinderung/antrag-und-verfahren). If it is below 50, you will receive a written notice stating your assessed degree of disability.
Recognition grants access to various rights and support measures, including workplace adaptations and social benefits.
If you have a GdB of 30 or 40, you can apply to the Federal Employment Agency for equivalence status. If this is approved, you have the same essential employment rights as severely disabled people.
Further information and application forms are available for applying for a severe disability pass (https://gesund.bund.de/schwerbehinderung) and for details about severe disability (https://www.familienratgeber.de/rechte-leistungen/schwerbehinderung).How are employers supported? 

Employers in Germany can access a range of support and funding options when hiring or training persons with disabilities or severe disabilities. These include:

Transport discounts and assistance:
People with disabilities are entitled to various discounts (https://gesund.bund.de/schwerbehinderung) on local and long-distance public transport, depending on the degree of disability and the markings on their ID card. Accompanying people may also be eligible for discounts.
Passenger rights:
If public transport is not an option, transport services or individual transport costs may be covered, depending on your situation and the purpose of travel (e.g. work, medical appointments).
People with reduced mobility have passenger rights when travelling across the EU by air, rail, bus/coach and boat. For more on EU passenger rights for people with disabilities or reduced mobility, see the following web page of the European Commission: https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/travel/transport-disability/reduced-mobility/index_de.htm
