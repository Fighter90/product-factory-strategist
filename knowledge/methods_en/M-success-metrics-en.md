# Success Metrics: NSM, Metric Tree, OKR (EN summary)

**When:** "how will we know the strategy works?"; "which single metric do all teams serve?"; "how do quarterly goals connect to long-term results?" Output: an NSM validated by criteria, a metric tree with owners and proven links, period objectives and key results, and a metric list with formulas, units and horizons.

**Core — four levels of strategy metrics / the pyramid** (one idea: from long-term result down to what the team directly controls): **NSM/Impact** (long-term value and growth; years; EBITDA, ROI, LTV/CAC), **Outcome** (result of the goal-setting period; quarter–year; revenue, deals, retention), **Output** (intermediate result, proxy; sprint–quarter; conversion, buyout share, response speed), **Input** (what the team directly influences; day–sprint; adoption, pushes sent, AOV). Teams manage bottom-up: input and output → outcome → NSM. Relative metrics (percentages, conversions) are dangerous as goals: conversion falls when scaling onto cold traffic while deals grow; you can "touch" money, deals, GMV — conversion and average check are attributes. NPS as a product metric is unreliable: it can fall while the product improves (tariff rises). **NSM** — the indicator best predicting long-term success: it leads to revenue growth, reflects customer value, measures real progress; it aligns teams around one goal, gives result transparency, supports customer focus. **Revenue is a bad NSM**: it pushes short-term squeezing, user burnout and churn. **Eight NSM criteria**: 1) coincides with the customer's "success" moment (a booked trip, not registrations); 2) expresses value, not just sales ("orders without complaints", "delivered in 15 minutes" beats "orders"); 3) quantitatively measurable (not "satisfaction" — a number of operations, frequency, time saved); 4) time-bound (day/week/month; avoid annual intervals); 5) under control (minimal external factors); 6) NSM growth = business growth ("reports downloaded" is bad — ten retries due to errors; "successfully downloaded reports per week" is good); 7) linked to the AARRR funnel (every team influences it); 8) changes often enough (the action repeats ≈3–4×/month per user). Good examples: MAU, listening time, purchases per month, booked nights, messages sent, trips per user per week, weekly active teams; for a delivery marketplace: successful deals with delivery.

**Metric tree** — a logical hierarchy: input metrics influence Layer 1 focus metrics, those influence the NSM. Rules: Layer 1 = 3–5 metrics directly influencing the NSM, each with an **owner** (a team or function; an unowned metric degrades); typical 3–5 teams per business, each "owning" its Layer 1. **Two-sided marketplace: a deal requires all conditions simultaneously** — successful delivery/booking happens only if (1) the buyer chose the service, (2) the seller onboarded it, (3) the card/listing has the activated service; Layer 1 must cover both market sides plus the product condition — measuring only demand is not enough (demand without supply yields no deals). Links between levels are **proven** (A/B test, correlation, retrospective), not assumed: +10% sellers may yield +1–2% deals — the link exists, the coefficient is low; links are coefficients, not simple sums/products. Acquisition growth ≠ deal growth (irrelevant traffic possible): quality metrics must grow together. The tree changes with stage: launch — acquisition and activation; growth — scale; maturity — retention and income; optimization — efficiency; 2–3 depths suffice. Purpose: visualization, prioritization, monitoring — the tree is a map of levers. **AARRR** as the tree's blocks: Acquisition (signups), Activation (aha-moment, free→paid conversion), Retention (retention, DAU/MAU), Referral (NPS, shares), Revenue (LTV, AOV, CAC).

**OKR**: Objective (where we want to arrive — an outcome) + Key Results (how we'll know we're moving) + scoring. Objective = verb + what + "so that" + which value is created; it sets direction, answers "why", follows from the team's mission, motivates, is understandable and memorable, tied to company strategy. KR = verb + value + "from X to Z"; achievable, ambitious, measurable, **sensitive within the period** (usually a quarter). KRs are formulated on **leading** metrics (action, process), not **lagging** ones (result): "reduce daily calories to X", not "lose weight". **A KR sits one Layer below the Objective** (Objective on Layer 1 → KRs on Layer 2). One company-wide format so anyone can read a neighbor team's goals. Example: Objective — "grow the number of buyers preferring delivery, to raise successful deals"; KR1 — delivery categories from 3 to 6; KR2 — share of traffic on delivery-enabled listings in ranking from 10% to 15%. The **proof of the KR → Objective link** is the hardest part of OKR; four mechanisms by decreasing strength: A/B test (gold standard), correlation analysis on history, counterfactual before/after with a control group, logical chain (allowed only for obvious links — "more categories → more choice → more buyers" — and still confirmed with data). Without a proven link, KRs can be met while the Objective isn't. **Technical teams** set infrastructure KRs ("make parallel A/B tests possible", "start using seller rating in recommendations") — their link to business metrics runs through enabling product teams and must be named explicitly. Metrics are born: goal → strategy and bets (how we'll reach it) → data and metrics; every bet gets its own **driver metric** with an obvious link to the target metric; if the chosen metric doesn't explain movement toward the goal, the bet is revised, not the data; bet prioritization — RICE. Leading/lagging division: leading (input/output) are managed and show progress fast; lagging (outcome/NSM) confirm the result. Expectation fixed before launch: which metric changes and by how much; after the experiment, fact vs expectation is decomposed by drivers; decision — **Scale / Iterate / Drop**, with counter-metrics checked before scaling and the conclusion recorded in the knowledge base.

**How:** 1) define the product stage and 2–3 NSM candidates; test each against the eight criteria; reject revenue; 2) build the tree top-down: NSM → 3–5 Layer 1 metrics (by AARRR blocks or another logic) → Layer 2 → Layer 3 if needed; assign an owner to each Layer 1; 3) mark each link's evidence status: proven by A/B / correlation / retrospective / hypothesis (hypotheses go into the validation plan); 4) choose team goals the team can "paint" (measure with significance) within the period: if the outcome is too coarse (company revenue), pick an output/input with a proven link; 5) formulate the quarterly OKR: the team's NSM and tree first, then drivers, then the Objective; KRs — leading, one layer below, "from X to Z"; 6) fill a passport per metric: definition, formula, unit, period, data source, owner, baseline, target, sensitivity horizon; 7) set a quarterly review (off-schedule on strategy change): are links confirmed, is any metric unowned; 8) pass metrics to the roadmap (output for the pilot, outcome for validation and scaling).

**Pitfalls:** NSM = revenue; an NSM not tied to the value moment; an NSM with an annual cycle; too many metrics; broken links; level confusion (retention as input); proxies as ends in themselves (average check, conversion, time-to-market); metrics without owners; links "by feel"; two hits taken for a correlation; absolute and relative goals mixed without explanation (a relative metric degrades at scale and the team is "punished"); KRs without "from-to", on lagging metrics, unmanageable (MAU instead of campaigns launched), over-ambitious ("double revenue in a quarter"), untied to the tree ("improve the design"), insensitive to the period (LTV); different OKR formats per team — nobody can read anyone else's goals.
