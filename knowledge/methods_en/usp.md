# USP and Value Proposition (Job Story + VPC) (EN summary)

**When:** "how do we differ so that they choose us", "which promise goes into communication and the MVP", "which feature is a competitive advantage and which is hygiene" — after customer and competitor knowledge, before communication and development.

**Core:** USP (unique selling proposition) — a product characteristic or feature that differentiates it and is hard or impossible to copy; if a competitor repeats it in a week, it's not a USP. What copies easily vs with difficulty (grocery delivery example): easily — a beautiful site, a convenient app (expensive but repeatable with resources), tone of voice, promos and discounts, gamification, a set of famous brands; hard/expensive/long — 15-minute delivery (logistics), pharmacy delivery, a special "one-click" delivery model, private label. Criterion: "hard, expensive, time-consuming" for competitors. Starting questions: for whom is the product, for what (which client job it solves) — look at needs, the jobs people want done, not at people and demographics. Job Story: "When [context/situation], I want/can/do [action], so that [motivation/outcome]" — hypotheses formulated from the consumer, not from a product feature (Jobs-to-be-Done). Value Proposition Canvas (Osterwalder), two halves: Customer Profile — Jobs-to-be-done (what the client wants to accomplish, why they use the product, personal and emotional needs), Gains (what would delight; results exceeding expectations; social, emotional, financial benefits), Pains (what annoys and worries; what blocks the job; what would make them quit); Value Map — Products & Services (which services and features close the job), Gain Creators (how to help exceed expectations, simplify life), Pain Relievers (how to ease the pain, remove anxiety and barriers — and which barriers can't be removed, only made less irritating). The USP lives at the junction of the unique, hard-to-copy part of the Value Map and the client's critical pain/benefit; honestly ask whether we remove the pain or merely reduce it, to avoid promising the unrealistic. Sources: desk analysis and benchmark (white space, market standard), trends, in-depth interviews (pains, barriers, language), segmentation, brand tracking (image gap vs competitor). Desk data gives a candidate; promise validation on the target audience is mandatory. Evidence strength: a USP candidate derived from benchmark and interviews is a hypothesis about value and difference; a headline test (clicks on an A/B listing) measures interest in the promise — not demand, not willingness to pay, not retention; value is confirmed by behavior and money (pre-order, payment, retention) and by repeat choice by the segment.

**How:** (1) define segment and scenario: take the key segment and a concrete situation (e.g. buying pet supplies); write from the buyer's side, not the seller's; (2) collect 3–7 Job Stories from interviews, analytics, support: context — action — motivation; (3) fill the VPC left side: per job, Pains and Gains with importance (frequency × severity); mark each item "fact" (interviews/data) or "hypothesis" — invent nothing; (4) fill the right side: products/services, pain relievers, gain creators — each mapped to the specific pain/gain it closes; (5) check against competitors: who already offers this pain reliever (competitor matrix); what is the market standard (hygiene), what is white space; (6) filter candidates by four questions: Valuable? (the client would change a habit for it); Rare? (competitors don't promise the same); Hard to copy? (not repeatable in a week); Clear? (understood at first reading) — any "no" means rework the advantage or the wording; (7) prioritize on Value × Effort: value = how much pain it removes × number of people with the pain; effort = complexity, time, money, partners; quadrants — high value/low effort: quick wins (into the MVP); high/high: the main USP bet (needs investment and a plan B of quick wins); low/low: residual effort; low value/high effort: postpone; one USP candidate in focus; (8) write the USP statement (~15 words): "For [concrete segment] we are the only ones who [solve a concrete pain] [in a unique way]" — one segment, one pain, a uniqueness marker, no vague words ("care", "convenient"); (9) validate the promise: desk and benchmark are done; next — concept interviews, headline test (A/B listing: same creative and targeting, only the headline changes; metric CTR or click-through; n, effect threshold, significance level set in advance), then MVP/pilot with behavioral metrics; (10) embed into communication and metrics: landing, sales, feature-scope criterion; tie to the product metric and OKR. Example (pet supplies on a classifieds platform): job — "when the food ran out, quickly buy a suitable one"; pains — catalog chaos, forgetting to buy/vaccinate; gain — "open and immediately see products for my pet"; candidates — pet profile with auto-catalog (hard to copy: attribute schema + ML matching), reminders (technically simple, competitors have it), SOS button with vet-clinic search (hard: partner network and SLA) — all hypotheses until validated on users. Before→after wording: "lots of food and toys, plus delivery" → "We'll pick and remind you to buy food for your exact breed"; bad: "convenient app", "we care about pets" (unmeasurable, easily copied). Checklist: one segment; one pain; ≤~15 words; a uniqueness marker; the four questions passed; Value/Effort scored; one bet and quick wins chosen; validation method and success metric defined; risks fixed (partner dependency, regulation).

**Pitfalls:** presenting hygiene as USP ("convenient interface", "quality service", discounts); formulating from the product ("we have smart matching") instead of the client's job and pain; one USP "for everyone" (blurred segment) or two pains in one phrase; writing a USP without competitor analysis ("uniqueness" may be the market standard); taking headline interest (a click) as demand confirmation; running a listing test without pre-set thresholds and control; changing creative/audience along with the headline in the A/B listing; not backing the promise with real delivery capability (logistics, partners) — communication outruns the product; treating the USP as eternal — the market and competitors move; return to validation.
