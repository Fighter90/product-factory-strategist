# Competitors — types, competitive landscape, five forces, Strategy Canvas, JTBD map

## When to apply
A target segment has been chosen (or is under consideration) and you must decide **where you compete and where you deliberately don't**. Competitor analysis is needed not to "know the market" as a checkbox in a document, but to make a strategic choice and to find the entry points, reinforcement points, and exit points from competition. The output: a map of the customer's alternatives, an assessment of how hard winning is, a list of choice factors, and a conclusion for strategy.

## Core idea
We compete not with companies but **for a specific customer choice in a specific situation**: for time, for money, for attention, for trust/risk. If a customer has a need, they are already solving it somehow — that solution is the competitor, and it follows directly from the segment choice.

**Competitor types**
| Type | Essence | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Direct | the same job, the same way and model | classifieds for listings and real estate |
| Indirect | the same top-level job, a different way/model; at the JTBD level — different contexts | Lavka ("ran out of eggs, need them fast") vs supermarket delivery ("planning dinner with friends") |
| Substitutes | a radically different solution, up to "no solution at all" | Netflix vs video tapes; "stopped taking taxis, started walking" |
| Cannibalization | products of one group take share from each other | Lavka and Eda within the same group |

In real practice it matters more to know which alternatives the customer actually picks than to classify flawlessly; looking at alternatives helps to avoid locking into a "head-on" solution.

**Group comparison table** (as in the strategy document example): competitor groups are the columns, and the two mandatory rows are "key advantages" and "risks for the company". Example structure for a classifieds marketplace:
| | Leading online marketplaces | Category stores | Social networks and messengers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Key advantages | scale of orders and buyers; logistics (pickup points, couriers, express); lower prices in key categories; B2B sales; bets on scaling, not optimization | offline inspection and consultation; deep interface adaptation to the category; brand partnerships; guarantees and transparent terms | huge monthly audience; recommendation-driven purchases (trust); deep targeting; user-flow tools |
| Risks for the company (with level) | expansion of logistics into the regions — high; "everything in one app" — high; growth of second-hand supply in popular categories — low | guarantees and smooth checkout may pull buyers out of narrow categories — high | sellers move communication onto messenger platforms and redistribute acquisition budgets — low |

Every "risks" cell gets a level (high/low) and a rationale; below the table the document continues with a **"growth and promo mechanics by stream"** block — what competitors have already launched to grow GMV, orders, and average order value:
| Stream | What leaders have launched (generalized) |
|---|---|
| Fintech & loyalty | own wallets, credit, installments, cashback, cards — they make the final price cheaper |
| Assortment | catalog expansion, parallel imports, replacing departed brands with affordable goods |
| Delivery | pickup-point network, shorter delivery times in the regions, express and bulky goods |
| Promo mechanics | two types of promotions — mandatory (for all sellers) and seasonal (tied to calendar dates); "fair" promos: freezing prices before a promo starts or showing price dynamics on the product card |

The promo block is complemented by buyer choice tools (price dynamics, discount request, comparisons, expert recommendations) — these are worth writing out separately: they are the choice factors by which the customer compares you with the leader. Mechanics analysis is not for copying but to understand which levers the leaders have already "occupied" and where differentiation is still possible.

**Data sources on competitors** — four types, honestly labeled: assumptions (embedded assumptions), benchmarks (market figures), own research, commissioned research under NDA. A level or share without a source is not a level — it is an opinion.

**Case lessons**
- **Zoom** entered late but found an enterprise pain point — the heavy first session (download, register, complete onboarding) — and made joining by link possible. A narrow but powerful differentiator that produced viral growth.
- **Tinkoff** found the banks' weakness (dependence on branches), consciously gave up offices, and focused on an online model, card delivery, and remote support.
- **Domclick** offered a ~0.3% mortgage discount when the property was found through it: people found the apartment on Domclick and closed the deal through the platforms they were used to. The discount did not create a habit; the service had no proprietary content. Lesson: in a mature market **habit and content beat a one-off discount**.
- **New builds on a listings classifieds**: the hypothesis "developers need more leads, cheaper" turned out to be wrong; the real job is selling exactly as many units as planned for the stage (sales come in waves by lot and price). The solution — a consultation service with its own managed call center and lead sales per plan. In a few years parity with the leader was reached. Lesson: understand the client's business process, not what they ask for.

**Five frameworks for five different questions** (a framework is a structure for thinking, not a template to fill in "head-on")
| Framework | Question | How to do it |
|---|---|---|
| Competitive Landscape | which models of solving the job exist and where our place is | pick two axes — the customer's choice factors; plot all solutions, not only direct competitors. Real estate example: the axis "independent control ↔ delegation" and the axis "broad market overview ↔ interpretation and risk reduction"; classifieds — independence and breadth, realtors/agencies — delegation and risk reduction, developer websites, Telegram channels and chats, AI matchmakers (still distrust) — separate points |
| Porter's five forces | how hard it is to win in the chosen model; what an incumbent should fear | assess in-industry rivalry, the threat of new entrants, buyer power, supplier power, the threat of substitutes; each force — level + arguments |
| Strategy Canvas | what to win with, where to strengthen and where to drop out | list the choice factors, rate yourself and the competitors; "green" factors — maintain and use in communication; "red" — fix; for a breakthrough ("blue ocean"), turn a strongly red factor into a strongly green one |
| JTBD and alternatives analysis | which customer jobs at each stage are important, hard, and poorly covered | map: journey stages × jobs; for each one — importance, difficulty, coverage (green / yellow / red / outside our territory), "why it's hard", the customer's current solutions, ideas |
| Feature Comparison | where we lag functionally | feature matrix; use it as a **source of ideas**, not as the basis of strategy: the leader may have 30% fewer filters and still win on its own territory, because users don't use every filter |

Example five-forces assessment for a real-estate classifieds: rivalry — medium-high (no clear differentiation, users search across several platforms); new entrants — low (formally entry is simple, but content and trust are expensive; even a strong player with its own brand took ~10% of the market); buyer power — medium-low; supplier power — medium (without listings there is no market, but each individual player is weak; a separate focus on large clients); substitutes — medium (AI matchmakers, vertical search, Telegram channels).

## Procedure
1. **Fix the segment and its job** (`M-customers`): without them a competitor list is meaningless.
2. **Collect the customer's alternatives**: ask ("how do you solve this now?"), look at behavior and payments, add substitutes and the "do nothing" option. A quick completeness test: to a list of 5–7 direct competitors add indirect ones, substitutes, do-it-yourself solutions, "Excel and a messenger".
3. **Draw the landscape** on two axes chosen from the customer's choice factors; mark the unoccupied zones and the zones not worth entering (changing the essence of your business model for someone else's territory).
4. **Assess the difficulty of winning** (five forces) for the chosen model.
5. **Measure the choice factors** (Strategy Canvas): build the list (for a large player it is on the order of a hundred factors), rate yourself and the competitors from survey data; find the green and red factors; for the red ones choose "fix" or "consciously don't fight".
6. **Analyze the customer's jobs** (JTBD map) and pick the important, hard, uncovered ones (red); look for hypotheses there.
7. **Compare features** and use the result only as an idea bank.
8. **For each competitor** record: group, key advantages, growth strategy and promo mechanics, risk to us (high/medium/low level + rationale), our response or refusal.
9. **Formulate the conclusion**: on which territory we fight, where we don't, which advantage we build; pass it into goals and bets (`M-goals-drivers-bets`) and risks (`M-risks`).

## Template / checklist
**Competitor group card** (modeled on the course example: online marketplaces / category stores / social networks and messengers)
| Field | What to record |
|---|---|
| Group and members | names |
| Key advantages | scale, logistics, prices, trust, offline experience, etc. (with figures and a source) |
| Growth strategy and promo mechanics | where they invest (logistics, advertising, subsidies, loyalty, assortment, financial services) |
| Risk to us | description + level + rationale |
| Our choice | fight / don't fight / partnership + why |
| Data type | fact (report, study) / benchmark / assumption |

**Strategy Canvas matrix**
| Factor | Our score | Competitor 1 | Competitor 2 | Green / red | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

**Feature Comparison** — by feature categories, as shares of the leader or "yes/no/partial"; each row is a category (filters, product card, listing verification, lead management), the columns are competitors. Remember: the result is a source of ideas, not a strategy.

**JTBD map**
| Stage | Job | Importance | Difficulty | Coverage | Why hard | How it's solved now | Idea |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

**Checklist**
- [ ] Competitors are defined relative to the segment and its job, not "by industry"
- [ ] Direct, indirect, substitutes and "do nothing" are present
- [ ] Every figure has a source (report, commissioned study, benchmark, assumption — labeled)
- [ ] At least one factor has been chosen for reinforcement and at least one for deliberate abandonment
- [ ] The switching habit/barrier has been accounted for
- [ ] The conclusion has been turned into a decision, not a description

## Typical mistakes
- **Analysis for the document's sake**: no conclusion on "where we fight and where we don't".
- **A list of only direct competitors** — substitutes and DIY solutions are missed.
- **Framework filled in head-on**: filling the template instead of answering the question.
- **Task substitution**: the client asks for "cheaper/more", but the real job is controllability, timing, risk.
- **Copying a competitor's feature** (more features ≠ better).
- **A discount instead of differentiation**: without content and habit, users "take the discount and leave".
- **Underestimating habit**: perceived "convenience" is subjective and depends on habit (different cities rate different services as more convenient).
- **Partner channels ignored**, as is the option of buying the missing competence (in the new-build case the call-center competence was bought).

## Questions that can change the decision
- Which alternatives does the target segment actually pick, and in which situations?
- Which choice factor really decides (not "convenience" that is identical for everyone)?
- Can a red factor be turned into a strongly green one within the strategy horizon, and at what cost?
- What will the strongest player do if we choose head-on competition — are the resources enough?
- Is there a territory it pays to abandon in order to concentrate?
- Which substitute might become stronger than the direct competitors in 2–3 years (AI services, adjacent platforms)?
