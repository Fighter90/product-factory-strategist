# Competitors — types, competitive landscape, five forces, Strategy Canvas, JTBD map

## When to use
When the target segment is chosen (or being considered) and you must decide **where we compete and where we deliberately do not**. Competitor analysis is needed not to "know the market" for the sake of a checkbox in a document, but to make a strategic choice and find entry points, points of strengthening, and points of refusing to compete. The result — a map of the client's alternatives, an assessment of how hard winning will be, a list of choice factors, and a conclusion for the strategy.

## Core idea
We compete not with companies but **for a specific client choice in a specific situation**: for time, for money, for attention, for trust/risk. If a client has a need, they are already solving it somehow — that solution is the competitor, and it follows directly from the segment choice.

**Types of competitors**
| Type | Essence | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Direct | the same job, the same way and model | classifieds for general goods and real estate |
| Indirect | the same job at the top level, a different way/model; at the JTBD level — different contexts | a corner shop ("ran out of eggs, need it fast") and supermarket delivery ("planning dinner with friends") |
| Substitutes | a radically different solution, up to "no solution at all" | Netflix vs videotapes; "stopped taking taxis, started walking" |
| Cannibalization | products of one group take share from each other | the corner shop and food delivery inside one group |
In real practice it matters more to know which alternatives the client chooses than to classify flawlessly; looking at alternatives is useful so you don't get stuck on a "head-on" solution.

**Comparative table of competitor groups** (as in the strategy document example): competitor groups — columns, two mandatory rows — "key advantages" and "risks for the company." Example structure for a classifieds platform:
| | Leading online marketplaces | Category-specific stores | Social networks and messengers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Key advantages | scale of orders and buyers; logistics (pickup points, courier, express); lower prices on key categories; B2B sales; betting on scaling rather than optimization | offline inspection and consultation; deep interface adaptation to the category; partnerships with brands; guarantees and transparent terms | huge monthly audience; purchases by recommendation (trust); deep targeting; user-redirect tools |
| Risks for the company (with a level) | expanding logistics into regions — high; "everything in one app" — high; growth of used supply in popular categories — low | guarantees and easy checkout may pull buyers out of narrow categories — high | sellers move communication onto other platforms and redistribute acquisition budgets — low |

Every "risks" cell gets a level (high/low) and a justification; below the table in the document comes the block **"growth mechanics and promo mechanics by streams"** — what competitors have already launched to grow GMV, orders, and average order value:
| Stream | What the leaders have launched (generalized) |
|---|---|
| Fintech & loyalty | own wallets, credit, installments, cashback, cards — cheapen the final price |
| Assortment | catalog expansion, parallel imports, replacing departed brands with affordable goods |
| Delivery | a pickup-point network, shorter times in the regions, express and bulky goods |
| Promo mechanics | two types of promotions — mandatory (for all sellers) and seasonal (tied to calendar dates); "fair" promotions: capping price growth before the start or showing the price history on the product card |

The promo block is complemented by buyer choice tools (price dynamics, a discount request, comparisons, expert recommendations) — these are worth listing separately: they are the choice factors by which the client compares you with the leader. Analyzing mechanics is not for copying but to understand which levers are already "taken" by the leaders and where differentiation is possible.

**Sources of competitor data** — four types, honestly labeled: assumptions (the assumptions we build in), benchmarks (market figures), own research, commissioned research under NDA. Levels and shares without a source are not a level but an opinion.

**Lessons from cases**
- **Zoom** launched late but found the corporate pain — a heavy first session (download, register, onboard) — and made entry via a link. A point but powerful differentiator that gave viral growth.
- **Tinkoff** found the banks' weakness (dependence on branches), deliberately gave up offices and focused on the online model, card delivery, and remote support.
- **Domclick** offered a ~0.3% mortgage discount when the home was found through them: people found the property on Domclick but closed the deal through familiar channels. The discount created no habit; the service had no proprietary content. Lesson: in a mature market **habit and content beat a one-off discount**.
- **New-builds on a classifieds platform**: the hypothesis "developers need more leads, cheaper" proved wrong; the real job is selling exactly as much as planned for the stage (sales go in waves by lots and prices). The solution — a consultation service with its own managed call center and selling leads according to plan. Within a few years they reached parity with the leader. Lesson: understand the client's business process, not what they ask for.

**Five frameworks for five different questions** (a framework is a structure for thinking, not a form to fill in "head-on")
| Framework | Question | How to do it |
|---|---|---|
| Competitive Landscape | which models of solving the job exist and where our place is | choose two axes — the client's choice factors; plot all solutions, not only direct competitors. Real-estate example: the axis "self-directed control ↔ delegation" and the axis "broad market overview ↔ interpretation and risk reduction"; classifieds — self-service and breadth, agents/agencies — delegation and risk reduction, developer sites, Telegram channels and chats, AI matchmakers (still distrust) — separate points |
| Porter's five forces | how hard it is to win in the chosen model; what an incumbent should fear | assess rivalry within the industry, the threat of new entrants, buyer power, supplier power, the threat of substitutes; each force — a level + arguments |
| Strategy Canvas | what to win on, where to strengthen and where to quit | list the choice factors, rate yourself and competitors; "green" factors — keep and use in communication; "red" — fix; for a breakthrough ("blue ocean") turn a strongly red factor into a strongly green one |
| JTBD and alternatives analysis | which client jobs at each stage are important, hard, poorly served | a map: journey stages × jobs; for each — importance, difficulty, coverage (green / yellow / red / outside our territory), "why hard," the client's current solutions, ideas |
| Feature Comparison | where the functional gap is | a feature matrix; use it as a **source of ideas**, not as the basis of strategy: the leader may have 30% fewer filters and still win on its own territory, because users do not use all the filters |
An example of a five-forces assessment for a real-estate classifieds: rivalry — medium-high (no vivid differentiation, people search on several platforms); new entrants — low (formally entry is simple, but content and trust are expensive; even a strong player with its own brand took ~10% of the market); buyer power — medium-low; supplier power — medium (without listings there is no market, but each individual player is weak, a separate focus on large clients); substitutes — medium (AI matchmakers, vertical search, Telegram channels).

## Procedure
1. **Fix the segment and its job** (`M-customers`): without them a competitor list is meaningless.
2. **Collect the client's alternatives**: ask ("how do you solve this now?"), look at behavior and payments, add substitutes and the "do nothing" option. A quick completeness test: to a list of 5–7 direct competitors add indirect ones, substitutes, a self-made solution, "Excel and a messenger."
3. **Draw the landscape** on two axes chosen from the client's choice factors; mark unoccupied zones and zones not worth entering (changing the essence of your business model for someone else's territory).
4. **Assess the difficulty of winning** (five forces) for the chosen model.
5. **Measure the choice factors** (Strategy Canvas): define the list (a large player has on the order of a hundred factors), rate yourself and competitors from survey data; find the green and red factors; for red ones — choose "fix" or "deliberately not fight."
6. **Analyze the client's jobs** (JTBD map) and pick the important and hard ones that are unserved (red); look for hypotheses there.
7. **Compare features** and use the result only as an idea bank.
8. **For each competitor** write down: group, key advantages, growth strategy and promo mechanics, risk to us (level high/medium/low + justification), our response or refusal.
9. **Formulate the conclusion**: on which territory we compete, where we don't, which advantage we build; hand it over to goals and bets (`M-goals-drivers-bets`) and risks (`M-risks`).

## Template / checklist
**Competitor group card** (modeled on the course example: online marketplaces / category stores / social networks and messengers)
| Field | What to record |
|---|---|
| Group and members | names |
| Key advantages | scale, logistics, prices, trust, offline experience, etc. (with numbers and a source) |
| Growth strategy and promo mechanics | where they invest (logistics, advertising, subsidies, loyalty, assortment, financial services) |
| Risk to us | description + level + justification |
| Our choice | fight / not fight / partnership + why |
| Data type | fact (report, study) / benchmark / assumption |

**Strategy Canvas matrix**
| Factor | Our score | Competitor 1 | Competitor 2 | Green / red | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

**Feature Comparison** — by feature categories, in shares of the leader or "yes/no/partial"; each row — a category (filters, product card, property verification, lead management), columns — competitors. Remember: the result is a source of ideas, not a strategy.

**JTBD map**
| Stage | Job | Importance | Difficulty | Coverage | Why hard | How it is solved now | Idea |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

**Checklist**
- [ ] Competitors defined relative to the segment and job, not "by industry"
- [ ] Direct, indirect, substitutes, and "do nothing" are present
- [ ] Every figure has a source (report, commissioned study, benchmark, assumption — labeled)
- [ ] At least one factor chosen for strengthening and at least one for deliberate refusal
- [ ] Habit/switching barrier accounted for
- [ ] The conclusion turned into a decision, not a description

## Common mistakes
- **Analysis for the document's sake**: no conclusion on "where we compete and where we don't."
- **A list of only direct competitors** — substitutes and self-made solutions missed.
- **A framework filled head-on**: filling the template instead of answering the question.
- **Substituting the job**: the client asks for "cheaper/more," but the real job is controllability, timeline, risk.
- **Copying a competitor's feature** (more features ≠ better).
- **A discount instead of differentiation**: without content and habit users "take the discount and leave."
- **Underestimating habit**: the perception of "convenience" is subjective and habit-dependent (different cities rate different services as more convenient).
- **Partner channels not considered** and the option of buying the missing competence (in the new-builds case the call-center competence was bought).

## Questions that can change the decision
- Which alternatives does the target segment actually choose, and in which situations?
- Which choice factor really decides (not "convenience," which is identical for everyone)?
- Can a red factor be turned into a strongly green one within the strategy's horizon, and at what cost?
- What does the strongest player do if we choose head-on competition — are the resources enough?
- Is there a territory worth giving up to concentrate?
- Which substitute could become stronger than direct competitors within 2–3 years (AI services, adjacent platforms)?
