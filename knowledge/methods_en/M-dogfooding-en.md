# Ethnography, Diary Studies and Dogfooding (EN summary)

**When:** you need to see what people **do**, not what they say: designing a new experience, redesigns, hunting hidden problems and growth points, studying competitors through real use, or when the product can't be reproduced in a lab. Dogfooding is a fast, cheap way to align the team and catch obvious problems before user contact. None of these replaces research with real customers.

**Core:** ethnography — the researcher immerses in the users' environment and observes behavior in real life; deep context, hidden needs, team empathy, real actions captured — but resource-heavy, small samples, observer effect, interpretation risk, and access problems. Observation axes: field (natural setting) / lab (controlled); participant / non-participant; overt / covert (covert requires ethical and legal clearance). Forms: passive; moderated (rare non-leading questions — "what did you just think?"); provoked ("imagine the courier is 20 minutes late"); contextual interview (the person shows and explains as they go — "memory lies, behavior doesn't"); shadowing (a full shift/day); diaries. Diary study: participants self-record experience over time (text, screenshots, voice, micro-surveys); fits habits, usage triggers, learning and adaptation, attitude change; in-the-moment capture (more detail, distorts the situation) vs end-of-day snippets (less distortion, gaps possible). Dogfooding ("eat your own dog food"): the team uses its own product as an end user — when a prototype exists but users haven't seen it, after UX tests before release, and for alignment; kinds: field (a real living need) vs lab (scripted, artificial need); team vs individual. Limits: employees ≠ users (they know the product, different motivation, habituated to bugs); can't fake expert domains ("pretending to be a doctor" fails); useless without structure. Analysis (shared by all three methods): collect everything in one place → first pass (what went wrong, confusion, stops, emotions, repetitions, surprises, deviations from the plan) → coding (one observation = one headline/theme; record "as on camera", no interpretation) → insight = observation + explanation + why it matters (e.g., order completed but the user sat in silence — no confirmation → lower confidence, repeated actions) → hypothesis (what to change if the cause is known; how to find out if not) → prioritization (frequency, user criticality, business criticality; Kano, ICE/RICE, cost, voting — for dogfooding, separate professional noise from real need) → patterns: a single observation is an anecdote, a repeat across ≥2–3 people is a candidate regularity (behavioral, emotional, contextual, social, instrumental) → update the as-is CJM, record insights in the UX backlog, verify with usability tests on real users. Recommended double loop: ethnography (outer, real context) → dogfooding (inner, fast, regular) → UX test (verification) → quantitative check (A/B, analytics); each method closes the other's blind spots. Health benchmarks (from course notes, calibrate): 80–100% team participation; 2–6 problems per person per session on active functionality; ≥70% of findings reach the backlog; regular cadence (weekly / before releases).

**How:** A. Field observation — goal as question + observable behavior ("how do sellers photograph and publish items", not "see what they do"); choose format; rules: don't prompt, minimal interference, don't hunt confirmation, watch nonverbals; 5–7 people per segment recruited from real practitioners; ethics: consent for recording and data processing, confidentiality, researcher safety; 45–90 min script (warm-up → environment tour → real actions → rare contextual questions → debriefing); recording table by CJM stage: stage | user task | stops (count) | think-aloud verbatim | errors | insight, plus photos, environment and device notes. B. Team dogfooding — goal and critical scenarios; mixed roles ≤7–8 people; moderator/executor/observer; run scenarios live without discussing; debrief per problem: bug or UX; template problem → insight → idea → priority; "ground" it: file tasks, fix agreements, list hypotheses needing real users. C. Individual — goal and scenarios → instructions → feedback form → analysis (groups, confusion, frequency, criticality, CJM position) → close the loop (thank, share results). D. Diary — goal and format → tool → recruit 7–15 (some drop) with a short briefing → reminders and follow-ups (staggered payouts for long studies) → closing interview and reward. E. Unified dogfooding record: scenario step | what the participant did | expectation | fact | emotion | severity | preliminary recommendation | recording link.

**Pitfalls:** interference, advice, leading questions (behavior changes) → neutral, minimal presence; interpreting before recording raw facts (confirming your own hypothesis); one day/one context conclusions → plan several contexts and repeats; product-only focus (causes outside the interface lost) — capture environment, interruptions, tools, people around; averaging "all users" → work with segments and stories; dogfooding in ideal conditions (fast internet, two monitors) → include field and "extreme" conditions; one-off dogfooding without backlog tasks → regularity, owner, mandatory task filing; treating the team as the target audience → a filter, not a substitute for respondents; covert observation without legal review → informed consent or legal sign-off.
