# Marketing Research: Brand Tracking, Kano, MaxDiff, TURF (EN summary)

**When:** zooming out to the market and potential buyers: how the brand is known and used relative to competitors, how perception changes, which product attributes matter more, and which set covers the maximum audience; also choosing between recurring tracking and a one-off ad hoc project, and reading their results correctly.

**Core:** marketing research = collecting, analyzing and interpreting information about the market, consumers and competitors (>100-year-old industry). The marketing/product split is conditional: marketing = zoom out (market, potential buyers, preferences, strategy); product = zoom in (deep user needs for building the product); tools are shared. Two formats: tracking (recurring, month–half-year: Brand Health Tracking, CSAT/CES measures, channel and ad tracking) and ad hoc (one-off answers to a specific question: U&A — category penetration, habits and attitudes; segmentation; Kano; MaxDiff; TURF; concept tests). A good product runs an always-on tracker; ad hoc joins when something is unclear or a choice is needed. Evidence strength: mass-survey data describe **declared** attitudes of the sampled people at measurement time — they prove differences and dynamics with correct sampling and method, but not causes, not behavior, not future demand; "importance" ratings tend toward "everything is important" — use forced-choice methods (MaxDiff) and behavioral anchors (analytics, experiments). BHT tracks: brand funnel — top-of-mind (first named), spontaneous (unprompted), prompted awareness → usage (consideration, trial, regular, most-used); satisfaction (CSAT, CES, NPS = %promoters(9–10) − %detractors(0–6); CSAT/CES often computed as top-2-of-5 share); brand image attributes vs competitors; market context. Questionnaire order is fixed, broad → narrow: screening → top-of-mind (open) → spontaneous awareness → prompted awareness → usage → image attributes (agreement scale) → CSAT/NPS; prompts go **after** spontaneous questions or the funnel distorts; identical wording in every wave. Sample: ≈385 respondents per wave per analyzed segment at ±5pp/95%; ≈450–500 with design effect and a 10–15% buffer; QC traps, timers, duplicate checks. Reading results: dynamics vs the previous wave with confidence intervals (a change inside the margin of error is not a signal); funnel leaks vs competitors (high awareness / low usage → price or trust barrier; low awareness / high usage → a reach problem); image gaps — an attribute where a competitor scores significantly higher is a USP candidate or a weakness; "is CSAT 60% good?" is meaningless without dynamics, competitors and segments. Attribute prioritization — three methods for the "everything is important" problem: **Kano** (paired questions per attribute: "how would you feel if X existed / didn't exist"; answer matrix classifies each attribute: must-be/hygiene — expected when present, bad when absent; performance — better present, worse absent; attractive/delighters — nice when present, indifference when absent; indifferent — skip; reverse — some people don't want it at all; order: fix must-be defects, grow performance, add delighters, skip indifferent; n ≈ 300+; the method doesn't explain why and is sensitive to wording); **MaxDiff** (screens of 4–5 attributes, pick most and least important; algorithm rotates combinations; output = ranking with utility/preference shares; forces trade-offs and reduces order effects; ≤≈25 non-overlapping attribute statements, 6–10 screens, n ≥ ≈200, ≥≈300 when splitting into segments); **TURF** (total unduplicated reach and frequency: tests attribute combinations to find the minimal set covering the maximum share of the audience — the best pair is not necessarily the top-2 rated, because they may attract the same people; e.g. a loyalty program helps more users, but a convenient app adds unique reach). MVP-set combination: Kano answers "which type of influence", MaxDiff "how important relative to others", TURF "which set covers the most"; must-be attributes are the base condition and don't compete in rankings — choose the set among performance + delighters by importance and reach, then filter by cost and time. Hybrid questionnaire: ≤≈7 minutes — Kano pairs and MaxDiff on the same 6–8 attributes with identical wording, Kano first, then MaxDiff, then a trap check.

**How:** (1) define the decision and question type — dynamics and competitive standing → tracking; feature choice → Kano/MaxDiff/TURF; category entry → U&A; grouping → segmentation; (2) prepare the attribute list from a qualitative stage (interviews) and remove overlapping statements; (3) choose the method, compute the sample, set quotas (gender, age, region, usage); (4) design the questionnaire: fixed order, identical wording across waves and methods, attention checks, ≤7–10 minutes; (5) pilot, field, clean data by pre-set rules; (6) analyze: funnel and dynamics with intervals, competitor gaps, Kano categories, MaxDiff ranking, TURF reach, segment differences with significance tests; (7) summarize: what changed, where the gaps are, what it means for positioning; action list with owners; hypotheses for the next wave or a qualitative stage; (8) fix baselines and set alert thresholds for key indicators.

**Pitfalls:** reading one number without dynamics and comparison ("60% — is that good?"); changing wording or order between waves; prompts before spontaneous awareness; ignoring confidence intervals (a 2–3 pp difference at n=400 is noise); importance ratings instead of forced choice; taking the MaxDiff top-3 directly without TURF (duplicated reach); Kano questions in "would you like X?" form (without the pair) and overlapping statements; interpreting Kano/MaxDiff as future demand or willingness to pay; mixing must-be and delighter attributes in one ranking; using brand tracking for interface decisions (that needs product methods — usability testing); a brand-health report without owners and actions.
