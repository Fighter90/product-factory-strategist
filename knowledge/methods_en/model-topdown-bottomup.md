# Top-Down and Bottom-Up Business Modeling (EN summary)

**When:** "what revenue and growth to expect from the product?", "what to lean on in the forecast — the market or our data?", "how to explain a divergence between two estimates?", "a quick potential estimate for an investor or a detailed budget model?" — the two approaches work as a pair: top-down frames and caps, bottom-up computes, and they cross-check each other.

**Core:** Top-down goes from the market down: a known industry money volume becomes a ceiling, filtered down to the company by successive filters. Bottom-up goes from operational metrics up: real users, conversions, checks, cohorts aggregated into revenue. Three top-down projections — not alternatives but three cameras on one object; divergence is information: Market/Money — TAM → SAM → SOM = market × share (frame and ceiling); Demand-side — revenue = clients × lead CR × paying CR × average check (who brings the money); Supply-side — page views × slots × fill rate × viewability × CTR × CPC/CPM (can we serve the demand). Concepts: TAM — whole market; SAM — reachable part (geo/segment/online); SOM — the company's real share. CAGR — annual market growth; monthly rate (1 + CAGR)^(1/12) − 1 (roughly CAGR/12). Seasonal coefficient — a month's multiplier (ad market: January ~0.6, November ~1.8); the percent base is fixed explicitly; the 12 coefficients sum to 12.0. Baseline — "what happens if we do nothing": without investment the business loses share (typical multiplier −0.1 pp/month) because competitors fight for the same market; growth − baseline = "the cost of inaction" = the value of the investment. Retention is ambiguous: by users, by transactions, by payments (a 3-month subscription = 100% payment retention with falling usage); typical curve M1 55% → M2 26% → M3 16% → plateau; a plateau ≠ zero — a PMF sign. Input vs output discipline: assumptions are set only for input metrics (new users, conversions, check); output (revenue, P&L) is computed by formulas — "you cannot forecast an outcome, you forecast changes": growth must be explained by a driver. Baseline vs traction boundary: can I get the effect without extra development/budget resources? Yes — baseline (always-on marketing, regular price raises, organic network effects); No — traction (a feature, a new channel, new geo). A feature retains and engages; marketing attracts — don't attribute acquisition to a feature; network effects are a "bonus" showing up after 2–3 months. Driver — a factor you can "turn" to get growth: product (features, engagement), marketing (acquisition), network effects (amplify both marketplace sides over time). Influence matrix: DAU — strongly product; acquisition — strongly marketing; retention — strongly product and network; ARPU — moderately product. Driver test: "can I do this without spending extra resources?" If yes, it's baseline, not traction. Averages trap, numeric illustration: cohort A (500 users, 60% retention, ARPU 2000) + cohort B (500, 15%, 500): "by averages" 468,750₽ vs 637,500₽ by cohorts — a 36% divergence.

**How:** (1) define the business's Supply — the limiting resource (ad inventory, couriers, hotel rooms, SKUs, servers); it's also the model's upper cap; (2) collect market assumptions on a separate sheet, each parameter with a source and evidence class: monthly market volume — start flat and check that month × 12 matches public data; CAGR (annual) → monthly rate; seasonal coefficients summing to 12.0 with the percent base stated; market share and its dynamics (sources: agency reports, associations, company dashboards, expert estimates — labeled as such); (3) decompose demand by segment (segments have different checks and conversion rates, e.g. performance SMB vs branding enterprise): market count → CR to lead → CR to payer → check (low/high season); reverse calculation from known payers upward is allowed; (4) build the supply funnel: resource × conversions (fill rate, viewability) × monetization (CTR × CPC or impressions/1000 × CPM); compute RPMI = revenue / inventory × 1000 — the end-to-end efficiency; (5) bring the three top-down numbers to the summary sheet (by year) and compare; comparability band ±10–20%; (6) interpret divergences — each one is a hypothesis to check, not an error; (7) build baseline separately from growth: a base share trend (often a negative multiplier −0.1 pp/month), retro-fact from dashboards up to the fact/forecast boundary, then formula extension; (8) bottom-up on top of baseline: driver hypotheses with uplift on specific input metrics, computed by cohorts (never aggregated), with cohort decay and phased retention (D1/D7/D30, M1/M2/M3); (9) reconcile bottom-up with top-down: gap <15% — green zone (close the remainder with explicit hypotheses); gap 15–30% — yellow; gap >30% — an error in one model's assumptions; bottom-up above top-down — inflated assumptions; (10) model hygiene: hardcoded assumptions on a separate sheet, color-highlighted, each with a source; everything else formulas ("extension by formulas"); not a single embedded constant. Divergence interpretation: SOM < Demand < Supply → underutilization — attract demand, don't expand the resource; SOM < Supply < Demand → resource-limited — grow supply; Demand < SOM → share inflated or checks understated — recheck data and conversions; everything within ±10–20% — the model is consistent, fix it as the working frame. Sheet structure (course reference): Assumptions (M/D/S/SS parameters with sources and confidence) → Traction model (metric rows × months, year marker, annual SUMIF totals) → Costs assumptions → Checks (GMV < SOM cap etc.).

**Pitfalls:** the "dream model" — market 100B × 1% = 1B with no answer where users come from and at what CAC/retention; bottom-up without top-down (no ceiling — the model pierces the market) and top-down without bottom-up (no mechanics — doesn't explain where the money comes from); flat where seasonality exists, or seasonality not correlated across parameters (market share shouldn't "breathe" with the season); confusing percents and absolutes; CPM without dividing impressions by 1000; base-wide averages instead of cohorts (segments with 80% and 20% retention produce an "average" 50% and an error of tens of percent); ignoring cohort decay inflates ARPU/LTV by 30–40%; a flat baseline instead of a decaying one — without investment share doesn't stand still, it declines; linear growth or a "hockey stick" without explanation.
