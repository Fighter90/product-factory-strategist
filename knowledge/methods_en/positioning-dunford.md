# Dunford positioning: context, not a slogan

## When to apply
You are launching a product or entering a new segment; the product is "good, but nobody understands what it is for and how it differs"; funnel conversion sags because the category is unclear; competitors explain themselves more simply and clearly.

## Essence
- Positioning is the **context** that makes the product's value obvious to specific buyers; it is not a slogan, not a comparison table, and not "our fun tone of voice".
- **6 components**: (1) **competitive alternatives** — what customers would use instead if your product did not exist (including "do nothing", manual processes, and Excel); (2) **unique attributes** — what only you have; (3) **value** — the outcomes the attributes deliver to the customer (a feature ≠ value); (4) **target segments** — those who need this value the most (best-fit customers); (5) **the market category** — the frame through which the buyer "reads" your product; (6) **trends** — external forces that amplify the value.
- The chain is mandatory in the order 1→6: alternatives define the attributes, the attributes — the value, the value — the segments, the segments and value — the category, and the category — whether trends are relevant.
- **3 category options**: head-to-head (fighting inside an existing category), a new category (creating your own — expensive and slow, the market has to be educated), a hybrid (moving the product into a different frame: "X for Y").
- A trend is only an amplifier, directly tied to the value of the attributes; a trend for the trend's sake adds noise and distrust.
- The result is a **positioning document**: alternatives, attributes, value, segments, category, trends; the pitch, the landing page, and the messaging are rewritten from it.

## How to do it
1. Write an honest list of competitive alternatives, including the status quo and "the customer does it by hand".
2. Find the attributes none of the alternatives have (including the non-obvious ones: data, integrations, the pricing model, support).
3. Translate the attributes into values: "what the customer gains / stops suffering from"; group them into 3–5 value themes.
4. Select the segments for which these values are an acute pain: who already pays the alternatives the most, and whom you can actually reach.
5. Choose the category frame in which the value is most obvious and, if justified, add a trend amplifier.
6. Fix the positioning in a document, rewrite the pitch/landing page, test the wording at the first touchpoints (qualitatively or via A/B).

## Pitfalls
- Positioning "for everyone" is positioning for no one: the segment is chosen deliberately, and the rest are deliberately ignored.
- Features instead of value: a list of attributes without "why this matters to the customer" does not position anything.
- A new category requires budget and time to educate the market; without a strong distribution resource, the hybrid frame is usually the better bet.
- Fitting into a hyped category without your own unique attributes is a guaranteed failure: the category is derived from strengths, not the other way around.
