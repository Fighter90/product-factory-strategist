# Design Thinking and UX Design (EN summary)

**When:** the solution isn't obvious, the product is new or being rethought, success depends on understanding the user, uncertainty is high, and there is access to people and readiness to experiment; helps avoid "building the product for ourselves". Not suitable when: the decision is already made, the task is regulated, strict quantitative calculations or scientific research are needed, there's no access to users, the deadline is "yesterday", or the system is rigidly standardized.

**Core:** design thinking is a human-centered systemic approach to complex tasks: it starts with empathy to the user, and ideas are validated through fast prototypes and tests. Not one method but a **framework** — a sequence of expanding and narrowing. Five stages (Stanford d.school): Empathize → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test — but the process is **nonlinear**: tests expose new problems, prototyping sparks ideas, you loop back. **Double Diamond**: two diamonds, each with a divergent phase (expand: generation, the whole field of options) and a convergent one (narrow: analysis, selection). Diamond 1: empathy → analysis and synthesis (defining the problem). Diamond 2: idea generation → prototype and test (the solution). Teams that ran interviews, extracted jobs and tested prototypes were already doing design thinking.

**Choosing the experience artifact:** CJM — the panorama and emotional line ("why is the cart abandoned"); User Flow — steps inside the interface, clicks and fields ("simplify signup to one screen"); BPMN — roles, systems, SLA ("coordinate the warehouse and the courier"). CJM first, then extract a User Flow for the narrow section; don't mix roles/processes with emotions in one artifact. Artifacts feed the roadmap: CJM → UX backlog (what to fix), User Flow → frontend tasks, point of view + HMW → ideation.

**How:** 1) **Empathy (divergence)**: walking the experience yourself ("step into the user's shoes" — go through the journey), observation without prompts or judgment (what they do, how and why; a desk full of sticky-note reminders signals a painful process), listening (interviews with open questions, stories, chains of "why"); raw data with context, no interpretation; mistakes: interviewing colleagues instead of users, questionnaires instead of interviews, jumping to solutions. 2) **Analysis and synthesis (convergence) → Define**: analysis narrows the field — patterns and tensions in raw data; synthesis packages findings into a working problem statement. Steps: reread the interviews, extract quotes and facts (no conclusions) → mark the unexpected, tense, contradictory → group the recurring or acute → package into an insight (observation + explanation + why it matters) → choose a synthesis form: point of view, empathy map, personas, Venn diagram, CJM, HMW. **Point of View — 4 stickies**: (1) "We met…" a specific person/role; (2) "And we were surprised to discover…" a real tension-fact; (3) "We think…" the need as a job, not a solution; (4) "It would be amazing to help…" a direction sketch. A good POV: a specific person, genuine surprise, the need as a job, the solution only sketched, the insight not too broad. Then HMW: `How might we <desired outcome> for <segment>, avoiding <constraint>?` — several formulations of one barrier. 3) **Idea generation (divergence)**: brainstorming rules — no criticism; welcome wild ideas; build on others' ideas; keep the problem in mind; write everything down; quantity breeds quality; brainwriting (silently, so introverts are heard); don't interrupt; practice: 8 ideas in 8 minutes on the chosen POV. 4) **Idea selection (convergence)**: dot-voting (3 dots each), ICE (Impact–Confidence–Ease, 1–10), the owner's "golden voice" under time pressure; ~5-minute timebox; never select by open discussion — the dominator wins. 5) **Prototype and test**: a prototype is a way to fail cheaply, **not a presentation**: paper + markers, FigJam blocks, Figma, Wizard-of-Oz (a human plays the system — good for AI, voice, assistants); low-fi in ~20 minutes: one or two key screens, no color or pixel-perfection. Corridor ("express") test: 3 respondents, 3 questions, ~15 minutes — show the prototype, ask them to do the task, ask "what's unclear", record the quote and the stuck moment; if 2–3 people stumble in the same place — return to the idea or redo the prototype. One key hypothesis, a legend and tasks, observation without leading questions; don't test on colleagues who know the task; full-scale tests → the usability-testing card. 6) **Use in parts**: with data already available, start at Ideate → Prototype → Test; think about the problem, not the checkbox of completing all steps; without user access, honestly mark empathy as replaced by assumptions and the conclusions as hypotheses. 7) **"Start tomorrow" checklist** (6 steps, ~2.5 hours): 2 users × 30 min → 5 quotes that surprised you → one point of view → Crazy-8 → dot-vote and a draft → show it to 3 people and ask what's unclear.

Templates: POV `We met [who, context] → And we were surprised to discover [contradicting fact, source] → We think [need/job] → It would be amazing to help [direction]`; HMW set of 3–5 formulations of one barrier from different angles (speed it up, remove a step, remove anxiety, make it pleasant); idea card: name | which POV/HMW it solves | why better than current | risk | ICE | what to verify with a prototype | metric; corridor-test log: `Prototype | Task | Participant | Where they stalled (time) | Quote | Conclusion | What we change`.

**Pitfalls:** skipping empathy → a product "for ourselves"; interpreting before extracting facts → confirming your own hypotheses; ignoring "small" oddities → strong insights lost; a POV/insight too broad → no concrete ideas; criticism during ideation → the flow blocks; a beautiful mockup instead of a prototype → time lost; testing on colleagues → barriers invisible; mixing roles and emotions in one artifact → mush; running the method when the solution is predetermined → imitation; treating a 3-person test as statistics → false confidence (it's a signal, not proof).
