# Platform Concept: Justification, Boundary, MVP, Patterns, Defense (EN summary)

**When:** "how to justify a platform opportunity to internal clients and the sponsor?", "what goes into the core and what stays with the client?", "which client to start with and what counts as the platform MVP?", "which integration pattern to choose and how to defend the concept so resources are granted?". Used after the ecosystem audit and before the economics/risks cards.

**Core:** a platform is not a technology but a **contract**: an ownership boundary + the price and rules of transition + trust. Roles: platform owner (product and its development), sponsor (investment, conflict resolution, rule enforcement, first clients), internal clients (migration, their own scenarios), teams affected by migration, engineering and other functions. Two-level value: for the company (less duplication, standardization, economies of scale, quality/risk control) and for the client (faster launch, less own development, ready data and capabilities); the typical conflict — the platform wants standardization and a long roadmap, the client wants specifics, a quick fix and customization while bearing migration costs — is resolved through the sponsor and explicit rules. **Boundary:** Core (common to all clients, fully platform-managed; putting an element in the core takes away the client's right to decide), Adaptations (client settings within pre-provided frames), Exceptions (the client does it themselves off-platform; the platform is not responsible for the result). Core test (3 questions; any "no" → adaptation): is it identical across all first clients (not "in theory"); are you ready to answer for it to everyone at once (core failure breaks everyone); would it hurt to take it back in a year (narrowing the core costs trust — widening costs a sprint, narrowing costs trust). Symmetric risks: a wide core (slow onboarding, "half a year reading docs", the platform gets bypassed) vs a narrow core ("we'll build our own anyway", the same code written three times, forks instead of a platform). Case-study boundary principles: modularity (the client takes part of the core, not all of it); a constructor instead of a finished product (the scenario moves to the client); flexible participants (roles and their number are a parameter, not a core constant); priorities driven by client requests; no business logic in the core. **Interface as contract (4 questions):** what and at what level the platform guarantees (SLA is a promise, not a dashboard metric); what it explicitly does NOT guarantee and leaves to the client (without an explicit list, boundary disputes are inevitable); how the client learns about changes (versions, backward compatibility, old-version lifetime); how much the first integration costs — in the client's work-days (compare "platform vs build it yourself in a month", not "platform vs nothing"). **Rollout and MVP:** top-down mandate — fast spread and a uniform standard, but the core is designed without feedback; works when the benefit is obvious and the directive encodes a strategic choice (the classic cloud-provider mandate for service interfaces). Bottom-up voluntary — the core is tested on real tasks from day one, but every team must be persuaded individually. First-client criteria (score 0–2; any zero = reject; "he agreed" is not an answer): high motivation (needs the solution now), a clear counted pain the client names themselves, integration readiness (resource in the nearest quarter), an influential sponsor (without them the first priority conflict kills the pilot). **Platform MVP = minimal core + first client + falsifiable value hypothesis**: minimal core means only what the first client needs to get value (more than three components is not minimal); the hypothesis is refutable — "the client launches in N weeks instead of M months", not "the platform speeds up development". Distinct from a product MVP: you prove to an internal team that your core is cheaper than building it themselves; the error cost is higher (the client spent sprints), the right to error smaller, and there is no cheap experiment. **MVP ≠ PoC**: a PoC proves feasibility to yourself and the resource-givers (opens the right to ask for resources); an MVP proves value to the first client (opens the right to approach the next ones) — confusing them loses trust at the first bug or loses the resources. The path "point solution → platform" beats "platform right away": generalization on facts (you know what worked and what the first client worked around), and a working result within a quarter is itself the resource argument; "platform right away" is justified only with a top-down standard and knowingly low variability. **Seven concept blocks:** problem (measurable, whose pain), clients (2–3 concrete first-launch teams), core, adaptations, integration (what the client must do, how many days), risks + mitigation, success criteria (metrics in six months). **Seven integration patterns** (flexibility traded against support cost): Shared service (ready screen/function as-is, cheap to plug in; client changes nothing, owner updates without approval), Core + adapters (maximum flexibility; every client's adapter is supported for years), Design system (unified language, fast typical builds; punishes originality), Event-driven (loose coupling, cheap new consumers; data divergence has no clear owner — logs of several systems), Rules engine (rules change without a release; configuration becomes a second product), API-first (total freedom of interface and scenario; quality and coherent experience controlled by the client), Boilerplate (instant start via code copy; versions diverge, one bug fixed ten times). Choose by 4 questions: how different the clients are (different → core+adapters/rules engine; similar → shared service); who owns the experience/UX (client → API-first; platform → design system; the choice is irreversible); real-time needed (yes → synchronous call; a fact suffices → event-driven); how often rules change (often → move to configuration). The pattern defines **who pays for change**: core+adapters — the platform; API-first — the client. **Resourcing the prototype:** a dedicated team (needs goal-setting and a stream), a share of neighbors' time (hard to coordinate), or a feature team inside a unit (self-sufficient); resources are often secured by a **deal** — trading measurable value now (e.g. taking on the cluster's tech debt) for team time; the exchange must be visible to both sides, otherwise it's hope, not a plan. **Defense:** architecture (boundary, pattern, constraints) + integration (rules and cost in client-days) + economics. Top-down defense — early and upward, protecting the idea and technical validity; bottom-up — through buy-in, with live clients and collected demand by defense time. Client commit vs politeness: "interesting, tell us when it's ready" costs nothing; commits are: integration in their quarterly plan, pain named in numbers, readiness to say it aloud at the defense, written confirmation conditional on a specific checkable deadline. Constraint→risk translation (3 steps): what breaks at the client (not at us) → how we compensate → if uncompensable, still disclose — stakeholders must know what they agree to. A strong defense answers: what exactly we ask for (people/money/time in numbers); what will count as failure (otherwise any result gets declared success); constraints stated honestly with plans; demand confirmed by clients, not only by the platform team.

**How:** (1) describe participants; per stakeholder answer 5 questions: value gained, risk lost, changes required, how they'll judge success, buy-in condition; (2) formulate the value proposition ("For [internal clients] who [try to do the job], the solution creates [value] via [shared capability]; the alternative [X] is impractical because [argument]"); (3) draw the boundary: allocate the capability into core/adaptations/exceptions (table: what · basket · why · error cost; ≥2 rows per basket), run the core test on every core row, peer-review the split; (4) choose rollout (top-down/bottom-up/hybrid) and the first client by the 4 criteria; (5) assemble the MVP: minimal core ≤3 components, falsifiable hypothesis, failure criterion; (6) choose the pattern by the 4 questions; fix the 5 integration rules (guarantees/SLA, what stays with the client, behavior on platform failure — degradation or stop, versioning, integration cost in client-days); (7) plan resources as an explicit deal; (8) assemble the 7 blocks and rehearse the defense in 4 parts: problem and clients (who confirmed, in what form) → core boundary and MVP with the first pilot → integration rules and integration cost → what we ask and what we promise; drill stakeholder questions by role (finance partner, commercial sponsor, vertical product leader, CTO).

**Pitfalls:** a concept with no post-launch owner, no migration rules for teams with their own solutions (the majority), or no measurable effect — cut at the first reprioritization; showing a PoC to a client as an MVP (they expect a finished product and lose trust) or an MVP to leadership instead of a PoC (the "will it work technically" question unanswered — no resources); a core shaped by one strong client's private need; client business logic in the core; SLA as a dashboard metric with no obligation and consequences; integration cost estimated in your own estimates instead of the client's work-days; "we need leadership support" as the ask instead of specific people, money and time.
