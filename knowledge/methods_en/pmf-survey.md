# PMF survey: measure product/market fit with one question

## When to apply
The product has been live for a few months and has active users — you need to know whether PMF exists and on which segment, before investing in growth.

## Core idea
- The key question to ask active users: **"How would you feel if the product were no longer available?"** — with answers "very disappointed" / "somewhat disappointed" / "not very disappointed" / "it didn't help".
- Sean Ellis's benchmark: **≥40% answering "very disappointed"** is a precursor of PMF. Below 40%, the product is not yet obligated to scale: product/segment work comes first.
- **What matters is not the average but segmentation**: find WHO those 40% are (by role, scenario, frequency, acquisition channel) and strengthen the product/marketing specifically for them (the Superhuman method: build a profile of the "high-score" users, narrow the positioning, work only with this segment until the next measurement).
- Add open-ended questions: "What type of people benefit the most?", "What is the main benefit you get?" — positioning copy is taken from the answers, not invented.
- The survey does not replace retention: a PMF score without repeat usage is a false signal.

## How to do it
1. Select the active users (for example, ≥N sessions per month); run the survey in-product, not on a cold database.
2. Collect ≥40–80 responses — a volume large enough to split into segments; compute the share of "very disappointed" answers and the breakdowns.
3. Single out the segment with the maximum score → interview them → phrase the value proposition in their own words.
4. Decision: score ≥40% plus retention → grow; below that → discovery (an opportunity tree) and/or a segment change.

## Pitfalls
- Surveying everyone indiscriminately (including those who "tried it once") — the score drops without giving a diagnosis.
- Believing the average percentage without breakdowns — the main signal (the segment) gets lost.
- Measuring once and forever: PMF is a movement, the measurement is repeated after significant changes.
