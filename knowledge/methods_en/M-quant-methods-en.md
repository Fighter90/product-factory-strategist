# Quantitative Methods: Survey, Sampling, Data Analysis (EN summary)

**When:** measuring "how many, how often, in whom": segment share, barrier prevalence, group differences, effect of a change, feature priority. The card covers the full path: hypotheses → operationalization → questionnaire → sample → fieldwork & QC → analysis → interpretation.

**Core:** Qualitative methods explain why and how (depth); quantitative show how many and how widespread. Combos: top-down (numbers → interviews explain) and bottom-up (interviews → survey checks frequencies and weights). Quant specifics: hypotheses mandatory; a firm plan before launch; you measure indirectly and can't fix a question mid-field — hence confidence in question clarity and in validity (measured what was intended; nothing else interfered). Hypothesis types: descriptive ("A is related to B" — any self-explanation of behavior is also descriptive); causal ("A influences B") — reliably checked only by an experiment with a control group (A/B), not by a regular survey; beware "because/since/influences" and the treacherous "why?" question in a questionnaire. Three levels: theoretical ("users prefer buying from businesses") → empirical ("under equal conditions they'll pick a professional seller") → statistical ("a significant click difference between business and private accounts"); a strong hypothesis is several questions together. Operationalization: write exactly what you measure, how you understand it, and how you'll measure it; avoid wording readable in different ways ("do you trust the service?" — trust in what: safety, price, quality?). Validity — three pre-launch checks: construct (we know exactly what we test; a question↔hypothesis matrix), content/face (respondents understand the question as we do; cognitive probing and pilot — people retell questions in their own words), internal (no extraneous influence: question order, social desirability, moment context; randomization, formulation rules). Scale types and allowed operations: nominal (=) — shares, mode, contingency tables (χ²; Fisher exact for small cells); mean is meaningless; ordinal (=, >, <) — median, top-2 shares, nonparametric tests, rank correlations; interval (equal distances assumed) — mean and stricter tests with caveats; ratio (absolute zero) — the same plus ratios of indicators. Transformations when analysis needs another form: collapsing interval to ordinal (NPS 0–10 → detractor/neutral/promoter for χ²) is a legitimate simplification for signals; ordinal → interval ("pseudometrization") only at N ≥ 7 with symmetric wording and an "equal intervals assumed" footnote, otherwise median + IQR; nominal → k−1 dummies for regression, effect-coding (−1, 0, +1) for ANOVA, rank coding for ordinal correlations (Spearman); zeros in ratio scales at log-transform: +1 or separate handling. Record scale_type and coding in the metric passport. Null measurement: a difference/preference hypothesis is always checked against something (before/after, control, competitor, previous wave) — always state the comparison object. Sample size for a share (binary question): n = z²·p·(1−p)/e², z = 1.96 at 95%, p = 0.5 if unknown (the maximum), e = acceptable error in shares; the result is the number of complete quality questionnaires. Reference points at 95%, p = 0.5: ±10 pp ≈ 96; ±5 pp ≈ 385 (course rounds to ≈400); ±3 pp ≈ 1067. Finite population correction n_corr = n/(1+(n−1)/N): N=2000 → ≈322; N=10,000 → ≈370; N>20,000 — negligible. If the expected share differs from 0.5, n shrinks (p ≈ 0.1 → almost half; rare-event example: conversion ~2% at ±1 pp → p = 0.02, e = 0.01 → n ≈ 750); plugging p = 0.5 for a 1% phenomenon inflates the budget. Design effect and weights: n_final = n_calc × DEFF × buffer 1.1–1.2; DEFF ≈ 1.0–1.1 for simple random/proportional stratification; ≈ 1.5–2.0 for clusters; ≈ 1.3–1.5 for a non-random online panel ("better to overestimate than under-recruit"). Post-stratification weights after the field: w_i = strata share in population / share in sample (target 12%, recruited 9% → w = 1.33); normalize so Σw = n; apply only to QC-passed rows; keep raw and weighted versions; a quota "hole" (no observations in a segment) can't be fixed by weights — order more. Two-group share comparison, per-group n at α = 0.05 two-sided, 80% power: n ≈ 7.85·[p₁(1−p₁)+p₂(1−p₂)]/(p₁−p₂)²: 40% vs 50% → ≈385 per group; 40% vs 55% → ≈170; 30% vs 40% → ≈353; 5% vs 6% → ≈8000 — the smaller the effect, the faster n explodes. Course practical guidance: feature-choice (Kano) surveys and CJM validation from ≈300; segmentation surveys several hundred (long questionnaires cut completion). Questionnaire: screening & legend (who qualifies, purpose, duration, data handling) → main part → close (open question, thanks, incentive terms, contacts); branching designed in advance. Question types: single choice (mutually exclusive options), multiple choice, scale (not only numeric; replace digits with words — 0–10 and −5…+5 feel different though computed the same), open (replace with closed where possible, limit count). Nine formulation rules: (1) terminologically clear to both sides; (2) no negations; (3) doesn't imply a single answer; (4) no double-barreled questions ("how often do you visit and buy?"); (5) doesn't provoke a socially desirable answer ("you know we have postpayment?"); (6) closed over open; (7) anchored to a concrete situation (last purchase); (8) account for survey context (right after order vs a week later); (9) account for branching logic. Sampling strategies: random (equal chance; full list or event stream; costly, needs a list); quota (artificial quotas by gender/age/region; group comparisons and panels; not random); stratified (strata with random inside; heterogeneous populations; needs known distribution); cluster (cities, branches; spread audiences, cheaper; higher error — design effect); snowball (rare and closed groups, B2B; strong bias). Fieldwork and QC: soft launch (30–50 questionnaires) to check timing, dropouts, logic; controls: 1–2 attention traps, timer (reject faster than a third of the pilot's median time), straight-lining (SD = 0 across scales), repeat limits (IP/cookie/fingerprint), meaningfulness of open answers, per-screen dropout monitoring (a sharp drop on one screen = a broken question or UI). QC pipeline: flags → quality score QScore = 0.3×time + 0.4×attention + 0.3×meaningfulness (0–100; cut below ~70) → weights on cleaned rows → loss report (N raw / N clean / reasons). Stop triggers: trap failures >25% in a row → pause delivery; sharp fall of median time in a short window → check for bot traffic; screen dropout >30–35% → fix and relaunch; rejected >15% of plan → reorder with a loss coefficient. Fix rules and stops before launch; the field log matters more than the presentation. Dropout reduction: progress bar "step X of N" (−10 pp), split big matrices into screens (−8), mobile layout with autosave (−6…−8), "~3 min left" (−5), skip-logic showing only relevant questions (up to −15). Analysis: the point of statistics is deciding whether a difference is sampling noise or significant; never compare "3.7 vs 4.2" or "43% vs 50%" without a test and interval. Decision tree: can answers be ordered? No (nominal) → shares and mode; to check that 48/52% statistically equals 50/50 — contingency tables (χ²; Fisher exact for small cells). Yes (ordinal/interval) → correlation (association, doesn't prove cause) or regression (only if A plausibly precedes B). One indicator measured repeatedly in one group (before/after)? Yes → paired tests (paired t or Wilcoxon; compute each respondent's after−before Δ, don't compare columns). No → independent-sample tests (t-test; Mann–Whitney for ordinal; ANOVA / Kruskal–Wallis for 3+ groups). Multiple comparisons → corrections (Bonferroni, Holm), otherwise false "wins". Normality before parametric tests: n < 50 → Shapiro–Wilk; 50 ≤ n ≤ 300 → Kolmogorov–Smirnov (Lilliefors); n > 300 → by CLT, plot and |skewness| < 1 suffice. Non-normality or outliers → nonparametric analogs (Wilcoxon instead of paired t; Mann–Whitney instead of t; Kruskal–Wallis instead of ANOVA). Power and errors: α = 0.05 — a 5% false-alarm risk; β (missed effects) — aim 1−β ≥ 0.8; "p = 0.051, almost significant" is not an argument: recompute the MDE, collect more data, or admit no conclusion. Effect size is mandatory: Cohen's d for two means (0.2 small / 0.5 medium / 0.8 large); odds ratio / relative risk for shares; Cramér's V (0–1) for categorical tables. Course example: n = 10,000, 0.1 pp difference, p < 0.001, d = 0.05 — statistically "significant", business-useless. Always report: test, p-value, effect size, and confidence interval (±1.96·SE for a share; "whiskers" on charts) — p-value without effect size is half a truth: large samples make trivial differences significant; small samples hide real ones. Three processing steps: state the fact ("80% of zoomers chose the no-communication option"), interpret (what it means), discuss (link to other data, propose new hypotheses — e.g. if 80% avoid communication but those who communicated rate highly, the problem may be at the start of the dialog, not in communication itself). Causality: a one-shot survey doesn't prove "A influences B"; feature effects need an experiment (A/B with control) or a control-group comparison; before/after without control mixes effect with time (season, promos). Visualization — the "3-B" rule: Bold (one accent color for the key series, others muted), Brief (one chart — one message, ≤5–7 series), Beautiful (even labels, CVD-safe colors, no 3D/shadows — cleanliness = trust). Chart per data: shares (yes/no, top-2) → 100% stacked bar with CI whiskers (±1.96·SE); 3–7 categories → horizontal bar sorted descending, numbers on bars; 8+ categories → Pareto (bars + cumulative % line, focus on top-80%); Likert scales → 100% stacked bar (polarization visible), heatmap at 5+ questions; means → box-plot or error-bar with CI; cohorts/dynamics → heatmap sorted by date. Anti-patterns: pie with >5 sectors; 3D and donuts; two Y-axes with different scales; red-green palettes (colorblind-unfriendly); heatmap without legend. Chart titles state the conclusion ("67% choose delivery — +6 pp YoY"), not the description ("a shares diagram"). Story-dashboard: Headline → Context (method, sample, date) → Main chart → Support (segments, details) → Action ("we rebuild onboarding step 3").

**How:** (1) plan: goal, decision, hypotheses (each typed descriptive/causal, with variables, measurement, comparison criterion and the statistical test chosen in advance); define the population and to whom conclusions extend (subgroups need their own sample sizes); choose a sampling strategy, compute size, add buffer; write the questionnaire, run cognitive probing (3–5 people retell questions in own words) and a pilot; (2) field: soft launch, QC controls and stop-triggers monitored, dropout reduced by the listed techniques; (3) clean data by the pre-fixed rules, weight where needed, then analyze per the decision tree; report test, p-value, effect size, confidence interval; (4) visualize by 3-B and the chart-per-data table; build the story-dashboard ending in an action.

**Pitfalls:** causal conclusions from correlation or a single survey; trusting the "why?" question; unoperationalized concepts; double-barreled, leading questions, negations, provoked "right" answers; mean of an ordinal scale as an exact number; means without distributions; comparing means without a test; no null measurement ("NPS became 40" with nothing to compare); conclusions about "everyone" from a one-channel sample; self-selection bias; ignoring who didn't answer; sample computed without subgroups, design effect and rejection losses; skipped pilot; questionnaire too long — completion collapse; multiple comparisons without corrections; significance hunting after collection; statistical significance taken as practical importance; ignored confidence intervals; paired data analyzed as independent.
