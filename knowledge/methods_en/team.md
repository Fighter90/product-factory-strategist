# Team and People (EN summary)

**When:** the strategy looks right on paper and you must check who, and in what time, will build it. The "team and resources" block requires assuming team composition and work distribution, and — given the organization's size and product stage — compiling the stakeholder list you need agreements with. Even with AI, launching without people is a fantasy.

**Core:** company stage defines team structure. Startup (0→1, PMF search): focus on speed and insight; founder-PM, full-stack engineer, growth generalist, light analytics; 5–8 people (another source: 3–7), T-shaped skills; instant decisions; finance/legal minimal (Airbnb 2009: founders photographed listings and did support themselves). Scale (1→10): reproducibility and unit economics; head of growth, performance marketing, product teams, data/BI, financial controller; decisions in days; from "heroes" to a system (Uber: city ops teams + central product). Platform/ecosystem (10→100): structure, governance, platform, AI; CPO/CPTO, platform team, data science, international operations, legal/compliance; decisions in weeks–months (Mercado Libre: marketplace + fintech as a separate growth machine). Scale-readiness signs: PMF confirmed (stable retention), positive unit economics or a visible path to it, a reproducible acquisition channel, the team "suffocating", data exists. Minimal startup roles and what breaks without them: founder/PM (decisions, strategy, priorities — without it chaotic motion; not AI-replaceable); full-stack engineer (prototype, MVP — no product; partially via vibe coding); growth generalist (GTM, channels, first users — the product is invisible to the market; not replaceable); light analyst (MAU, retention, conversion — blind decisions; partially AI dashboards). Practice rule: every role seems optional until needed (a lawyer for licenses, a marketer for launch, a CTO for scale); the most forgotten startup role is go-to-market — a product without a channel doesn't exist. **Vibe coding boundary:** sufficient for internal tools (10–100 users, limited liability, days to build); insufficient for external user services where data, UX, anti-fraud, legal requirements and support are mandatory; the question is not "can AI write code" but "who is accountable to the external user". Two paradigms: Netflix (density of strong executors, minimal bureaucracy) vs Amazon (two-pizza teams of 6–8 with full ownership and P&L). Org structures: functional (efficient early, fast decisions; many customers, every idea "most important", resource switching), product (P&L ownership, autonomy; duplication across departments, competition for resources), matrix (deep expertise; dual subordination, priority conflicts — business says "capture the market", product says "fit the strategy"). **Learn the rules of the game:** in any company find out who the key stakeholders are (without whose approval or review the project doesn't advance), who manages priorities on shared resources, where to bring ideas, who the "neighbors" doing similar work or competing for a journey segment/budget are, whom not to forget to ask (legal, support, moderation), who gives feedback. Decision culture varies wildly (a 2013 startup: decisions by the co-founder/CTO; a 2021 marketplace: project managers control development; a 2025 marketplace: teams propose initiatives, resources allocated by reprioritization and quarterly agreements; a 2026 startup: developers don't write code, they create agents). **Hiring is the hidden variable:** traction is often drawn as if the team is complete from day one, while in reality there's nobody to build yet; bake hiring timing into traction (e.g. team 2→3→4→6 gives 50%→70%→85%→100% of target pace by month — an illustration, not a norm). Hire vs outsource: hiring 3–6 months, higher carrying cost, high quality/control, low flexibility; outsourcing 1–2 months (tender + contract), cheaper at start and costlier long-run, medium control, high flexibility; other options for deficits — vibe coding/AI for simple tasks, redistribution (conflict-prone). Team growth must track revenue/metrics: inflated headcount without results is a red flag for finance control. **Corporate launch gates:** investment case → legal/compliance → stakeholders → budget → launch, each taking days to months; budget windows fall in specific cycle months (4, 7, 11 in the course example) — learn the local calendar. **Hidden costs of neighboring teams:** support (playbooks, training, FAQ — 2–4 weeks, else tickets grow and CSAT drops), moderation (rules, ML models — 1–3 months, else fraud and complaints), marketing (budget, channels — planned 3–6 months ahead, else no traffic for the MVP), supply partners (onboarding, integration — 1–6 months, else no supply), finance (budget in the cycle window, else no money), IT infrastructure (servers, load — 2–4 weeks, else outages); traffic inside a superapp (homepage placement) is a separate resource without which the service is invisible. **Realistic timing formula:** Real time = T_development + T_hiring (3–6 mo) + T_approvals (2–8 wk) + T_neighbors (2–4 wk) + T_buffer (20–30%). Illustrative launch: discovery (2 wk) → team and resources (4–8 wk) → approvals in parallel (2–6 wk) → neighbor preparation in parallel (2–4 wk) → soft launch and full launch (2–4 wk) ≈ 10–22 weeks + 20% buffer — an illustration of logic, not a norm.

**How:** (1) determine product and company stage, org structure, decision culture; (2) describe the target team per bet: roles, FTE, who exists now, who's missing (guideline: a 6–8-person team owning its metric); (3) for each missing role choose a path — hire / outsource / AI tools / redistribution; estimate timing and impact on traction and phases; run two scenarios: "hired" and "not hired"; (4) build the stakeholder map: who, goals (OKR/KPI), influence (high/med/low), attitude, needed action; high influence → a personal meeting, verify the project doesn't conflict with their KPIs; resource rivals → seek win-win; refresh quarterly; (5) check the gates (investment case, legal, compliance, budget, traffic placement) and the budget calendar; put them on the timeline; (6) prepare neighboring teams — support, moderation, marketing, partners, finance, infrastructure: owner, deadline, status; (7) compute the realistic timeline by the formula and compare with the desired one; if it doesn't converge — change scope, phase or expectations, not "speed up hiring"; (8) fix agreements (DACI + the risk table) and human/organizational risks.

**Pitfalls:** traction drawn with a full team from day one; "freeze everything until hiring" or "plan as if already hired"; hiring before PMF and inflating headcount without revenue; hidden blockers (legal, support, moderation, traffic owner never consulted); underestimating the outsourcing tender (1–2 months) and shared-resource dependencies (designer, architect); applying one company's rules of the game to another; souring relationships in resource fights (people remember the tone, not the content); ignoring matrix dual-subordination conflicts; at scale: headcount detached from metrics (finance will ask "why" — every role justifies its contribution), a startup process left undocumented (duplication, conflicts), no data/BI investment before scaling (blind decisions); at platform: launching without a duplication check against existing functionality — the governance gate sends the project back to start.
