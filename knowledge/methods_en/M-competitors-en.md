# Competitors: Types, Landscape, Five Forces, Strategy Canvas, JTBD Map (EN summary)

**When:** a target segment is chosen (or considered) and you must decide **where you fight and where you deliberately don't**. The point is not to "know the market" for a document: the output is a map of the client's alternatives, an estimate of how hard winning is, a list of choice factors, and a conclusion for strategy.

**Core:** you compete not with companies but **for a specific customer choice in a specific situation** — for time, money, attention, trust/risk; whatever the client already does about the need is the competitor. Types: direct (same job, same way and model), indirect (same top-level job, different way/context), substitutes (radically different solutions up to "none at all"), cannibalization (products of one group taking share from each other). Group comparison table (as in the strategy document sample): competitor groups as columns; mandatory rows "key advantages" and "risks for us" — every risk cell gets a level (high/low) and rationale; below it, growth and promo mechanics by stream — fintech & loyalty (wallets, credit, installment, cashback), assortment (catalog expansion, parallel import), delivery (pickup network, regional speed, express), promo mechanics (mandatory and seasonal promotions, "fair" promos) — to see which levers leaders already occupy and where differentiation is possible, plus buyer choice tools (price dynamics, discount request, comparisons) written out separately as choice factors. Data sources four types, honestly labeled: assumptions, benchmarks, own research, commissioned research under NDA — a number without a source is opinion. Case lessons: Zoom entered late but found enterprise pain (heavy first session) and made join-by-link — a narrow but powerful differentiator with viral growth; Tinkoff found banks' branch dependence and consciously went online-only; Domclick's ≈0.3% mortgage discount didn't create habit — on a mature market **habit and content beat a one-off discount**; new-build classifieds: the hypothesis "developers want cheaper leads" was wrong — the real job was "sell exactly the planned volume" (waves by lot and price), solved with a consultation service and its own managed call center (competence can be bought) — understand the client's business process, not their request. Five frameworks for five questions: Competitive Landscape (which solution models exist, where we sit — two axes from the client's choice factors; plot all solutions, not just direct rivals; real-estate example: "self-service control ↔ delegation" × "broad market view ↔ interpretation and risk reduction"), Porter's Five Forces (how hard winning is: industry rivalry, threat of new entrants, buyer power, supplier power, substitutes — each with level + arguments; classifieds example: rivalry medium-high, entrants low — content and trust are expensive, even a strong branded player took ~10%, buyer power medium-low, supplier power medium, substitutes medium — AI matchmakers, vertical search, telegram channels), Strategy Canvas (what to win with: list choice factors — about a hundred for a large player; score self and competitors from surveys; green factors — hold and communicate, red — fix or consciously drop; blue-ocean breakthrough = flip a red factor to strongly green), JTBD alternative map (journey stages × tasks with importance, difficulty, coverage color, "why hard", current solutions, ideas — hypotheses live where important+hard+uncovered overlap), Feature Comparison (matrix of functions — an **idea bank, not a strategy**: a leader with 30% fewer filters can still win on its own territory because users don't use all filters).

**How:** (1) fix the segment and its job — without them a competitor list is meaningless; (2) collect the client's alternatives: ask "how do you solve this now", watch behavior and payments, add substitutes, DIY and "Excel + messenger"; (3) draw the landscape on two choice-factor axes; mark unoccupied zones and zones not worth entering (changing your business model for someone else's territory); (4) assess difficulty of winning with the five forces for the chosen model; (5) measure choice factors via Strategy Canvas, decide fix-or-drop per red factor; (6) analyze the JTBD map for important, hard, uncovered tasks — mine hypotheses there; (7) run Feature Comparison strictly as an idea bank; (8) per competitor record: group, key advantages, growth/promo mechanics, risk for us (level + rationale), our move or refusal; (9) conclude: which territory we fight for, which we abandon, which advantage we build — feed into goals/bets and risks.

**Pitfalls:** analysis for the document's sake (no "where we fight, where not" conclusion); a list of only direct competitors (substitutes and DIY missed); filling the template instead of answering the question; substituting the task (client asks "cheaper/more", the real job is control, timing, risk); copying competitor features (more features ≠ better); a discount instead of differentiation (users "take the discount and leave" without content and habit); underestimating habit — perceived "convenience" is subjective and habit-dependent; ignoring partner channels and the option to buy the missing competence.
