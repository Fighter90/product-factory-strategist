# JTBD and Advanced JTBD

## When to apply
You need to understand **why** people use (or hire) the product, how to segment the audience by jobs, where value is created, why conversion is low despite a good product, which moment gives the user a sense of value, and which market/job to bet on while the product does not yet exist.

## Essence

### Classic JTBD
- The user "hires" a solution (a product, an action) to get a **job** done — a task they set for themselves. The job describes a transition from point A (a troubled state) to point B (the desired one).
- The job formula: **When** [context, trigger, emotional state] **I want** [the expected outcome with criteria], **so I can** [the result and positive emotions]. Examples from the lecture: Avito — "when I have a thing I don't use, I want to sell it quickly nearby, so I can free up space and get money"; Uber — not "a taxi" but stress relief and time savings.
- The job is primary; products compete for the right to be hired. The focus is not on features but on context and motivation. The best way to segment for product decisions is "job + solution" pairs, not demographics. The method applies to B2B (account for the roles in the buying process and personal motives).
- "Fixing pains" is not enough: a pain is a consequence of the chosen solution doing the job poorly; mature markets offer more opportunity than the fixing of problems.
- **JTBD vs user story:** a user story is "as [a persona], I want [an action], so that [an outcome]" (tied to a persona and a solution); JTBD is situation, motivation, outcome (not tied to a solution). For MVP scope, JTBD helps to see the alternatives (a hotel, an apartment, a friend's place for a night during a match).
- JTBD as a source of problem hypotheses: "The user wants to do X but cannot, because Y — what is blocking them? what workaround are they using?" ([[M-problem-design]]).
- Roles: user / buyer / decision maker; segment also by behaviour (novices vs experienced) and by motivation (an urgent vs a deferred need).

### Advanced JTBD (AJTBD)
- **The job graph** — a hierarchy of a person's jobs: "jobs one level up" require "jobs below"; "many-to-many" links; the relevant part of the graph is analysed (Big Jobs + Core Jobs + Small Jobs). Value, strategy and algorithms for solving business problems follow from the graph.
- **Value = benefit − investment** (money, time, energy): get the job done with smaller investments and greater benefit than the competitor. Expectations are the key to how the brain estimates value; a problem is a signal of a prediction error.
- **The mechanics of value creation:** start performing a job that cannot be performed today; solve problems; perform more jobs with one solution; **kill jobs** (the main mechanic — simplification and convenience is the killing of unnecessary jobs); shorten the delays between jobs; reduce costs without losing functionality. Innovation = applying one or several mechanics. Strategic moves: move to the preceding job (Aviasales — "find cheap tickets", Domclick — the apartment buyer's preceding jobs) or to a higher-level one (LiFT — "level up the personal brand").
- **The value-creation algorithm:** study the job graphs of segments with their current solutions → identify problems and expectations → generate hypotheses about applicable mechanics → rank them by job level and critical sequences.
- **AJTBD gives algorithms for the tasks:** create value; find a segment for a new product; reduce churn and improve retention; grow conversion; communicate the product; scale; escape competition; build disruptive products.
- **AJTBD interviews** — 30–60 minutes, they reveal the customer's job graph; you need ≥ ~30 interviews per market to choose a market through customers (slow and expensive), so expert interviews are used at the market-selection stage.

### Superdiscovery
The stage **before** Discovery: the job to compete for is not yet chosen (there are resources, no idea; a corporation is looking for a direction; a team after an exit; an investor checking the market choice and channels in due diligence). The goal is to choose high-level jobs/a market. Tools: expert interviews, interviews with "shut-down" companies, desk research (competitors, prices, TAM/SAM/SOM, reports). Principles: **experts, not customers** — at this stage talk to those who sell, not buy; **the shut down are gold** — NDAs no longer bind them, there are no reputational risks, and they know the real risks better than the survivors.
The algorithm: set the geography (one country/region) → start expert interviews as early as possible (they are found unpredictably) → find the shut down → desk research → **≥ 3 interviews per market** (5–10 is optimal) for triangulation → run several markets in parallel → choose the jobs and move to AJTBD-Discovery. Data from one region cannot be transferred to another (culture, regulation, behaviour).

### Expert interviews
Interviews with those who already sell products for the jobs of interest (direct and indirect competitors, former participants, consultants, investors). The script blocks: **Segments** (which exist, how they differ, who hurts more, what unites them) · **Job graphs** (the sequence of the segment's subtasks) · **Solutions** (how they solve, how satisfied, what problems) · **New value** (where you see an opportunity; what you would do) · **Trends** · **Risks** · **How to sell**. A minimum of 2 experts (with agreement you can trust it; with divergence, collect more); 5–10 interviews often reveal promising segments or the unpromisingness of a market — instead of months of work and hundreds of thousands of dollars on an MVP. Conversion into such an interview can reach 50% and above; even at 5–10%, a few conversations are enough for the key insights. The price of one interview starts at hundreds of dollars, and it pays off in 9 out of 10 cases. An example of the value: during Superdiscovery for an edtech product, an expert (a sales lead at a local startup) reported that the B2C direction was shutting down — the online-edtech economics in that country did not add up; 8 more interviews confirmed the thesis, and the market entry was cancelled. Those who shut down — minimal barriers to a conversation and the most honest information about risks. Expert motivation: money, contact with the market's projects, helping an acquaintance; you can offer the research results.

### Barriers
Obstacles that prevent the customer from getting the job done with your solution; "value eaters" — they can devour even enormous value, and the customer will leave for the competitor who lowered the barriers. Three categories: **objective** (no documents/money/access, legal and technical constraints — they require changing the product or helping the customer; example: wants a mortgage but has undocumented income — the bank will not accept the papers), **subjective** (fear of the job "I won't manage it", fear of the solution "the contractor is unreliable" — they require communication and proof; example: "building an app is too complicated for me") and **the habit of the current solution** (won't switch simply because they are used to it). The work: uncover them in interviews ("what stopped you from starting?", "were there fears?") → sort by type → handle the subjective ones in sequence (first "the job is doable", then "this particular solution is reliable") → reduce them in communication (cases, guarantees, a trial period, transparency). Sometimes lowering a barrier is easier and cheaper than growing value — one of the most effective mechanics for growing conversion; customers may not talk about fears — emotional contact is needed; it is more profitable to compete where barriers are lower.

### The aha moment
The moment a person feels the value: the brain compares the expected energy efficiency with reality, and a positive gap produces surprise. The stronger the surprise, the higher the probability of purchase and return. Examples: Klarna — "bought now, paid later"; ChatGPT — the first answer that solved a task in seconds instead of hours of googling; delivery — the first order arrived hot in 20 minutes. Goals: move the aha moment as early as possible (ideally before registration and investment: Replit/Lovable let you assemble a first product for free before full registration) and get the maximum share of users to it. Long-time customers do not remember their aha moment — ask those who made their first purchase recently. Different segments have different aha moments.

## Procedure
1. Determine the level of the task: **market selection** (no product) → Superdiscovery; **a product exists / the market is chosen** → AJTBD-Discovery; **conversion/retention** → barriers and the aha moment.
2. Market selection: the given region boundaries → 3–10 expert interviews per market + interviews with the shut down + desk research. For each market record the segments, job graphs, solutions and their problems, risks, how to sell; compare the markets; fix the chosen jobs and their grounds (at least two agreeing sources).
3. Segmentation by jobs: for each segment — Big/Core/Small Jobs, the hired solution, satisfaction, problems, expectations, barriers.
4. Value: choose the mechanics (kill jobs, move to the preceding/high job, combine jobs), write them down as value hypotheses and test ([[M-solution-design]]).
5. The aha moment: (a) ask recent customers "at what moment did you feel the value?"; (b) find in the data the bend of the retention curve and the events correlating with return (compare those who stayed and those who left); (c) define the metric "conversion to value activation" = % of users who reached the aha event, assign an owner; (d) shorten the path: remove steps, give value before payment/registration.
6. Barriers: write them down from the customers' words, mark the type, assign measures (product/communication/guarantees), measure the impact on funnel conversion.
7. Record the result as hypotheses and a conclusion with a confidence estimate (the number of interviews, segments, agreement of sources).

## Template / checklist
```
Segment: ___   Job (When… I want… so I can…): ___   Level: Big | Core | Small
Hired solution: ___ | satisfaction | problems | expectations
Barriers: objective ___ | subjective ___ | habit ___   Measure: ___
Aha moment: event ___ ; activation metric = % who reached ___ ; owner ___
Value mechanic: kill the job ___ | move to the preceding/high ___ | combine ___
Sources: n interviews = ___ ; ≥2 experts agree: yes/no ; region: ___
```
Checklist: the job is described through context–motivation–outcome, not through a feature · the segment is defined by the job, not only by demographics · ≥ 3 expert interviews per market · the shut down are accounted for · the conclusions are not transferred to another region without validation · for the aha moment there is an event in the data.

## Typical mistakes
- Formulating a "job" as a feature or a solution ("I want a filter").
- Segmenting only by age/income; ignoring the buyer/decision maker roles.
- Going into customer interviews without choosing a market (30× the number of markets in interviews).
- One expert — one truth; not seeking diverging opinions.
- Transferring Superdiscovery results from one country to another.
- Treating low conversion with value growth when the real cause is fears and barriers.
- Requiring registration/payment before the aha moment; assuming one aha moment for all segments.
- Ignoring that the customer will not talk about fears without emotional contact.

## Questions that can change the decision
- Which jobs does the segment really hire now, and what is it dissatisfied with?
- Are we competing for the same job, or is there an earlier/higher job?
- What exactly surprises the user the first time, and at which funnel step does it happen?
- Which barriers are objective (a product is needed) and which are subjective (communication is needed)?
- Do the conclusions of ≥ 2–3 independent experts in this region agree?
