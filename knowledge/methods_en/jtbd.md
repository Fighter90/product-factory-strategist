# JTBD and Advanced JTBD

## When to apply
You need to understand **why** people use (or hire) a product, how to segment an audience by jobs, where value is created, why conversion stays low despite a good product, which moment gives the user a feeling of value, and which market/job to bet on while the product doesn't exist yet.

## Core idea

### Classic JTBD
- The user "hires" a solution (a product, an action) to perform a **job** — a task they set for themselves. A job describes the transition from point A (a problematic state) to point B (the desired one).
- Job formula: **When** [context, trigger, emotional state] **I want** [expected outcome with criteria], **so that** [result and positive emotions]. Examples from the lecture: Avito — "when I have a thing I don't use, I want to sell it fast nearby, to free up space and get money"; Uber — not "taxi", but stress relief and time savings.
- The job is primary; products compete for the right to be hired. The focus is not on features but on context and motivation. The best way to segment an audience for product decisions is job + solution pairs, not demography. The method also applies in B2B (account for the roles in the buying process and personal motives).
- "Fixing pains" is not enough: pain is a consequence of the chosen solution performing the job badly; mature markets offer more opportunity than just fixing problems.
- **JTBD vs user story:** a user story is "as [a persona], I want [an action], so that [a result]" (tied to a persona and a solution); JTBD is situation, motivation, outcome (not tied to a solution). For MVP scoping, JTBD helps you see the alternatives (a hotel, an apartment, a friend's place for the night of a match).
- JTBD as a source of problem hypotheses: "The user wants to do X but can't, because Y — what blocks them? which kludge do they use?" ([[M-problem-design]]).
- Roles: user / buyer / decision maker; segment also by behavior (novices vs experienced) and by motivation (urgent vs deferred need).

### Advanced JTBD (AJTBD)
- **Job graph** — a hierarchy of a person's jobs: "jobs one level up" require "jobs one level down"; the links are many-to-many; you analyze the relevant part of the graph (Big Jobs + Core Jobs + Small Jobs). Value, strategy and algorithms for solving business problems follow from the graph.
- **Value = benefit − investments** (money, time, energy): perform the job with smaller investments and more benefit than the competitor. Expectations are the key to how the brain estimates value; a problem is a signal of a prediction error.
- **Value-creation mechanics:** start performing a job that nobody can perform; solve problems; perform more jobs with one solution; **kill jobs** (the main mechanic — simplification and convenience are the killing of redundant jobs); shorten the gaps between jobs; cut costs without losing functionality. Innovation = applying one or several mechanics. Strategic moves: move onto the preceding job (Aviasales — "find cheap flights"; Domklik — the apartment buyer's pre-purchase jobs) or onto a higher-level job (LiFT — "grow a personal brand").
- **Value-creation algorithm:** study the job graphs of the segments together with their current solutions → identify problems and expectations → generate hypotheses about applicable mechanics → rank them by job level and critical sequences.
- **AJTBD provides algorithms for:** creating value; finding a segment for a new product; reducing churn and improving retention; growing conversion; communicating the product; scaling; escaping competition; building disruptive products.
- **AJTBD interviews** — 30–60 minutes, they reveal the client's job graph; you need ≥ ~30 interviews per market to choose a market through clients (slow and expensive), so expert interviews are used at the market-choice stage.

### Superdiscovery
The stage **before** Discovery: the job to compete for hasn't been chosen yet (there are resources but no idea; a corporation looking for a direction; a team after an exit; an investor checking the market choice and channels during due diligence). The goal is to choose high-level jobs / a market. Tools: expert interviews, interviews with shut-down companies, desk research (competitors, prices, TAM/SAM/SOM, reports). Principles: **experts, not clients** — at this stage you talk to those who sell, not those who buy; **shut-downs are gold** — their NDAs are no longer in force, there is no reputational risk, and they know the real risks better than the survivors.
Algorithm: define the geography (one country/region) → start expert interviews as early as possible (they are found unpredictably) → find the shut-downs → desk research → **≥ 3 interviews per market** (5–10 is optimal) for triangulation → run several markets in parallel → choose the jobs and move to AJTBD Discovery. Data from one region cannot be transferred to another (culture, regulation, behavior differ).

### Expert interviews
Interviews with those who already sell products for the jobs you are interested in (direct and indirect competitors, former market participants, consultants, investors). Script blocks: **Segments** (which exist, how they differ, who hurts the most, what unites them) · **Job graphs** (the sequence of the segment's sub-jobs) · **Solutions** (how they solve it, how satisfied they are, what problems remain) · **New value** (where do you see an opportunity; what would you do) · **Trends** · **Risks** · **How to sell**. Minimum 2 experts (agreement can be trusted; if they diverge, collect more); 5–10 interviews often reveal promising segments or the futility of the market — instead of months of work and hundreds of thousands of dollars on an MVP. Conversion into such an interview can reach 50% and higher; even at 5–10%, a few conversations are enough for the key insights. One interview costs from hundreds of dollars and pays off in 9 out of 10 cases. An example of the value: during Superdiscovery for an edtech product, an expert (a sales lead at a local startup) reported that the B2C direction was shutting down — the economics of online edtech in that country didn't add up; 8 more interviews confirmed the thesis, and the market entry was cancelled. Those who shut down are the lowest barrier to a conversation and the most honest source of information about risks. Expert motivation: money, contact with the market's projects, helping an acquaintance; you can offer the research results in return.

### Barriers
Obstacles that prevent the client from performing the job with your solution; "value eaters" — they can fully consume even enormous value, and the client will leave for a competitor who lowered the barriers. Three categories: **objective** (no documents/money/access, legal and technical restrictions — these require changing the product or helping the client; example: wants a mortgage but has undeclared income — the bank won't accept the documents), **subjective** (fear of the job "I won't manage it", fear of the solution "the contractor is unreliable" — these require communication and proof; example: "building an app is too hard for me") and **habit of the current solution** (they won't switch simply because they are used to it). The work: identify them in interviews ("what stopped you from starting?", "were there any fears?") → sort them by type → handle the subjective ones sequentially (first "the job is doable", then "this particular solution is reliable") → reduce them in communication (case studies, guarantees, a trial period, transparency). Sometimes lowering a barrier is easier and cheaper than growing value — it is one of the most effective conversion mechanics; clients may not talk about their fears — you need emotional contact; it pays to compete where barriers are lower.

### Aha-moment
The moment when a person feels value: the brain compares the expected energy efficiency with reality, and a positive gap produces surprise. The stronger the surprise, the higher the probability of purchase and return. Examples: Klarna — "bought now, paid later"; ChatGPT — the first answer that solved a task in seconds instead of hours of googling; delivery — the first order arrived hot in 20 minutes. Goals: move the aha-moment as early as possible (ideally before registration and investment: Replit/Lovable let you assemble a first product for free before full registration) and bring the maximum share of users to it. Long-time customers don't remember their aha-moment — ask those who made their first purchase recently. Different segments have different aha-moments.

## Procedure
1. Determine the task level: **market choice** (no product) → Superdiscovery; **a product exists / the market is chosen** → AJTBD-Discovery; **conversion/retention** → barriers and the aha-moment.
2. Market choice: set the region bounds → 3–10 expert interviews per market + interviews with the shut-downs + desk research. For each market record the segments, job graphs, solutions and their problems, risks, how to sell; compare the markets; fix the chosen jobs and their grounds (at least two agreeing sources).
3. Segmentation by jobs: for each segment — Big/Core/Small Jobs, the hired solution, satisfaction, problems, expectations, barriers.
4. Value: choose the mechanics (kill jobs, move onto the preceding/higher job, combine jobs), write them down as value hypotheses and validate ([[M-solution-design]]).
5. Aha-moment: (a) ask recent clients "at what moment did you feel the value?"; (b) find in the data the break point of the retention curve and the events correlating with return (compare stayers and leavers); (c) define the metric "conversion to activation into value" = % of users who reached the aha event, assign an owner; (d) shorten the path: remove steps, give value before payment/registration.
6. Barriers: capture them in the clients' own words, tag the type, assign measures (product/communication/guarantees), measure the impact on funnel conversion.
7. Record the result as hypotheses and a conclusion with a confidence estimate (number of interviews, segments, source agreement).

## Template / checklist
```
Segment: ___   Job (When… I want… so that…): ___   Level: Big | Core | Small
Hired solution: ___ | satisfaction | problems | expectations
Barriers: objective ___ | subjective ___ | habit ___   Measure: ___
Aha-moment: event ___ ; activation metric = % reaching ___ ; owner ___
Value mechanic: kill job ___ | move onto preceding/higher ___ | combine ___
Sources: n interviews = ___ ; ≥2 experts agree: yes/no ; region: ___
```
Checklist: the job is described through context–motivation–outcome, not through a feature · the segment is defined by the job, not only by demography · ≥ 3 expert interviews per market · the shut-downs are accounted for · conclusions are not transferred to another region without validation · for the aha-moment there is an event in the data.

## Typical mistakes
- Formulating a "job" as a feature or a solution ("I want a filter").
- Segmenting only by age/income; ignoring the buyer/decision-maker roles.
- Going to client interviews before choosing the market (30× the number of markets in interviews).
- One expert = one truth; not seeking diverging opinions.
- Transferring Superdiscovery results from one country to another.
- Treating low conversion as a value problem when the real cause is fears and barriers.
- Demanding registration/payment before the aha-moment; assuming one aha-moment for all segments.
- Ignoring that a client won't talk about fears without emotional contact.

## Questions that can change the decision
- Which jobs does the segment actually hire right now, and what is it dissatisfied with?
- Are we competing for the same job, or is there an earlier/higher job?
- What exactly surprises the user the first time, and at which funnel step does it happen?
- Which barriers are objective (a product is needed) and which are subjective (communication is needed)?
- Do the findings agree across ≥ 2–3 independent experts in this region?
