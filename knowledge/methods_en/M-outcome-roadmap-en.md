# Strategy Timing and Outcome Roadmap (EN summary)

**When:** the bets are chosen and you must answer "what do we do in Q1, what do we defer, what happens if resources fall short or the hypothesis fails". A roadmap here is not a promise to stakeholders but a decision tool under constraints: it shows the choices, fixes the trade-offs, and leaves room for change — a management document that can be challenged.

**Core:** strategy without timing stays an intention. **Feature-roadmap vs outcome-roadmap:** a feature roadmap lists what will be built ("onboarding, filter, page") and creates an illusion of control by substituting activity for result; an outcome roadmap states the result to achieve and verify ("cut time-to-value", "raise activation", "reduce drop-off at the critical step"); a feature roadmap is acceptable only as an internal delivery plan layered on top. **Eight elements of an outcome roadmap:** strategic bet (a large outcome-linked hypothesis; the count is bounded by resources); phases (pilot → validation → scaling; pilot always starts with minimal investment); outcome metric (the specific metric change that is the goal of the whole bet, not just a phase); output metric (a fast proxy for early phases); time-box per phase; dependencies (who we depend on, aligned priorities, blockers); numeric gate criteria for the next phase ("CTR > N% → validation"); a pre-agreed stop signal. **Phases as sequential knowledge purchases:** Pilot/discovery buys a *signal* — does the idea work at all? Qualitative signal, not statistical significance; simple means (scenario simplification, pre-filling, rule-based instead of ML); days–weeks (three months with serious development is already a mini-MVP); output metric. Validation/MVP buys *proof* — a statistically significant effect on the outcome plus side effects; working version, A/B test; weeks–months; outcome metric. Scaling buys *scale* — is the effect stable and does it pay off; full rollout, marketing; months–quarters; outcome + ROI. No phases = weak planning: without them you get "half a year of development → wrong effect → restructuring". Output vs outcome: outcome (retention, revenue, LTV) lags — action → 1–2 weeks to output (block CTR) → 1–2 months to retention → 3–6 months to business; output shows direction but doesn't prove outcome impact, so validation targets the outcome; the outcome metric never disappears — it's the contract with stakeholders (pilot: output; validation: outcome; scaling: outcome + ROI). **The right to fail is planned, not requested:** an error buffer lives in the roadmap, pre-agreed with leadership, time-boxed and resource-capped, with failure criteria and a plan B; a failed hypothesis is a planned outcome, not a catastrophe. **Management contract (course example):** bet — personalized recommendations for a marketplace; metric — 15-day repeat-purchase share 22% → 27% in 6 months; ambition 27%, acceptable 25% (economics converge, fewer resources allocated), below 25% → the bet closes and resources are reassigned. Pilot 2–4 weeks on the current team: rule-based recommendations, output = block CTR above threshold, else plan B (CRM triggers, new pilot). Validation 2–3 months: full system, A/B test, outcome +2 pp significant, else closure. Scaling 2–3 months: full rollout, marketing, ROI, 27% (25% acceptable). **Grid format "bets × phases × months":** per bet — rows for bet/phase/milestone (Pilot → MVP → Scale), resources written concretely ("2 BE, 1 FE, analyst 50%, designer outsourced", not "the dev team"), outcome metric baseline → goal with a revision threshold ("if <+10% after 3 months → correction"), output threshold + plan B ("CTR <1% → rework UX"; "<+3% CR → simplify, plan B"), dependencies/hiring with months ("3 dev: hire M2→M5"), a comment (bet type: quick win / big bet; overlap with other bets), and month columns 1–12 with 2-week strips marking active phases. Quick wins (~3 months, visible this quarter) and big bets (6–12 months, Discovery on thin resources until confirmation) coexist; shared resources split explicitly ("BE: 30% capacity, 70% on bet 2"). Hiring tracked as its own line (search → offer → onboarding per vacancy, by month); phases depending on hiring shift with it rather than "believing" in the ideal date. **Initiative contributions** to the target metrics are stated in percentages and sum ≈100%, making priorities measurable; partnership/BD/marketing tracks run alongside and must not fall out of the roadmap. **Seasonality rules:** (1) mass launches and effect measurement in the peak demand season; off-season for preparation, integrations and supply retention; (2) promo and growth mechanics live **before** the trade season starts (sellers need weeks to join); (3) each bet's card marks which season the outcome measurement falls in — no decisions on a "dead" season without adjustment. **Resources are part of the strategy:** with 2 developers and 1 designer you cannot run four strategies in parallel; hiring is a long chain (vacancy → search → interviews → offer → start → onboarding), guideline 3–6 months + 1–3 months onboarding — not an SLA; a resource needed in Q3 is decided in Q1. Mature approach: pilot on current resources, expansion after confirmation, parallel search; alternatives — cut scope, outsource, shift the phase; two extremes to avoid: "freeze everything until hiring" and "plan as if already hired". Dependencies (platform, technology, organizational, human) go into a registry: bet, dependency, team, approval status, risk, mitigation. Bets compete (short-term revenue vs retention vs infrastructure) and company context re-prioritizes them; choosing one bet = giving up another — the roadmap shows which is #1 and what's deferred.

**How:** (1) take bets with effect and effort estimates; (2) per bet set the outcome metric (baseline → goal → deadline), an "acceptable" level, and the closing threshold; (3) split into phases: choose the output proxy and minimal means for the pilot; define what counts as a qualitative signal; (4) set time-boxes and numeric gate criteria per phase, plus a stop signal and plan B; (5) build the dependencies registry and align priorities with owners; dependent teams go into the agreements table; (6) determine resources and hiring: who's here now, what's needed later, alternatives if hiring fails; fold hiring into timelines and the traction model; (7) prioritize and fix trade-offs: which bet is #1 and why, what's deferred and why, how company context changes the order; (8) lay out by quarters with "if it doesn't work" and "if the resource isn't hired" scenarios; (9) run the completeness checklist (bet outcome-linked; phases with per-phase metrics; time-boxes; dependencies aligned; numeric gates, stop signal, buffer, plan B; decision-maker named; deferrals shown with reasons; hiring and alternatives folded in; stakeholders aligned) and agree with stakeholders (DACI); set a review date.

**Pitfalls:** roadmap = feature list; no phases; no dependencies; output unlinked to outcome; no gates or stop conditions; endless testing ("we need more data") without a stop criterion; a "pilot" of three months with serious development; one outcome metric for all phases (months of waiting for feedback); "we'll find resources later" — a strategy requiring 20 developers who don't exist; a single region for a regional pilot; a pilot without a defined signal; a roadmap as promise rather than choice tool (no room for change; the right to fail requested after failure); de-prioritized work invisible and unexplained.
