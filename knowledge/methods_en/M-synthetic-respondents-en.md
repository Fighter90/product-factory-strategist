# Synthetic Respondents: Rules of Use (EN summary)

**When:** the team has an idea or a guide but no live respondents yet and needs cheap "draft" thinking: generate hypotheses, objections, wording, question traps — then verify everything on real people. The card protects against the main error: using invented AI respondents as proof of demand or prevalence.

**Core:** A synthetic respondent is a language model assigned a human role ("cat owner, 35…") answering the researcher's questions; its answer is generated text, not observation — it reflects training data, prompt wording and expectations embedded in the prompt, not the life of a real segment. Hard rules follow: (1) synthetic data is a source of ideas and a training simulator, not a source of evidence — it doesn't know your market and shows nothing about frequency; (2) any conclusion from synthetic data is a hypothesis with "unverified" status until confirmed on real people/data; (3) everything synthetic is labeled everywhere it's visible — text, table, quote, report; (4) course principle: check AI answers against your own data; for analyzing real interviews a model must cite uploaded files, not fantasize. Permitted (low risk): generating and expanding hypothesis lists about barriers, motives, objections (for later verification); piloting an interview/test guide (find unclear, leading, ambiguous questions; estimate timing — but a pilot with at least one real human is still mandatory); checking task wording ("how else could this be understood?"); devil's advocate: objections to the value proposition, client fears and alternatives; interviewer warm-up, practicing probes; draft personas/scenarios for a workshop — as inspiration, labeled. Prohibited: "we surveyed 100 AI respondents, 72% would buy" — shares, percentages, segment sizes, NPS/CSAT, willingness to pay, churn probability; replacing a usability test ("the model said the button is noticeable"); synthetic quotes in a report as the client's voice; investment or launch decisions based on synthetic data; validating your own hypothesis — models tend to agree and adapt to the implied answer.

**How:** (1) define the purpose: hypotheses / guide pilot / objections; if the purpose is "get the market's opinion" — stop and return to real data; (2) collect input facts about the segment from real sources (interviews, support, analytics, reviews), each with a source; anything known only by assumption is labeled; (3) write the persona specification — label "SYNTHETIC RESPONDENT — not a real person"; segment (who, where, category/product experience); situation/trigger; job (JTBD): functional, emotional, social; current alternatives; constraints (time, money, skills, device); known facts only, with references; what the persona doesn't know; answer style (short, conversational, with doubts); source per field (interview / analytics / assumption); invent no numbers or quotes, build no desired answer into the role; (4) prompt frame: the model is reminded it's a simulation; answers briefly, in first person, may say "I don't know / never used it"; need not agree; relies only on the given spec; (5) run several independent runs (different personas within the segment, different wording, repeats) — identical, too-rational, too-smooth answers signal an artifact, not segment unanimity; (6) ask for divergences: "what would change your decision", "what doesn't suit you here", "what are your alternatives", "what are you unsure about"; (7) register the output with the label [SYNTHETIC, unverified], model, date, prompt version; (8) convert to testable hypotheses in the format "If [segment] in situation [trigger], then [observable behavior/answer] at a share ≥ X% / at least N of M participants; checked by [method]; refuted if [criterion]" — the researcher sets X and N, not the model; choose a validation method on real people (in-depth interviews, usability testing, survey); (9) calibrate where real interviews exist: does the simulation reproduce known real answers; if not — lower trust and don't use it for that segment; (10) update statuses after validation (confirmed / refuted / needs refinement); synthetic sources stay archived as "a source of ideas", not "a source of facts". Registry row: ID | model claim | [SYNTHETIC] | model/date/prompt version | persona | why it might be false | testable hypothesis | validation method | threshold/refusal criterion | status. Output labeling is mandatory: at the top of the block "the following is a simulation result, not respondent data"; each quote marked [SYN]; summaries must not contain percentages or "clients say"; in the final document synthetic material is separated into its own section "Hypotheses to verify". Admissibility checklist: purpose is hypotheses/pilot/objections; input from real facts; labeling everywhere; nothing called "data" or "market research"; no prevalence numbers; a validation plan on humans exists; the decision doesn't rest on synthetic data alone.

**Pitfalls:** presenting an "AI respondent" share as a market share (no sample, no representativeness — percentages banned, qualitative hypotheses only); asking "would you like it?" (sycophancy and politeness — ask for objections, alternatives, refusal conditions); one persona, one run (smoothness ≠ segment opinion — several runs and divergences); a persona invented from the head without facts (stereotype amplification — base it on real interviews, mark assumptions); the desired answer leaking into the prompt (self-confirmation — neutral wording, red-teaming); mixing synthetic and real quotes in one table (provenance lost — separate columns/labels, source status); accepting synthetic output instead of a pilot (real misunderstandings missed — at least one real guide pilot); using it to evaluate an interface (the model doesn't see or use it like a human — heuristics + real test); using it as "the voice" on sensitive topics (health, finances — real respondents only).
