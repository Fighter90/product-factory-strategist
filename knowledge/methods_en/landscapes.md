# Adjacent landscapes — marketing, technology, business model and the functions of business

## When to apply
When a product growth strategy has to survive the check "does it hold together with the rest of the business?": can the product actually be built and delivered (technology, marketing, sales), does it make money (business model), and does the client need it at all. The map helps complement product bets with a picture of the internal environment, choose or revise the business model, and prepare the case for the investment committee.

## Core idea
**A product is a value-creation mechanism, but a business is a system of functions.** Value has to be delivered, monetized and repeated (retention/expansion). Functions:

| Function | Purpose | How it affects the product | What it gives the product |
|---|---|---|---|
| Product / Tech | feasibility, speed, quality, security, scaling | architecture, tech debt and platforms limit or accelerate; they set the cost of development and operational risks | data, experiments, stable delivery of value |
| Marketing | create demand and the right expectations, bring in the "right client" at an acceptable price | packaging: message, pricing tiers, segmentation | signals about the market, JTBD, comparison with competitors, channel quality |
| Sales | turn demand into contracts and predictable revenue | walks the client through a complex decision (why change, why us, why now) | signals about barriers, decision-maker requirements |
| Support / success | deliver the promised value, retain users | reaction to complaints, processes and playbooks | feedback, retention |
| Finance / operations | sustainability of the business | budget, unit economics | frames for decisions |

**Three lenses of innovation** — an initiative succeeds only if it is simultaneously: desirable for the user (desirability), technologically and operationally feasible (feasibility), and viable for the business (viability).

| Lens | Questions |
|---|---|
| Desirability | who is the main user, which segments get the maximum value; which need are we serving; what makes people come back |
| Feasibility | is there expertise and who are the key people; is capacity sufficient; which external systems do we depend on |
| Viability | who pays, for what and when; what are the fixed and variable costs and does the economics converge; which clients are the most profitable and are we attracting exactly those; why now |

**Marketing**: four areas — performance (measurable acquisition; CPA, CPC, ROAS; days–weeks), brand (awareness and trust; months–years), product marketing (positioning, launch), growth (experiments: referrals, SEO, A/B). Funnel: awareness → interest → consideration → conversion → retention → referral. Acquisition metrics: CPA = spend / target actions; CPC = spend / clicks; CPM = spend / (impressions / 1000); ROAS = ad revenue / ad spend; CAC = (marketing + sales) / new clients per period; K-factor = invites per user × invite conversion. Marketing is not advertising, but a system for building demand and meaning.

**Sales**: three questions — why the client should change anything (the risk of "doing nothing"), why with us (the value proposition and an ROI model tailored to the client), why now (the decision-maker, the budget cycle, approvals). Types: self-service (minutes, low CAC), inside sales (days/weeks), field sales (months, high CAC), channel sales (through partners). The translation "feature → benefit → outcome" (dashboard → visibility of the metrics → faster decisions).

**Tech**: roles — development, QA, analytics, design, technical lead; rules of engagement: discuss the "why", not the "how", respect estimates, involve engineers in discovery early, budget for tech debt, align roadmap and priorities.

**Business model** — the mechanism by which the company earns money: who pays, for what, and how the price is formed. It is not a business type (marketplace, SaaS — those are types). A large business is always a set of models. Choosing or changing the model can be the main growth driver.

| Model | When applicable | How it earns (formula, unit and period) | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Retail | physical goods, logistics | margin = selling price − purchase price, ₽/unit | M.Video, Magnit |
| Subscription | recurring value, recurring costs | revenue per period = subscribers × ARPU; MRR/ARR; condition LTV > CAC | Yandex Plus, SaaS |
| Freemium | low marginal cost, scale is needed, product-led | revenue = free users × conversion to paying × ARPU of paying users | VK Music, Telegram Premium |
| Pay-per-use | high variability of consumption, high variable cost | revenue = price per unit × volume of consumption | clouds, APIs, carsharing |
| Advertising | large traffic and engagement | CPM/CPC/CPA: revenue = impressions × CPM / 1000 × fill rate | search engines, social networks |
| Commission | intermediation, expensive transaction | revenue = GMV × take rate | realtors, brokers, taxi aggregators |
| Revenue share | the service is delivered by a partner, the platform brings the client | platform revenue = total revenue × platform share | app stores, financial products from partners |

Others: white label, licensing, franchise, "razor and blades", data monetization, retainer. A classifieds business is asset-light (it does not own physical goods) and uses almost every model except retail; classifieds have two basic schemes — payment for listing and payment for outcome (a buyer/a transaction). Signals for revisiting the model (from the lecture notes): falling LTV/CAC, rising churn with a stable product, a competitor growing faster with a different model, an uncovered segment appearing, an opportunity to control the transaction appearing (a commission can be added), "free" users creating costs.

**Subscription — metrics and the main risk.** MRR decomposes into components: New + Expansion − Churned − Contraction (it shows the source of growth: acquisition or expansion among existing customers); alongside it: ARR, Churn, LTV, CAC, ARPU, Payback. Subscription types: fixed, tiered, usage-based, all-you-can-eat. The main risk is churn: at 5% monthly churn more than half of the base is lost within a year, so a subscription is justified only when the need is long-term and the value recurs.

**Freemium — free-to-paid conversion is the key multiplier.** Benchmarks: SaaS ~2–5%, games ~1–5%, highly engaging products ~10–15%. Freemium is the main instrument of product-led growth: the free tier must create value on its own (collection, network), while the paid tier removes a constraint. Risk: a generous free tier "feeds" users who will never pay but still create costs.

**Commission model — the economics in one chain** (the numbers are illustrative): `active sellers (10,000) × transactions per seller/month (5) × average order value (3,000 ₽) = 50,000 transactions/month`; `GMV = transactions × order value = 150M ₽/month`; `revenue = GMV × take rate (8%) = 12M ₽/month`; `minus COGS per transaction (payments + delivery) = gross profit`; the margin shows what to grow through: raise the take rate (the risk of sellers leaving and disintermediation), reduce COGS (logistics, payments) or raise the order value (recommendations, cross-sell). Marketplace liquidity = `completed deals / published listings` — the basic metric of "does the market converge". Two structural risks: **disintermediation** (the parties agree directly, bypassing the platform — the commission bypasses it too) and **chicken-and-egg** (buyers need sellers, sellers need buyers; launching a two-sided market requires concentrating on one side). Comparison with revenue share: commission is a % of the transaction (one side pays, the platform is an intermediary, benchmark 5–30% of GMV); revenue share is a % of total revenue (both sides are partners, benchmark 30–70%, examples: App Store 70/30, video platforms 55/45). The take rate grows with the added value of the deal: classifieds ~3–10%, e-commerce marketplaces ~10–25% (logistics, fulfillment), services ~10–20% (verification, guarantees), delivery and taxi ~20–35% (logistics, payments, traffic), hotels ~15–25% (global traffic), app distribution ~15–30% (monopoly on the channel).

**Investment committee** — a formalized "internal pitch": resources are always limited and there are more ideas than hands. Structure of the defense: the problem (for whom and how big), the solution, metrics and deadline, resources, risks, alternatives (including "what happens if we don't do it" and a cheaper option). Prioritization: ICE, RICE (Reach × Impact × Confidence / Effort), MoSCoW, the Value/Effort matrix.

## Procedure
1. **Run the chosen bet through the three lenses**; answer every lens question with a data type attached (fact / assumption / gap).
2. **Describe the current and the target business model**: who pays, for what, the revenue formula, unit and period, unit economics (`M-unit-economics`). If the bet changes the model (for example, from classifieds to transaction), assess the impact on each side of the market and write out the risks.
3. **Check model compatibility** (subscription and advertising can conflict, freemium and commission require care): do the revenue sources cannibalize each other.
4. **Describe the marketing landscape**: channels, CAC per channel, the role of brand, dependence on paid traffic, season windows.
5. **Describe the technology landscape**: platforms and data to lean on; dependencies on other teams; tech debt; constraints on the speed of testing hypotheses.
6. **Describe the business landscape**: legal and financial frames, partners, the role of sales and support.
7. **Assemble the conclusion**: which functions become the bottleneck of the bet and what has to be done before the start.
8. **Prepare the committee pitch** along six sections and prioritize the portfolio (ICE/RICE or Value/Effort), stating what is being postponed.

- [ ] The bet passes the three lenses; the weakest lens is named
- [ ] The business model is described by a formula with units and a period
- [ ] It is shown who pays and when
- [ ] Dependencies on Tech, marketing, sales and support are listed with owners
- [ ] The alternatives include "do nothing" and a cheaper check

## Typical mistakes
- "The product is everything": ignoring that the business rests on the cost structure, resources, partners, distribution and the monetization model.
- Marketing is treated as advertising; sales as "a department that makes calls".
- One model for everything: the business model does not match client behavior.
- A skipped lens: desirable but infeasible or unprofitable; feasible but wanted by no one.
- No answer to "why now".
- Planning by development estimates without buffer and without dependencies.
- A pitch without alternatives and risks.

## Questions that can change the decision
- If the bet changes the business model, are we losing a revenue source that feeds the current business?
- Which lens is the weakest, and can it be strengthened with a cheap check?
- Does a competitor have a different business model that gives it a structural advantage (costs, distribution)?
- Which functions (lawyers, support, moderation) cannot be plugged in quickly, and how much would that shift the timeline?
- Who pays and when: does the moment of payment coincide with the moment of receiving value?
- What happens if the committee says no: what does the minimal option look like?
