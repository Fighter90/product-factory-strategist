# Product Context: Role, Cycle, Perimeter, Strategic Slice (EN summary)

**When:** at the start of any task ("our metric fell", "we need a strategy", "what to do with the product"): before advising, fix where the product is now — stage, audience, market, goals, metrics, constraints, resources, responsibility perimeter. The card sets the minimal input set and the rule "strategy = stage × context".

**Core:** The PM is the link between strategy/business goals, technology and user problems: defines strategy, formulates value, works at the junction of business, UX and technology. Two model modes: "product leader" (large companies — manages the process, not execution: "I don't do it, but I'm responsible") and "generalist executor" (startups — analytics, prototypes, copy, launch personally). Product ≠ project manager: the PM owns the product lifecycle and value (retention, conversion, LTV, ARPU, NPS, DAU/MAU, hypothesis delivery); the PMg owns deadlines, budget, resources, scope (on-time delivery, budget adherence). PM types with different accents: B2C (funnels, UX, retention), B2B (customization, long sales cycles, communication), monetization (multi-monetization, business models), DS/ML (uncertainty, model metrics), platform/SaaS (APIs, scale, internal clients). Org structures shape who really decides: product-team leader (clear responsibility, isolation risk), cross-functional team (speed, role conflicts), matrix (resource flexibility, dual subordination, slow decisions). Lifecycle: IDEA → DISCOVERY (only hypotheses, many interviews) → MVP (minimal product, testing core value and risky hypotheses) → GROWTH/SCALE (value confirmed; users and revenue grow; focus on unit economics, channels, automation) → MAINTENANCE (growth slowed; stability, retention, cost reduction) → DECLINE (churn, niche burnout, competitor replacement). Pivot — a deliberate change of a key hypothesis while keeping assets (team, technology, audience): changes one or more of audience, problem/value, monetization, functionality; based on metrics and feedback, not "a restart from scratch"; examples: Burbn → Instagram (kept only the photos people actually used), Tiny Speck → Slack (internal tool became the product), snowboard shop → Shopify (built own CMS — which was the product), video dating "Tune In, Hook Up" → YouTube (users uploaded ordinary video; the team didn't interfere). Closure is a deliberate exit, not failure: hypothesis unconfirmed and prospects murky, market changed and value obsolete, no growth trajectory; saying "stop" is a PM skill (Google Stadia — no critical mass, Apple Newton — technology ahead of its time, Amazon Dash Buttons — smartphones replaced them, ICQ — format obsolescence after ~30 years). Double Diamond (Discover → Define → Develop → Deliver) and HADI cycles (Hypothesis, Action, Data, Insights) are thinking frames, not instructions: a framework is a simplified model that keeps structure when drowning in uncertainty; product thinking = common sense + structure + critical reading of patterns; always check four things — context (what's happening with product, team, market), adequacy (are conclusions justified and actions feasible), meaning (why this step, what goal), responsibility (you can follow a template and still ruin the product). Perimeter can be: one screen/feature; the whole funnel or part of it; a module inside an ecosystem (e.g. ranking in search); a separate direction. Responsibility ≠ control: the PM influences but doesn't command all parts; in ~98% of cases the product depends on other teams. Perimeter artifacts: base context (goal, segment, problems), stakeholder map and decision matrix (DACI), dependency list (teams, APIs, backends), target metrics, CJM, impact map / value stream. Base context — the minimal input set: stage (Idea / Discovery / MVP / Growth / Scale / Maintenance / Decline); audience and roles (user / buyer / decision maker; buyer / seller; courier/driver/partner; admin/moderator); market and segment — including "non-competitors" (habit, Excel, "doing nothing", the user's time); period goals; key metrics (NSM, growth, LTV, retention); constraints (technical, resource, legal); resources (team, budget, infrastructure); main hypotheses the development rests on (marked as hypotheses). Stage detection via four signals: PMF, usage regularity, unit economics, base growth speed and feedback. Metric-to-stage mapping: Task Success Rate (does the user solve their task — Discovery/MVP); activation rate (value understood — MVP); retention D1/D7/D30 (return exists — MVP→Growth); DAU/MAU stickiness (habit, frequency — Growth/Scale); NPS/CSAT (loyalty — Growth/Maintenance); LTV/CAC, ROMI (economics sustainability, marketing efficiency — Growth/Scale); ARPU/ARPPU, GMV/revenue (monetization scale — Scale); churn (retention problems — Growth/Maintenance); organic vs paid share (paid-traffic dependency — MVP/Growth); bugs/release speed (development maturity — MVP/Maintenance). Reading examples (teaching cases): D30 22%, DAU/MAU 35%, LTV/CAC 2.5, NPS 52, MRR growing, churn 3%, releases every 2 weeks → Growth (value exists, economics on the edge — LTV/CAC < 3 by the common guideline, not yet Scale). D7 6%, activation 25%, DAU/MAU 5%, CAC high and rising, organic ≈ 0, NPS 15, no revenue → MVP/Discovery without PMF; scaling premature. D30 40%, NPS 60, revenue stable and slowly growing, roadmap about resilience and tech debt, MAU almost flat → Maintenance. Market context: competitors ≠ analogs — the user compares with habit ("Excel", "doing nothing") and competes for time (TikTok, YouTube). TAM/SAM/SOM: counting "all iPhone owners" as the market is a classic error; SOM is the starting fight; TAM without go-to-market is an investor number. SWOT is not a checkbox ("the product is convenient") — tie it to market, stage and risks; BCG matrix for product portfolios ("question marks" — grow or kill; "cows" not forever; "stars" need investment); Porter's five forces for B2B/SaaS/platforms (competitors, entrants, substitutes, suppliers, buyers). Strategy = Stage × Context; also Strategy = [Goals] + [Opportunities] − [Constraints]. Strategy functions: perspective, team synchronization, focus (against the "feature factory"). A good strategy leans on strengths, is a set of sequential choices, is aligned and logical. Models: DHM (Gibson Biddle: Delight, Hard-to-copy, Margin), ecosystem strategy, Blue Ocean (differentiation and cost reduction simultaneously). Stage × market focus: MVP on an empty market — validate value, collect signals; MVP on an occupied market — niche, early users, unique USP; Growth on a growing market — retention, channel scaling, unit economics; Growth on a competitive market — advantages, retention, NPS, positioning; Scale on a stable market — process optimization, LTV growth, new segments; Decline on a shrinking market — sunset plan, resource transfer, niche search; Decline on a transitional market — pivot (audience, monetization model, channel).

**How:** (1) write down what is known per the base-context table; mark each cell as fact (source + date + period), hypothesis or forecast; empty cells go to the question list; (2) define the perimeter: what the product controls, what it only influences, which teams it depends on; list dependencies and decision owners; (3) determine the stage by ≥ 3 independent signals (retention, regularity, unit economics, base dynamics); if signals contradict, say so explicitly and name the data that would resolve it; (4) describe the market context: direct competitors, substitutes, habit/"do nothing", time competitors; rough TAM/SAM/SOM; market type (empty / occupied / growing / competitive / stable / shrinking / transitional); (5) formulate the strategic focus from the stage × market pair and list constraints; (6) extract 3–5 key hypotheses the product's development rests on, each with a validation plan (hand over to problem-design and solution-design); (7) explicitly record what is NOT resolved and which inputs would change the stage/focus.

**Pitfalls:** determining the stage "by feel" or by company size instead of metrics (retention/activation/unit economics); one signal instead of a set — GMV growth with falling retention masks absence of PMF; counting "all potential users" (TAM) as the market without linking it to channels and monetization; SWOT untied to stage and market; strategy as a feature list ("feature factory"); confusing responsibility with control — promising a metric managed by other teams; applying a framework (Double Diamond, HADI) as ritual without checking whether conclusions fit the context; ignoring the close/pivot option — every product strategy must state under what conditions it is discussed.
