# Roadmap (EN summary)

**When:** turning strategy and a set of hypotheses into a time plan: what, in which order, who, with which metrics and decision points; also assessing someone else's roadmap for feasibility, dependencies and measurability. Here a roadmap is a strategic document, not a feature list.

**Core:** The roadmap aligns the business vision with tactical steps, synchronizes participants, structures key stages. Two main areas: (1) product and technical development — where P&T resources are invested and what effect is expected; (2) synchronization of interdependencies with other teams (marketing, sales, adjacent products). Value: transparency and fewer delays; prioritization and communication; flexibility under market changes; performance management (risk minimization, resource reallocation). Elements: (1) vision and goals; (2) key initiatives/stages; (3) milestones; (4) task decomposition; (5) owners and resources; (6) timing (quarters, months, sprints); (7) links and dependencies; (8) success metrics / expected result. Types: thematic (by directions; high uncertainty), stage/time-based (by dates; classic IT), specific (UX/UI, mobile, integrations), cross-functional (several departments), technical (infrastructure, tech debt). Choice depends on stage (launch — flexibility and fast iterations; maturity — optimization and support), business model (B2B — more detailed and longer; B2C — more flexible) and product type (traditional — more linear; technological — frequent revisions). Detailing by stage: idea — high abstraction; development — detail and dependencies; pilot — testing tasks and fast fixes; scaling — resource planning, calendar, inter-team alignment. Frameworks: Agile roadmapping, OKR, Lean roadmapping, Impact Mapping, Business Model Canvas. OKR: Objective — an inspiring direction, not a metric, ambitious (~50% probability of achievement); Key Result — measurable (SMART), not a feature or task, shows whether we move toward the goal. Chain: long-term strategy → annual goals → quarterly OKRs → cross-unit synchronization; lecture rule of thumb "0.9 of the annual goal = 0.7 of the OKR"; goals transparent to all, achieved jointly. Outcome-based (problem) roadmap: rows are problems/outcomes, not features — problem, symptom metric (baseline), status (Discovery / In progress / Done), planned release, owner, links to dashboard and interviews; hypothesis confirmed → status changes; not confirmed → hypothesis revised. 12-month scaling roadmap: "months × stages" table with quarterly milestones (Discovery → MVP → PMF proof → Gate "start scaling" → final review); a cell holds hypothesis → validation method → expected metric/threshold; a row holds five fields: stage, hypothesis/goal, tasks, metric + threshold, owner + date.

**How:** (1) goal: write down vision/mission and 1–3 period goals (OKR); every KR has baseline, threshold and deadline; (2) inputs: collect ideas and requirements from product, development, design, analytics, support, marketing and external bottlenecks (legal, moderation, DS, infrastructure); each initiative traces to a problem or hypothesis; (3) resources: estimate each team's capacity (person-weeks, available days per quarter, holidays) and historical velocity; effort estimates come from development and QA, not the PM; (4) prioritization: coarse MoSCoW (Must/Should/Could/Won't), then RICE; Must ≤ ~40% of real volume for 1–2 sprints; cut explicitly ("No" is a PM tool) — record what we don't do and why; (5) structure: choose the roadmap type; plan 1–2 quarters in detail, beyond that — big themes; each initiative: hypothesis, validation method, metric + threshold, owner, date, dependencies (incl. external with ETA), risks and mitigation; (6) decision points: for every milestone record which decision is made (go / rework / stop), by which criterion, who decides — this distinguishes a roadmap from a task list; (7) buffers and critical path: reserve for force majeure and Discovery insights (guideline: 10–15% of time and 1–2 sprints for risks); highlight the critical path and peak role load (QA, backend); (8) alignment: discuss with executors and key stakeholders (buy-in); keep a "public" and an "internal" (with risks) version; one master roadmap; (9) revision rhythm: review monthly/quarterly; after a quarter — what worked, what didn't, why slips; change is normal, not failure. Quality rubric (7 criteria): logic and realism of stages (2 points — no unexplained pauses; dates survive capacity); clear hypothesis-to-stage binding (2 — it's clear which hypothesis is checked when; a problem hypothesis isn't validated in parallel with a solution for an unconfirmed problem without reason); dependencies and critical path (2: 0 — none stated; 1 — 1–2 without the full picture; 2 — key intra- and inter-team dependencies, critical path, external factors); roles, teams, alignment points (1 — concrete roles, not "development" in general; at forks it's visible who participates — marketing, support); risks and measures (1 — 2–3 key risks (product, team, external), blocking/non-blocking, realistic mitigation); metrics and checkpoints bound to decisions (1 — each milestone has a metric and the decision taken on it); presentation quality (1 — readable, structured, legend, not overloaded). Also check: are specific teams overloaded in the same weeks; is there idle time.

**Pitfalls:** no strategy link (a "wishlist"); unrealistic timing (10 releases per quarter for 3 people); capacity and seasonality ignored; detail overload (hundreds of tasks, quickly stale — use abstraction levels: initiatives for leadership, tasks for the team); unaccounted dependencies and blockers; no buffers; no metrics and decisions at milestones (the classic point loss in final-work reviews); desynchronized team roadmaps; a roadmap "born in the PM's head" without buy-in; no flexibility — frozen, revised only in crisis; launching "everything at once" without phasing and without a user entry point/channel; validating a solution hypothesis in parallel for a not-yet-confirmed problem.
