# Target Audience Research: Segmentation, Personas, Recruiting (EN summary)

**When:** answering "who is the product for, which segment do we target first, how do we describe these people so design, USP and communication hit a concrete person, and how do we find respondents" — the base before formulating the USP and before any field research.

**Core:** Target audience = the group of people or companies the product is intended for; "everyone" is never a TA — even mass products split into niches by barriers, constraints, values. Types: primary and indirect (who decides/buys vs who influences), wide and narrow, B2B and B2C. Segmentation = grouping homogeneous clients by a shared basis; two stages — qualitative (interviews, focus groups: traits and cluster hypotheses) and quantitative (survey or behavioral data: sizes and differences). Segmentation bases: demography (weak alone, always combine with others); cost (Basic vs Premium, average check, company size — good for B2B, weak B2C); behavior (purchase frequency, feature engagement, loyalty — good for both); attitude (psychographics, lifestyle, risk appetite — closer to B2C); needs (JTBD, benefit sought, emotional state — the strongest and hardest; survey + factor and cluster analysis). A good segment is large enough, reachable, homogeneous inside, clearly different from others (no full overlap); additionally check stability over time and fit with strategy/resources. Pipeline portrait → segment → persona: portrait = first description after desk research and internal data (socio-demographics, psychographics, goals, behavior, product specifics; white spots are normal); segment = refined after both stages (with size and distinguishing traits); persona = key-segment portrait completed with quantitative characteristics, interaction scenarios and quotes — a working tool for design, USP and communication, not a fact about the market: validate with a pilot campaign or MVP and revise as the market moves. Evidence strength: a portrait from 5–10 interviews plus analytics is a segment hypothesis; segment sizes, shares and differences are proven only by the quantitative stage.

Recruiting drives reliability (course estimate: ~70% of TA research success). Profile from the portrait with mandatory and exclusion criteria, agreed with the product. Screener ≤5 min (longer → drop-off): kill-filter question, behavioral block (frequency, scenario, time/money spent), motivation/barrier question, control trap ("choose option 3"), contact + recording consent; break on a key "no"; test on colleagues. At least two independent channels: client base (CRM extracts, sales managers), site/service intercepts, socials and communities, agencies and online panels, snowball (relevance over availability for rare and B2B profiles). Exclude marketing/media professionals, recent participants of similar studies, people connected to the team or brand. Incentives (money, gifts, discounts, early access) proportionate to time and complexity, terms fixed in advance; personal data per privacy law (consent, minimization, retention).

**How:** (1) analyze market and competitors (trends, products, positioning, own position); (2) gather internal data (product analytics, UX research, support tickets, store reviews, past desk research, colleagues' knowledge, own user experience) into a "source → fact → tag (pain, motive, barrier, trigger)" table; (3) draft portraits (socio-demographics, psychographics, goals and behavior, specifics) marking gaps and hypotheses; (4) check you got several portraits — one or two means the basis is too generic; (5) choose one main basis (behavior or needs) plus auxiliaries; if unsure what segments exist, start qualitative; (6) qualitative stage in 5 steps: goal and basis → interview/focus-group guide → recruiting TA representatives → field → refine or form segments; output: refined portraits and difference hypotheses; (7) quantitative stage: method per basis (demography — survey + descriptive; cost — analytics + reports; behavior — analytics + survey; attitude/needs — survey + statistics) and sample size: ~385 answers for a ±5 pp share estimate at 95% (rare segments need wider screening reach); (8) build the segment matrix: columns = segments, rows = size (% of buyers), behavior/channel choice, socio-demographic profile, attitude, willingness to pay, barriers, key needs; (9) select key segments by five criteria: potential market size, profit capacity (buying power), accessibility, in-segment competition, compatibility with goals and resources — explicitly record who you don't take and why; (10) write personas for key segments: profile, motivation, top-5 choice criteria, 3–4 characteristic quotes, drivers and barriers, purchase scenario, willingness to pay, expectations; (11) validate in reality (pilot campaign, MVP, ads): watch behavior, metrics, feedback; adjust; (12) link to the product via the Value Proposition Canvas left side (Jobs-to-be-done, Gains, Pains per segment).

**Pitfalls:** "TA is everyone" or one wide portrait; segmenting only by age and gender; overlapping segments, ten or more, or key segments chosen without business rationale; jumping to the quantitative stage without the qualitative one (measuring segments that don't exist); fictional personas — pretty cards without data and without "hypothesis/fact" labels; recruiting from friends or only the loyal base (bias toward friendly, experienced users); computing segment sizes from interviews ("3 of 8 means 40%"); no post-segmentation testing and revision (segments drift); ignoring the indirect TA (who pays/influences but doesn't use); AI personas and synthetic respondents presented as segment data — hypothesis sources only, never segment size or demand.
