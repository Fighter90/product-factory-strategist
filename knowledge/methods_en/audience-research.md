# Target audience research, segmentation, personas and recruiting

## When to apply
You need to answer: "who are we building the product for, which segment do we target first, how do we describe these people so that design, the USP and communication hit a concrete person, and how do we find respondents for research". The map is the foundation before formulating the USP ([[M-usp]]) and before launching any field research.

## Core idea
**Target audience (TA)** — the group of people or companies a product is intended for. "Everyone" is never a TA: even the most mass-market products split into niches by barriers, constraints and values. A mistake in understanding the TA produces "features spent for nothing", weak adoption and poor retention. TA knowledge is needed in order to: build the product around real pains; focus marketing and the USP; make decisions on data; discover new segments and niches.

**Types of TA.** Primary and indirect (those who decide on the purchase and those who influence the decision); broad and narrow; B2B (organizations, which have different needs and different roles in the decision) and B2C.

**Segmentation** — grouping homogeneous clients by a shared basis; it lets you evaluate segments and pick the key ones. It consists of a qualitative stage (interviews, focus groups: we form the traits and the hypotheses about clusters) and a quantitative one (a survey or behavioral data: we estimate the sizes and the differences).

**Segmentation bases.**
| Basis | Example traits | Assessment from the course | How to measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Demographics | age, gender, life stage, social group | a weak stand-alone basis; always look at it together with others | simple surveys, desk research |
| Cost | Basic vs Premium, average check, turnover, company size | good for B2B, weak for B2C | own analytics, industry data |
| Behavior | purchase frequency, engagement with features, loyalty | good for both B2B and B2C | analytics, surveys, statistical methods |
| Attitude | psychographics, lifestyle, interests, risk appetite | like behavior, closer to B2C | surveys, statistical methods |
| Needs | the benefit sought from the product, choice factors, emotional state (JTBD) | the strongest and the hardest | surveys + statistics (factor and cluster analysis) |
Simple bases (demographics, basic behavior) are covered by analytics and short surveys; complex ones (attitude, needs) require a well-designed questionnaire and statistics.

**A good segment:** large enough, reachable (it can be found and reached), homogeneous inside, different from others in its set of traits (no full overlap); it is additionally useful to check its stability over time and its fit with strategy/resources.

**TA portrait → segment → persona.** The *portrait* is the first description after desk research and data about clients (socio-demographics, psychographics, goals, behavior, product specifics); white spots are normal at this point. The *segment* is the refined portrait after the qualitative and quantitative stages (with a size and distinguishing traits). The *persona* is the portrait of the key segment completed with quantitative characteristics, interaction scenarios and quotes; it helps address a concrete person when designing the interface, the USP and communications.

**Evidence strength.** A portrait built from 5–10 interviews plus analytics is a *hypothesis about segments*. The segment size, the shares and the differences in traits are proven only by the quantitative stage. A persona is a working tool, not a fact about the market: it must be validated with a pilot campaign or an MVP on the chosen segment, and it must be revised as the market moves.

## Procedure
1. **Analyze the market and the competitors**: trends, products, positioning, your own position ([[M-research-types]], desk research and benchmark).
2. **Gather data about the product and the clients**: product analytics, UX research results, support tickets, reviews in app stores and on other platforms, past desk research, colleagues' knowledge (sales, support), your own user experience. Put it into a "source → fact → tag (pain, motive, barrier, trigger)" table.
3. **Draft the portrait**: socio-demographics, psychographics (values, lifestyle, pains, motivation), goals and typical behavior, specifics (region, device, payment method). Mark the white spots and the hypotheses.
4. **Check that there are several portraits**: if you got 1–2, the basis for splitting is too generic.
5. **Choose the goal and the segmentation basis.** One main basis (behavior or needs) + auxiliary ones (demographics). If you are not sure which segments exist at all, start with the qualitative stage.
6. **Qualitative stage** (5 steps): goal and basis → interview/focus-group guide ([[M-in-depth-interviews]]) → recruiting TA representatives → running the sessions → refining or forming segments ([[M-interview-analysis]]). The result — refined portraits and hypotheses about the differences.
7. **Quantitative stage**: pick the method per basis (demographics — survey and descriptive statistics; cost — analytics and reports; behavior — analytics and surveys; attitude and needs — surveys and statistics) and compute the sample ([[M-quant-methods]]). To estimate a segment's share with ±5 pp at 95%, ~385 answers are enough (if the segment is rare, you need a wider screening reach). The result — segment sizes and the main differentiating factors.
8. **Consolidate into a segment matrix**: columns — segments; rows — size (% of all buyers), behavior/channel choice, socio-demographic profile, attitude, willingness to pay, barriers, key needs. The set of rows depends on the basis, the CJM stages and the funnel.
9. **Pick the key segments** by five parameters: the size of the potential market (the number of clients); the ability to generate profit (buying power); accessibility (can we work with it efficiently); the degree of competition inside the segment; compatibility with goals and resources. Explicitly write down whom we do *not* take and why.
10. **Describe the personas** for the key segments: demographic profile, motivation, the top choice criteria, attitude to the category (quotes), drivers and barriers towards your product, functional needs.
11. **Validate in reality**: a pilot campaign, an MVP or advertising for the segment; watch behavior, metrics, feedback; adjust the segments.
12. **Link to the product:** the left side of the Value Proposition Canvas — Jobs-to-be-done, Gains, Pains per segment (see [[M-usp]], [[M-jtbd]]).

## Respondent recruiting
Recruiting quality is the main factor of reliability (in the course: "about 70% of the success of TA research").
1. Define the profile (from the portrait, agreed with the product): mandatory and excluding traits.
2. The screener — a short filtering questionnaire with control questions and "traps" (to filter out the inattentive and the dishonest); break on a key "no"; test it on colleagues.
3. Find respondents (mix at least two independent channels): the client base (extracts by criteria, sales managers and key account managers); the site/service (embedded surveys, invitation banners); social media, communities, thematic channels; recruiting agencies and online platforms; personal connections and "snowball" (relevance matters more than availability; for rare and B2B profiles).
4. Selection and confirmation: screener analysis, a call/letter about the time and the format, written confirmation.
5. Exclude: marketing and media professionals, recent participants of similar studies, people connected to the team or the brand.
6. Motivation (money, gifts, discounts, early access): the amount is proportionate to the time and the complexity, the terms are fixed in advance.
7. Handle data about people according to personal-data protection rules (in Russia — Federal Law No. 152-FZ): consent, minimization, retention period.

| Behavior and channels (where they search, what they compare with, whose opinion they listen to) | | |
| Role in the decision (buyer / influencer / user) | | |

**Persona of a key segment:** the segment's name-designation; size (%) and source; a short profile; the top-5 choice criteria; 3–4 characteristic quotes; drivers and barriers towards your product; the search/purchase scenario; willingness to pay; what they expect from us.

**Mini-checklist before "going into the field":** the segmentation goal and the success metric are written down; one main basis is chosen; the guide covers pains, motivation, barriers, triggers; there is protection against biases (a screener, mixed channels); the questionnaire for the quant stage is ready; the criteria for selecting the key segments are agreed.

**Example segments from the course (renovation, by needs):** undemanding "DIYers"; delegating specialists (do not understand it themselves, ready to overpay for support); those looking for simple solutions (minimal money, inexpensive materials); perfectionist controllers (experienced, pay for the perfect result and for control of the process). It is visible here: demographics does not distinguish these groups, needs do.

## Typical mistakes
- "The TA is everyone" or one broad portrait; splitting only by age and gender.
- Segments overlap, there are too many of them (ten or more), or the key segments are chosen without a business rationale.
- Jumping to the quantitative stage without the qualitative one: we measure segments that do not exist.
- A made-up persona: a pretty card without data and without a "hypothesis/fact" label.
- Recruiting "from friends" or only from the loyal base: a bias toward friendly and experienced users.
- Segment sizes computed from interviews ("3 of 8 — so 40%").
- No testing after segmentation (a pilot campaign) and no revision: segments drift over time.
- Ignoring the indirect TA (who pays/influences but does not use).
- AI "personas" and synthetic respondents ([[M-synthetic-respondents]]) presented as segment data: they are suitable only as a source of hypotheses to check, and they never estimate segment size or demand.

## Questions that can change the decision
- Who makes the purchase decision, who pays, who uses it: one person or different people?
- Which segmentation basis is best connected with a difference in the product's *behavior* (rather than with convenience of description)?
- Is there data about clients inside the company (CRM, analytics), so that part of the work can be done without the field?
- How rare is the required respondent profile, and how long will recruiting take?
- What happens to the product/communication if we choose segment A rather than B (which decision depends on the segmentation)?
- Can the chosen segment be validated quickly with a pilot or advertising?
