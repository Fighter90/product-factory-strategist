# Van Westendorp PSM: the price through the customer's eyes

## When to apply
You are planning the price of a new product or a revision of the price of an existing one; the category is familiar to buyers and there are price reference points; you need to quickly outline the frame of acceptable prices before expensive price experiments. It works for understandable B2C and B2B products; it works worse for radical novelties without analogues.

## Essence
- **4 questions to every respondent** about one product/plan: (1) at which price does it seem **too cheap** to you — you doubt the quality; (2) at which price is it **a bargain/cheap**, but acceptable ("a good deal"); (3) at which price is it **expensive**, but you would still consider the purchase; (4) at which price is it **so expensive** that you will not buy.
- From the answers, **4 cumulative curves** are built (the share of respondents who gave an answer at or below the price) in "price → %" coordinates.
- **OPP (Optimal Price Point)** — the intersection of the "too cheap" and "too expensive" curves: the point of minimal purchase resistance.
- **IPP (Indifference Price Point)** — the intersection of "bargain" and "expensive": the people who consider the price a bargain are as numerous as those who consider it expensive.
- **PMC (Point of Marginal Cheapness)** — the intersection of "too cheap" × "expensive"; **PME (Point of Marginal Expensiveness)** — the intersection of "bargain" × "too expensive".
- **RAI (Range of Acceptable Prices)** — the range between PMC and PME: the prices at which the majority of target buyers accept the product rather than reject it as "too cheap/expensive".
- The method shows the **perceived frame of the price, not the final price**: the final price must still pass the unit economics (margin, LTV/CAC) and be confirmed by real conversion tests.

## How to do it
1. Assemble a sample of target buyers of the category (typically 100–300+; necessarily by segments) and describe the product exactly the way it will be sold.
2. Ask the 4 questions separately for each audience segment and each plan/package (different plans have different frames).
3. Build the 4 curves, find the intersections OPP, IPP, PMC, PME and the RAI range.
4. Overlay the unit economics: the minimally margin-positive price against the lower bound of the RAI — if the economics do not fit into the range, the problem is not in the price but in the model/positioning.
5. Confirm the price with a pilot or an experiment (an A/B test on price, a fake door): Van Westendorp gives the hypothesis, conversion gives the verdict.
6. Repeat when the positioning, the category or the market shifts noticeably.

## Pitfalls
- Hypotheticality: nobody pays real money, so the estimates are biased toward the "average" price of the category — without real tests, do not accept the result.
- A radical novelty without price reference points yields noise: the respondents "pull" the price from neighboring categories; you need the context of use and analogies.
- A sample that is not from the target audience devalues all the points: segment the survey and analyze the points by segment, not on average.
- OPP/IPP are not "the correct price": the decision is set inside the RAI and is aligned with the positioning (`M-usp`) and the unit economics, otherwise you will get a price "loved by the respondents but loss-making".
