# Van Westendorp PSM: the price through the client's eyes

## When to apply
You are planning the price of a new product or a revision of the price of an existing one; the category is familiar to buyers and there are price anchors; you need to quickly outline the frame of acceptable prices before running expensive pricing experiments. Works for understandable B2C and B2B products; it works worse for radical novelties without analogies.

## Core idea
- **4 questions to every respondent** about one product/tier: (1) at which price does it feel **too cheap** — you start doubting the quality; (2) at which price is it **a bargain/good value** but acceptable ("a good deal"); (3) at which price is it **expensive**, but you would still consider buying; (4) at which price is it **so expensive** that you won't buy.
- From the answers, **4 cumulative curves** are plotted (the share of respondents whose answer is at or below the price) in "price → %" coordinates.
- **OPP (Optimal Price Point)** — the intersection of the "too cheap" and "too expensive" curves: the point of minimal resistance to purchase.
- **IPP (Indifference Price Point)** — the intersection of "bargain" and "expensive": the number of people who consider the price a bargain equals the number who consider it expensive.
- **PMC (Point of Marginal Cheapness)** — the intersection of "too cheap" × "expensive"; **PME (Point of Marginal Expensiveness)** — the intersection of "bargain" × "too expensive".
- **RAI (Range of Acceptable Prices)** — the range between PMC and PME: prices at which most target buyers accept the product rather than reject it as "too cheap/expensive".
- The method shows the **perceived price frame, not the final price**: the final price must still pass unit economics (margin, LTV/CAC) and be confirmed by real conversion tests.

## How to do it
1. Recruit a sample of target buyers of the category (typically 100–300+; always broken down by segments) and describe the product exactly the way it will be sold.
2. Ask the 4 questions separately for each audience segment and each tier/package (different tiers have different frames).
3. Plot the 4 curves, find the intersections OPP, IPP, PMC, PME and the RAI range.
4. Overlay the unit economics: the minimum marginally profitable price against the lower RAI bound — if the economics don't fit into the range, the problem is not the price but the model/positioning.
5. Confirm the price with a pilot or an experiment (a price A/B, a fake door): Van Westendorp gives the hypothesis, conversion gives the verdict.
6. Repeat when the positioning or the category changes, or on a noticeable market shift.

## Pitfalls
- Hypothetical nature: nobody pays real money, so the estimates drift toward the category's "average" price — don't accept the result without real tests.
- A radical novelty without price anchors produces noise: respondents "pull" the price from neighboring categories; you need usage context and analogies.
- A sample that is not from the target audience invalidates all the points: segment the survey and analyze the points by segment, not on average.
- OPP/IPP are not "the right price": the decision is set inside the RAI and is consistent with positioning (`M-usp`) and unit economics, otherwise you end up with a price that is "the respondents' favorite but loss-making".
