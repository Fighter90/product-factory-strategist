# Customers and segments — who the client is, how to segment and choose the target segment

## When to apply
When you need to answer: "for whom exactly are we growing, what are we ready to give up for them, and on what grounds was this choice made?" The card is needed before competitor analysis (competitors are defined through the choice of client) and before goals and bets. The output is a segmentation, selection criteria with scores, and a recorded refusal of the remaining segments.

## Core idea
**A segment** is a part of the market outlined by differences between participants (geography, industry, context, job) identified through research. **A target segment** is one or several segments for which the company's product fits best in this place, time and set of conditions. The course's working definition: *a target segment is the group of customers for whose sake we are ready to give up other growth opportunities*.

Why the choice is needed: a product "for everyone" = a product "for no one"; most strategic failures are not execution mistakes but choice mistakes (or the failure to choose a segment); customers are not a "source of requirements" but a constraint and a support of strategy; deep knowledge of the segment speeds up decisions and reduces product noise. Examples of focus: Airbnb at the start — people who needed a cheap alternative to a hotel, not the entire travel market; Wealthfront — young tech-savvy users who preferred an algorithm to "a man in a suit"; Whoop — professionals for whom load and recovery matter more than the number of steps.

**Client ≠ user.** The client is the one who makes the purchase/partnership decision and carries financial responsibility. In B2B this is not "the organization" but specific people — the decision maker (DM), approvers, recommenders; end users are secondary (they may not be consulted at all). Example: at a property developer, depending on the company, the decision is made by an agency, the commercial director, marketing leads — each needs their own arguments. For a task tracker paid for by the company, the client is the company's DM, not the employees.

| Role in B2B | Influence | What matters |
|---|---|---|
| DM (director, commercial/technical) | final decision | payback, fit with strategy |
| Procurement, HR | approval, budget | policies, price |
| Team lead | recommendation | functionality, integrations |
| Employee-user | feedback | convenience |

**Segmentation approaches** (none of them gives the strategic answer on its own)
| Approach | When useful | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Demographic | a simple supplementary layer | explains behavior poorly |
| Geographic | a scaling factor, supplementary layer | not the main strategic criterion |
| Personas | describing and representing the user | does not explain behavior |
| Behavioral | digital, growing the current product | shows "how", not "why" |
| JTBD segmentation | new products, growth points outside the current scenario | requires qualitative research |
| Profitability-based (ABCD, RFM) | optimizing the client portfolio | does not open new segments |

**How to phrase a segment**: context (life situation) + motivation (why it is needed) + job (what it solves). Good: "IT companies that regularly launch new projects and want to manage tasks systematically so as not to lose control over deadlines and quality". Bad: "small business", "people aged 25–35", "those who are uncomfortable with trackers", "people who want to enter IT" (a desire is described, not a context). Segmentation produces no result if no focus choice follows it.

**The four forces of progress** (why people change behavior, and not easily): Push — problems with the current solution, a trigger; Pull — the attractiveness of the new solution; Habit — inertia, automatic behaviors; Anxiety — fears and doubts. If the restraining forces (Habit + Anxiety) are stronger than the driving ones (Push + Pull), the segment will not move no matter how much you invest.

**Methods for studying segments**: qualitative (interviews, diaries, observation) — depth and the "why"; quantitative (panels, surveys) — segment sizes and budgets; product analytics (cohorts, paths) — the behavior of current customers.

## Procedure
1. **Define the market sides and roles**: who pays, who decides, who uses. For each role — a specific person, not "the company".
2. **Build 4–8 segment hypotheses** based on several approaches (at minimum JTBD + behavioral; demographics and geography as supplements). Write each segment with the formula "context + motivation + job".
3. **Collect data**: what is known from analytics and surveys (size, behavior), what from interviews (motives, barriers). For each statement, mark: fact / assumption / gap.
4. **Score each segment against six criteria** (a 1–5 scale with justification and source):
   1) size; 2) sustainability (growing or dying, can we influence its growth); 3) problem intensity (how often and how badly it "hurts" — verify the motivation); 4) potential margin and willingness to pay; 5) confidence — how well we understand the segment; 6) reachability (can we create value, can we acquire efficiently, whom will we have to compete with).
5. **Check the forces of progress** for the leading candidates: is Push+Pull enough to overcome Habit+Anxiety.
6. **Choose the focus segment (or two) and record the refusal**: which segments we decline for now and under what conditions we will return. There is no "right answer" — the decision is made on data but without guarantees; revisit it in a quarter, when there is more data.
7. **Define what can be offered to the segment**: more value in the current job (cut costs, provide benefit), fix the competitor's problems, kill the old "job", do more jobs with one solution (a superapp), do the next job in the sequence, go back to the previous one, solve a job that is currently unsolved.
8. **Fill in the summary for each focus segment**: focus segment → what we can offer → how behavior will change (which action will become more frequent/easier/new).
9. Pass the segment to `M-competitors` (competitors are the alternatives this client chooses between) and to `M-goals-drivers-bets`.

## Template / checklist
**Segment scoring**
| Segment (context + motivation + job) | Size | Sustainability | Problem intensity | Margin/WTP | Confidence | Reachability | Total | Decision | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
Do not sum mechanically: first cut on confidence and reachability (low confidence → research first), then compare the rest.

**Customer type description (following the course example)**: who they are; why they come to the platform; what difficulties they hit at each stage of the journey (search, choice, payment, post-purchase); what holds them back from acting more often; the metric that can be influenced. In the course example, buyers are split into "basic needs", "temporary", "calculating" and "hunters of the rare"; sellers — into private, informal and official. Attach evidence (clicks, funnel, complaints) instead of descriptions "from the top of your head".

**Checklist**
- [ ] The client is named as a specific role (the DM), not an abstraction
- [ ] The segment is described by context, motivation and job
- [ ] More than one segmentation approach was used
- [ ] Scoring against six criteria with sources
- [ ] The choice and a deliberate refusal of the other segments are recorded
- [ ] The forces of progress were checked
- [ ] A date for revisiting the choice is set

## Typical mistakes
- **Segmented but did not choose** — wasted work.
- **The segment is described as a desire** ("wants to enter IT") or by demographics ("25–35 years old").
- **Obvious pain is taken for attractiveness**: self-employed novices "need help more than anyone", but the segment is small, margins are low, capacity is limited, churn is high, fraud risk exists, and "help" itself is vaguely defined.
- **The user is taken for the client** in B2B (optimizing employee convenience without persuading the DM).
- **Waiting for perfect data**: perfect data does not exist; decide on what you have and revisit.
- **Relying on demographics/geography** as the main criterion.
- **Habit is underestimated**: "convenience" is often subjective (Muscovites find one service more convenient, people in the regions — another): switching hurts, and the new thing feels inconvenient.
- **A loud story instead of scale**: a single large client or a vivid interview is passed off as a segment.

## Questions that can change the decision
- Who exactly will say "yes" or "no" to our product, and what guides them?
- Is the focus segment large and stable enough to hit goal B (check against the SOM from `M-external-environment`)?
- Are the driving forces of progress stronger than the restraining ones? What can be done to weaken Habit and Anxiety?
- Which segment are we ready to lose for the sake of the focus, and what happens if we give it up entirely (including the "change nothing" option)?
- How quickly can the choice be verified cheaply (interviews with 8–12 representatives, a fake door, a pilot) before major investment?
- Which data about this segment does the company already have, and which needs to be bought/collected?
