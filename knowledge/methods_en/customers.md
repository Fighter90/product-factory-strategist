# Customers and segments — who the customer is, how to segment, and how to choose the target segment

## When to use
When you need to answer: "exactly whom are we growing for, what are we willing to give up for them, and on what basis was this choice made." The map is needed before competitor analysis (competitors are defined through the choice of customer) and before goals and bets. The output — a segmentation, choice criteria with scores, and a recorded deliberate rejection of the remaining segments.

## Core idea
**Segment** — a part of the market outlined by differences between participants (geography, industry, context, task) identified through research. **Target segment** — one or several segments for which the company's product, in this place, time, and set of conditions, fits best. The course's working definition: *the target segment is the group of customers for whose sake we are ready to give up other growth opportunities*.

Why the choice is needed: a product "for everyone" = a product "for no one"; most strategic failures are not execution mistakes but mistakes of choosing (or failing to choose) a segment; customers are not a "source of requirements" but a constraint and a foundation of strategy; deep knowledge of the segment speeds up decisions and reduces product noise. Examples of focus: Airbnb at the start — people who need a cheap alternative to a hotel, not the entire travel market; Wealthfront — young tech-savvy users who prefer an algorithm to "a man in a suit"; Whoop — professionals for whom load and recovery matter more than the number of steps.

**Customer ≠ user.** The customer is the one who makes the purchase/partnership decision and bears financial responsibility. In B2B this is not "the organization" but specific people — the decision maker, approvers, recommenders; end users are secondary (they may not even be asked). Example: at a property developer the decision is made, depending on the company, by an agency, the commercial director, or marketing leads — each needs their own arguments. For a task tracker paid for by the company, the customer is the company's decision maker, not the employees.

| Role in B2B | Influence | What matters |
|---|---|---|
| Decision maker (director, commercial/technical) | final decision | payback, fit with strategy |
| Procurement, HR | approval, budget | policies, price |
| Team lead | recommendation | functionality, integrations |
| Employee-user | feedback | convenience |

**Approaches to segmentation** (none of them delivers a strategic answer on its own)
| Approach | When useful | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Demographic | a simple supplementary layer | explains behavior poorly |
| Geographic | a scaling factor, supplementary layer | not a primary strategic criterion |
| Personas | describing and representing the user | does not explain behavior |
| Behavioral | digital, growing the current product | shows "how," not "why" |
| JTBD segmentation | new products, growth points outside the current scenario | requires qualitative research |
| Profitability-based (ABCD, RFM) | optimizing the customer portfolio | does not open new segments |

**How to phrase a segment**: context (life situation) + motivation (why it is needed) + task (what it solves). Good: "IT companies that regularly launch new projects and want to manage tasks systematically so as not to lose control over deadlines and quality." Bad: "small business," "people 25–35," "those who find trackers inconvenient," "people who want to get into IT" (described by a wish, not a context). Segmentation yields no result if no focus choice follows it.

**Four forces of progress** (why people change behavior, but not easily): Push — problems with the current solution, a trigger; Pull — the appeal of the new solution; Habit — inertia, automatisms; Anxiety — fears and doubts. If the restraining forces (Habit + Anxiety) are stronger than the driving ones (Push + Pull), the segment will not move, no matter how much you invest.

**Methods for studying customers**: qualitative (interviews, diaries, observation) — depth and the "why"; quantitative (panels, surveys) — segment size and budgets; product analytics (cohorts, paths) — the behavior of current customers.

## Procedure
1. **Define the sides of the market and the roles**: who pays, who decides, who uses. For each role — a specific person, not "the company."
2. **Build 4–8 segment hypotheses** based on several approaches (at minimum JTBD + behavioral; demographics and geography as supplements). Write each segment with the formula "context + motivation + task."
3. **Collect the data**: what is known from analytics and surveys (size, behavior), what from interviews (motives, barriers). For each statement, mark: fact / assumption / gap.
4. **Score each segment on six criteria** (a 1–5 scale with justification and source):
   1) size; 2) resilience (growing/dying, can we influence its growth); 3) problem intensity (how often and how badly it "hurts" — validate the motivation); 4) potential margin and willingness to pay; 5) confidence — how well we understand the segment; 6) reachability (will we create value, will we acquire efficiently, whom will we have to compete with).
5. **Check the forces of progress** for the leading candidates: is Push+Pull strong enough to overcome Habit+Anxiety.
6. **Choose the focus segment (or two) and record the rejection**: which segments we are giving up for now and under what conditions we will return. The right answer does not exist — the decision is made on data but without guarantees; revisit in a quarter, when there is more data.
7. **Define what can be offered to the segment**: more value in the current job (cut costs, deliver benefit), fix the competitor's problems, kill the old "job," do more jobs with one solution (a superapp), take on the next job in the sequence, step back to the previous one, solve a job that currently is not solved at all.
8. **Fill in the summary for each focus segment**: focus segment → what we can offer → how behavior will change (which action becomes more frequent/easier/new).
9. Hand the segment to `M-competitors` (competitors are the alternatives this customer chooses between) and to `M-goals-drivers-bets`.

## Template / checklist
**Segment scoring**
| Segment (context + motivation + task) | Size | Resilience | Problem intensity | Margin/WTP | Confidence | Reachability | Total | Decision | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
Do not sum mechanically: first cut by confidence and reachability (low confidence → research first), then compare the rest.

**Customer-type description (modeled on the course example)**: who they are; why they come to the platform; what difficulties they face at every stage of the journey (search, choice, payment, post-purchase); what holds them back from acting more often; the metric that can be moved. In the course example, buyers are split into "basic needs," "occasional," "calculating," and "rare-item hunters"; sellers into private, informal, and official. Attach evidence (clicks, funnel, complaints) instead of descriptions "from the head."

**Checklist**
- [ ] The customer is named as a specific role (decision maker), not an abstraction
- [ ] The segment is described by context, motivation, and task
- [ ] More than one segmentation approach was used
- [ ] Scoring on six criteria with sources
- [ ] The choice and the deliberate rejection of the other segments are recorded
- [ ] The forces of progress have been checked
- [ ] A review date for the choice is specified

## Typical mistakes
- **Segmented but did not choose** — work wasted.
- **The segment is described by a wish** ("wants to get into IT") or by demographics ("25–35").
- **Obvious pain mistaken for attractiveness**: self-employed novices "need help more than anyone," but the segment is small, the margin is low, the capacity is small, churn is high, there is fraud risk, and "help" itself is poorly defined.
- **The user mistaken for the customer** in B2B (optimizing employee convenience without convincing the decision maker).
- **Waiting for perfect data**: perfect data does not exist; the choice is made on what is available and revisited.
- **Relying on demographics/geography** as the main criterion.
- **Habit underestimated**: "convenience" is often subjective (residents of the capital consider one service more convenient, other regions another): switching hurts, and the new thing seems inconvenient.
- **A loud story instead of scale**: a single large client or a vivid interview presented as a segment.

## Questions that can change the decision
- Who exactly will say "yes" or "no" to our product, and what guides them?
- Is the focus segment large and resilient enough to hit goal B (check against the SOM from `M-external-environment`)?
- Are the driving forces of progress stronger than the restraining ones? What can be done to weaken Habit and Anxiety?
- Which segment are we willing to lose for the sake of focus, and what happens if we abandon it entirely (including the "change nothing" option)?
- How quickly can the choice be validated in a cheap way (interviews with 8–12 representatives, a fake door, a pilot) before major investment?
- Which data about this segment does the company already have, and which needs to be bought/collected?
