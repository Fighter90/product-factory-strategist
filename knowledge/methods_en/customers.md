# Customers and Segments (EN summary)

**When:** answering "for whom exactly do we grow, what are we willing to give up for them, and on what grounds is this choice made?"; needed before competitor analysis (competitors are defined through the client choice), before goals and bets. Output: segmentation, scored selection criteria, and a recorded rejection of the other segments.

**Core:** a **segment** is a part of the market outlined by differences between participants (geography, industry, context, job) identified in research. A **target segment** is the segment(s) the company's product fits best in this place, time and context; working definition: *the group of customers for whose sake we are ready to give up other growth opportunities*. A product "for everyone" = a product "for no one"; most strategic failures are segment-choice errors, not execution errors; customers are a constraint and a support of strategy, not a "source of requirements".

**Client ≠ user**: the client makes the purchase decision and carries financial responsibility; in B2B this means specific people — the decision maker (DM), approvers, recommenders — while end users are secondary (sometimes not consulted at all). B2B roles: DM (final decision — payback, strategy fit), Procurement/HR (approval, budget — policies, price), team lead (recommendation — functionality, integrations), employee-user (feedback — convenience); each needs its own arguments.

Segmentation approaches, none sufficient alone: demographic and geographic (supplementary layers; poor explanation of behavior); personas (description, doesn't explain behavior); behavioral (digital; shows "how", not "why"); JTBD segmentation (new products, growth points beyond the current scenario; requires qualitative research); profitability-based ABCD/RFM (portfolio optimization; doesn't open new segments). Segment formula: **context (life situation) + motivation (why needed) + job (what they solve)**. Good: "IT companies that regularly launch new projects and want systematic task management to keep control of deadlines and quality". Bad: "small business", "people 25–35", "those who want to enter IT" (a desire, not a context). Segmentation yields nothing if no focus choice follows. **Four forces of progress**: Push (problems with the current solution, trigger) + Pull (attraction of the new) vs Habit (inertia) + Anxiety (fears and doubts); if the brakes outweigh the drivers, the segment won't move no matter the investment.

Research methods: qualitative (interviews, diaries, observation — depth and "why"), quantitative (panels, surveys — segment sizes and budgets), product analytics (cohorts, paths — current-client behavior).

**How:** 1) define market sides and roles: who pays, who decides, who uses — a specific person per role, not "the company"; 2) build 4–8 segment hypotheses from several approaches (minimum JTBD + behavioral; demography/geo as supplements), each written as context + motivation + job; 3) gather data — what's known from analytics and surveys (size, behavior), what from interviews (motives, barriers); tag every statement fact / assumption / gap; 4) score each segment on **six criteria** (1–5 with justification and source): size; sustainability (growing or dying, can we influence growth); problem intensity (how often and how badly it "hurts" — verify motivation); potential margin and willingness to pay; confidence (how well we understand the segment); reachability (can we create value, acquire efficiently, whom will we compete with); don't sum mechanically — cut first on confidence and reachability, then compare the rest; 5) check the forces of progress for the leaders: is Push+Pull enough to beat Habit+Anxiety; 6) choose the focus segment (or two) and record the explicit rejection: which segments we drop now and under what conditions we revisit; no "right answer" exists — decide on available data, review in a quarter; 7) define what to offer the segment: more value in the current job, fix the competitor's problems, kill the old job, do more jobs with one solution (superapp), do the next job in the sequence, return to the preceding job, solve a currently unsolved job; 8) summarize per focus segment: segment → offer → which behavior changes (which action becomes more frequent/easier/new); 9) pass the segment into competitor analysis (competitors = the client's choice alternatives) and into goals and bets.

**Pitfalls:** segmented but didn't choose — wasted work; segment described as a desire ("wants to enter IT") or demography ("25–35"); obvious pain mistaken for attractiveness (self-employed novices "need help most" — but the segment is small, low-margin, high-churn, fraud-prone); taking the user for the client in B2B (optimizing employee convenience without persuading the DM); waiting for perfect data — there are none, decide on what exists and revisit; leaning on demography/geography as the main criterion; underestimating habit ("convenience" is subjective — switching hurts and the new thing feels inconvenient); a loud story instead of scale (one big client or a vivid interview mistaken for a segment).
