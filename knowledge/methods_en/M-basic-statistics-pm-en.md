# Basic Statistics for Product Decisions (EN summary)

**When:** judging whether a "good week" is luck; how much to trust an average; what effect range to promise the business; what "scale / don't scale" decisions risk. Foundation for A/B testing and reading any metric.

**Core:** statistics turns "lucky/unlucky" intuition into managed risk — it gives probabilities, not guarantees (23 sales in a week with breakeven 20 can be pure chance: such a week happens in ≈1/3 of weeks at a true mean of 20, and ≈27% at a mean of 19). Mean is sensitive to outliers but stable at large n via CLT; median is robust and describes the "typical" user; percentiles p90/p95/p99 are the SLA standard — mean can look fine while tails are terrible; t-tests don't work on raw medians/quantiles — use bootstrap of the quantile. Rule: for money and time always show median/percentiles next to the mean; divergence signals asymmetry. Variance σ² defines the "normal corridor" and required sample; for shares σ² = p(1−p); larger σ → wider CI, longer tests (reduce via ratio-metric linearization, stratification, CUPED). Product metrics are often right-skewed with heavy tails → log transform, medians/percentiles, nonparametric tests; binary outcomes at large n approach normal, enabling CI/sample formulas with p(1−p). H₀ ("no effect") and H₁ are stated before analysis; p-value = probability of a difference this extreme if H₀ holds — small p ≠ large effect; p < α → reject H₀; p ≥ α → "not proven", not "no effect". Confidence interval: `CI = x̄ ± z·σ/√n` (z ≈ 1.64 for 90%, 1.96 for 95%, 2.58 for 99%); for shares use √(p(1−p)/n); example: {15, 17, 19}, σ=5 → 17 ± 1.96·5/√3 ≈ [11.3; 22.7]; mean shifts the interval, doesn't widen it. For A/B build the interval around the difference; forest plots compare segment effects (point estimate, 95% CI line, zero line). Decide by CI, not the point: lower bound below breakeven or CI covering 0 → no durable success even if the mean looks good; 95% CI excluding 0 ⇔ p < 0.05 — show both to the business ("+6%, p=0.10, CI [−1%; +13%]" → no basis to scale; "payers +10%, p=0.04, CI [+3%; +17%]" → even the worst bound beats the threshold → scale, checking unconfirmed segments). Type I error (α, false positive): scaled randomness — cost is spend, reputation, rollback; type II (β, false negative): missed effect — buried feature. α fixed before the test: 5% standard, 1% for payments/security, up to 10% for MVPs where missing effects matters more; power = 1−β ≥ 0.8; raise power via sample, duration, lower variance, a more sensitive proxy metric. Accuracy grows as √n (halving the CI needs 4× the data; at n≈100 only large effects are visible); sample size never fixes bias. Correlation ≠ causation: joint movement can be coincidence, common cause, reverse causality, or sample bias — prove causality by experiment or quasi-experiment (Diff-in-Diff, matching, synthetic control); in a metrics tree every "influences" arrow is either a proven A/B or a hypothesis with a test plan.

**How:** (1) state H₀/H₁ and the decision rule before analysis; (2) choose descriptive statistics per the metric's nature (mean + median/percentiles for asymmetric); (3) estimate spread and the "normal corridor" on 3–6 months of history — a deviation inside the corridor is not news; (4) compute the CI (and p-value when comparing); check against 0 and the business threshold; (5) for tests fix α/β/MDE, compute the sample, verify power; (6) phrase the conclusion as risk: which error type is possible and what each scenario costs; (7) rule out alternative explanations (season, base structure, external events) before causal readings of a correlation.

**Pitfalls:** deciding on a single point/week without spread or a normal corridor; mean without median on skewed metrics; p90 ignored for SLA questions; "small p = big effect" and "big p = definitely no effect"; CI covering the breakeven threshold while the decision is made on the mean; α chosen post hoc "so it turns green"; extending a test until significance (p-hacking, same as post-hoc window/segment tuning); correlation reported as "therefore we'll do X"; comparing shares with different denominators/windows; percentage points confused with percent.
