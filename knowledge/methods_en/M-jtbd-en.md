# JTBD and Advanced JTBD (EN summary)

**When:** understanding **why** people use (or "hire") a product; segmenting an audience by jobs; locating where value is created; explaining low conversion despite a good product; finding the moment that gives the user the feeling of value; choosing a market/job to bet on before the product exists.

**Core — classic JTBD:** a user "hires" a solution (product, action) to perform a **job** — a transition from a problematic state A to a desired state B. Job formula: **When** [context, trigger, emotional state] **I want** [expected outcome with criteria], **so that** [result and positive emotions] (Avito: "when I have a thing I don't use, I want to sell it fast nearby, to free space and get money"; Uber is not "taxi" but stress removal and time saving). The job is primary; products compete for the right to be hired; focus on context and motivation, not features; the best segmentation for product decisions is job + solution pairs, not demography; works in B2B (buying-process roles and personal motives). "Fixing pains" is not enough: pain is a symptom of the chosen solution performing the job badly; mature markets hold more opportunity than fixing problems. JTBD vs user story: a story ("as [persona], I want [action], so that [result]") is tied to a persona and a solution; JTBD is situation–motivation–outcome, solution-free — which helps see alternatives when scoping an MVP. Roles: user / buyer / decision maker; segment also by behavior (novices vs experienced) and motivation (urgent vs deferred need).

**Advanced JTBD (AJTBD):** the **job graph** — a hierarchy of a person's jobs where higher-level jobs require lower-level ones, with many-to-many links; analyze the relevant part (Big Jobs + Core Jobs + Small Jobs); value, strategy and business-solution algorithms follow from the graph. **Value = benefit − investments** (money, time, energy): perform the job with fewer investments and more benefit than the competitor; expectations are how the brain estimates value — a problem is a prediction error. Value-creation mechanics: start performing an unperformable job; solve problems; perform more jobs with one solution; **kill jobs** (the main mechanic — simplification is killing redundant jobs); shorten gaps between jobs; cut costs without losing functionality. Strategic moves: enter the preceding job (Aviasales — "find cheap flights"; Domklik — the buyer's pre-purchase jobs) or a higher-level job (LiFT — "grow a personal brand"). AJTBD interviews (30–60 min) reveal the client's job graph; ~30+ interviews per market are needed to choose a market via clients — too slow and expensive, hence expert interviews at the market-choice stage.

**Superdiscovery** — the stage before Discovery, when the job to compete for isn't chosen yet (resources but no idea; a corporation seeking direction; a team after an exit; an investor checking market and channels at due diligence). Goal: choose high-level jobs / a market. Tools: expert interviews, interviews with shut-down companies, desk research (competitors, prices, TAM/SAM/SOM, reports). Principles: **experts, not clients** — talk to those who sell, not buy; **shut-downs are gold** — their NDAs expired, no reputational risk, they know the real risks better than survivors. Algorithm: set the geography → start expert interviews as early as possible (they're found unpredictably) → find shut-downs → desk research → **≥3 interviews per market** (optimal 5–10) for triangulation → run several markets in parallel → choose jobs and move to AJTBD discovery. Never transfer data between regions (culture, regulation, behavior differ).

**Expert interview**: with those already selling products for these jobs (direct and indirect competitors, ex-participants, consultants, investors). Script blocks: Segments · Job graphs · Solutions (how solved, satisfaction, problems) · New value · Trends · Risks · How to sell. Minimum 2 experts (agreement = trust; divergence = collect more); 5–10 interviews often reveal promising segments or market futility — instead of months and hundreds of thousands on an MVP; conversion can reach 50%+; one interview costs from hundreds of dollars and pays off in 9 of 10 cases (edtech example: an expert revealed B2C economics don't close in the country; 8 more interviews confirmed; market entry cancelled). **Barriers** — obstacles preventing the client from performing the job with your solution; "value eaters" that can consume even huge value. Three categories: **objective** (no documents/money/access, legal and technical limits — need product change or client help), **subjective** (fear of the job "I can't do it", fear of the solution "the contractor is unreliable" — need communication and proof), **habit** (won't switch just from inertia). Sometimes lowering a barrier is cheaper than growing value — one of the most effective conversion levers; clients won't talk about fears without emotional contact; compete where barriers are lower. **Aha-moment** — the moment the person feels value: the brain compares expected energy efficiency with reality; a positive gap = surprise, and stronger surprise raises purchase and return probability (Klarna "buy now, pay later"; ChatGPT's first answer; hot delivery in 20 minutes). Goals: move the aha-moment as early as possible (ideally before registration and investment — Replit/Lovable let you build before full signup) and maximize the share of users who reach it; long-time customers don't remember their aha — ask those whose first purchase is recent; different segments have different aha-moments.

**How:** 1) pick the task level: market choice (no product) → superdiscovery; product/market chosen → AJTBD discovery; conversion/retention → barriers and aha-moment. 2) Market choice: set region bounds → 3–10 expert interviews per market + shut-down interviews + desk research; record per market: segments, job graphs, solutions and their problems, risks, how to sell; compare markets; fix the chosen jobs with at least two agreeing sources. 3) Segment by jobs: per segment — Big/Core/Small jobs, hired solution, satisfaction, problems, expectations, barriers. 4) Value: choose mechanics (kill jobs, enter the preceding/higher job, combine jobs), write as value hypotheses, validate. 5) Aha-moment: (a) ask recent clients "when did you feel the value?"; (b) find the retention-curve break point and events correlating with return (compare stayers and leavers); (c) define the activation metric = % of users reaching the aha event, assign an owner; (d) shorten the path — remove steps, give value before payment/registration. 6) Barriers: capture verbatim, tag the type, assign measures (product / communication / guarantees), measure the funnel-conversion impact. 7) Record outputs as hypotheses with a confidence estimate (number of interviews, segments, source agreement).

**Pitfalls:** phrasing a "job" as a feature or solution ("I want a filter"); segmenting only by age/income and ignoring buyer/decision-maker roles; going to client interviews before choosing the market (30× interviews per market); one expert = one truth, no diverging opinions sought; transferring superdiscovery results between countries; treating low conversion as a value problem when the real cause is fears and barriers; demanding registration/payment before the aha-moment; assuming one aha-moment for all segments; forgetting that clients don't discuss fears without emotional contact.
