# Research Types and Method Choice (EN summary)

**When:** a product leader arrives with a vague request ("understand users", "test the idea", "learn the market"). The card determines in one pass: which type of question this is, which method answers it, with what evidence strength, and in what sequence (desk → qualitative → quantitative → experiment).

**Core:** research serves a decision, not a report — if no action follows from the result, the method was chosen wrong. Two process frames: **Double Diamond** (Design Council) — Discover (explore the problem), Define (synthesis, one clearly stated problem), Develop (search and test solutions), Deliver (build and launch); the first diamond is the "right problem", the second the "right solution"; widen first, then focus; non-linear, ideas constantly checked with people — a *thinking mode* answering "expand or narrow now?". **Customer Development** (Blank; Lean Startup) — Customer Discovery (find and validate the problem, including willingness to pay) → Customer Validation (a repeatable sales model, first deals) → Customer Creation (positioning, demand generation) → Company Building (scale) — a *checklist of actions*; in RU practice "custdev" is often reduced to in-depth interviews, which is only part of Discovery. Mapping: Discover ≈ Customer Discovery; Define = synthesizing insights into a problem statement; Develop ≈ Validation (MVP, hypothesis tests); Deliver ≈ Creation/Building. Classification axes: data source (desk/secondary vs field/primary; benchmark as a desk subtype — how competitors solve the same job); data nature (qualitative "why/how/what for" vs quantitative "who/how many/how often"); purpose (generative — discovery, ethnography, diaries; evaluative — usability tests, A/B, solution interviews); what is captured (what people *say* — interviews, surveys vs what they *do* — analytics, observation, tests; survey + behavioral pairs give both). Qual vs quant: sample (a few to dozens vs hundreds–thousands); collection (un/semi-structured guide vs rigid questionnaire); questions (deep, contextual vs simple, context-free); skill (moderation vs valid wording); analysis (descriptive, slow coding vs statistical, often fast); hypotheses (can start without a precise hypothesis vs mandatory, plan fixed before launch); what they prove — qualitative **generates hypotheses** and explains mechanism and client language; quantitative **measures prevalence** and significance but not why. Key principle: qualitative finds and explains hypotheses but doesn't prove scale or allow transferring a share to the market; quantitative proves scale and differences but only indirectly explains; desk data is a starting point, not a decision basis. Method choice with course norms: in-depth interviews (40–60 min; 5–15 respondents, informally ≥10 per audience — cannot claim shares, segment sizes, "majority" as a number); online survey (≥~300–400 at ±5pp — cannot claim causes); field observation/ethnography/dogfooding (site visits; no "representativeness"); diary study (10–12 participants, 7–10 days — no market frequencies); usability testing (series of ~5; up to ~80% of critical problems found per series — cannot predict conversion); SBS/A/B (SBS ≈300+; A/B by power calculation — cannot claim why a variant won); Kano/MaxDiff/TURF (≥300 Kano; ≥200 MaxDiff — no willingness to pay); segmentation (interviews → survey + clustering, hundreds at the quant stage — no stability over time without a re-measure); desk + benchmark (market, capacity, competitors — not your clients' behavior); loyalty metrics (CSAT/CES/NPS/SUS — no causes without open questions); UVP testing (A/B listing, concept interviews — clicks ≠ purchases). **Desk research:** analysis of existing data; fast and cheap but the question can't be refined, data ages, narrow product tasks go unanswered; good for forming hypotheses, defining a USP (competitive analysis + trends) and finding the target audience. Sources by goal: industry reports (market picture and trends), statistics (Rosstat, Statista, Google Trends, OECD/World Bank — capacity, demographics), competitor publications, business press (fresh niches), conference talks and expert blogs (inside depth). Four source-choice questions: how much do you already know; general picture or competitor assessment needed; competitive environment assessment needed; are competitor advantages known (if yes but "how they solve the job" remains — competitor research, conferences, blogs, interface benchmarks). **Benchmark in 5 steps:** state which decision depends on the result → assemble the pool (direct, indirect, non-standard from adjacent industries) → choose criteria (features and UX patterns, price and monetization model, channels and positioning, operational processes) → collect data (public materials, "field" — register and walk the path, expert contacts; fix source + date on every figure) → structure (players × criteria matrix: market standard = cells filled by almost everyone; white-space = empty cells; feature counts as a quick cut; SWOT only with objective data). Desk→decision table: attach source and date to every figure → flag data older than ~12 months as needing verification → find patterns and gaps → write "if… then…" hypotheses → check against demographics and segments → formulate 1–2 promises (USP) and hand to the field. Course chain example: entering another country with a business banking service — reports: the market is conservative, digital services fragmented (hypothesis); competitors: nobody assembles business financial services in one place (USP candidate); statistics: the large-business market is crowded, SMB is not (target-audience candidate); then field verification.

**How:** (1) state the decision to be made (launch/don't launch, what to prioritize, whom to target) — without a decision, return to the brief; (2) classify the question: "what/how many/how often/who" → quant; "why/what for/how exactly/what blocks" → qual; "how is the market structured" → desk; "does this specific solution work" → evaluative; (3) start with desk and internal data (analytics, support, reviews, past research) — cheaper and often more accurate; record what's missing; (4) determine the DD/CustDev phase: problem unclear → generative; problem clear → evaluative; solution validated → scaling and tracking; (5) pick the method from the table given constraints (time, budget, audience access, segment count); (6) build a chain, not a lone method: top-down (quant showed the attitude → interviews explain why) and bottom-up (interviews yielded behavior variants → survey shows frequency and weight); the typical cycle: signal (metric drop, complaint spike) → qual diagnosis → quant scale check → experiment (A/B on part of the traffic) → rollout or back to diagnostics; (7) record in the brief: method, rationale, expected evidence strength, next step on confirmation and on refutation. Timing guides: desk screening 3–5 days; benchmark 5–7 days; field validation 7–10 days; iteration and prioritization 2–3 days.

**Pitfalls:** choosing a method by habit (always interviews or always surveys) without linking to the question type; trying to get "how many" from 8 interviews or "why" from a closed-option survey; desk as the only source (outdated report, other-country numbers, a trend taken as a cause, internal data ignored); blind faith in SWOT and feature counts without user meaning; research with no next step; dogfooding as the sole hypothesis source (staff ≠ target segment); full trust in what people say without behavioral checks; ignoring DD non-linearity (returning to Define after tests is normal).
